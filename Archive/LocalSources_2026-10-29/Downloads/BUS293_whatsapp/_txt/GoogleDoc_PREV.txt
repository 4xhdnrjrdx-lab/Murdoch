2.a         Report Topic 
Team dynamics relate to the behavioural and interactive processes of how individuals communicate, cooperate and collaborate with one another in teams. Team dynamics is fundamental to organisational effectiveness by enhancing information exchange, building trust and team members cohesion, developing knowledge sharing, and boosting the level of employee engagement (Collier, LaRocca and Stoeber 2012). Poor team dynamics have a negative effect on an organisation by fostering conflict and decision making problems, reducing productivity levels, and lowering the level of employee satisfaction (Jones and Bartlett Publishers 2010). Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) is one of Australia's larger financial institutions, and an ASX200 company. 
WBC functions on a larger scale, hence communication must take place across various departments of WBC such as, technology, risk management, compliance, operations, and customer service to provide financial solutions. These different departments rely upon effective communication, trust and collaboration to fulfill common objectives of the organisation.Therefore, factors that facilitate team dynamics can be analysed further by conducting this report on the issues impacting team dynamics within WBC. This study focuses on four primary challenges influencing team dynamics impacting on WBC’s performance, Firstly, challenges in communication between teams and impacting upon organisational performance will be researched. Second, this report will discuss the significance of team trust for building psychological safety, boosting the level of employee engagement and collaboration (Edmondson 1999; Mayer, Davis and Schoorman 1995). Thirdly, this report will research remote work arrangements and the impact they have on effective communication, employee well-being and loyalty to the organisation (Gilson et al. 2015). Lastly, this research will focus on collaboration and team cohesion across various departments of WBC and the impact on organisational performance (Mathieu et al. 2008; Beal et al. 2003).

3.   Identified Challenges 
3.a         Communication problems between teams
Communication problems are a considerable problem for WBC, the largest financial company trading as part of the ASX200; in particular due to the close interaction between the departments such as Technology, Risk, Compliance, Operations and Customer Service and the close relationship between the parts of the company. Communication transmits information necessary for trust, cohesion, coordination and decisions and it creates operational risk and reduces organisational performance in case of communication breakdowns (Mathieu et al 2008).
One of the main issues is with effective communication where each department has a different focus; for example, technology teams concentrate more on innovative or digital transformation objectives whereas risk and compliance teams would more likely focus on governance requirements. This divergence in understanding the main expectations is likely to lead to conflicts or communication issues. As per team mental model theory, performance will increase where everyone involved in a project has the same understanding of the goals, roles and processes (Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006).
Communication issues further impact on trust and cohesion. Trust establishes what information needs to be shared, and efforts to work with others in collaborative activities, and is actually harmed by the breakdown in communication resulting from inconsistent and delayed information transfers, potentially damaging the positive interdepartmental relationships within the organisation (Edmondson 1999). In a large organisation like WBC, the different departments may inhibit contacts between departments and restrict opportunities to construct cohesion with FOCUSyears of shared experience and achievement.
Communication becomes even more problematic in hybrid and telecommuting workplaces. Virtual work, for example, may lead to decrease in informal communication and knowledge transfer, making collaboration more complicated (Allen, Golden, and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015). Even though WBC applies digital communication technologies, virtual communication is unable to substitute face to face communication completely.
Communication problems have a direct effect on organizational performance. Information sharing time lag may have resulted in slowing down decision-making, service delivery quality and waste of resources. In a competitive banking industry such an impact may cause poor customer satisfaction and organizational image and reputation. Good communication practices are highly correlated to team effectiveness and employee commitment (Salas, Sims and Burke 2005).
Finally, communication challenges have an impact on sustainability. Trust, adherence to regulation and ethical decision-making among stakeholders are crucial to WBC's sustainability. Poor communication may contribute to increased stress and pressure on employees, higher operating risks, and violation of regulations. Such a conclusion is also related to Principle 10 of United Nations Global Compact that encourages business to be transparent and accountable. In addition, we should also see communication as a matter of performance as well as of sustainability.
Potential recommendations

Cross-functional cooperation: WBC can enhance cross-functional cooperation via frequent meetings among departments, shared goals and teamwork initiatives. Collaboration improves trust, communication and shared mental models (Mathieu et al., 2008; Kozlowski and Ilgen, 2006). The organization must also place effort on developing leadership on the topics of psychological safety and open communication. People who feel comfortable stating their concerns and new thoughts are most likely to generate more appropriate solutions and decisions (Edmondson 1999). 
Secondly, WBC should establish new procedures for communication of hybrid and remote teams, i.e. By using communication standards,virtual interaction tools and consistent interaction opportunities (Allen, Golden and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015).
 
Finally, team-building initiatives should be expanded to strengthen cohesion and collaboration across departments, improving both employee engagement and organisational performance (Beal et al. 2003; Salas, Sims, and Burke 2005).










3.b         Team trust (Faiaz)
Communication problems and team trust are closely connected, but trust deserves attention on its own. It shapes how teams at Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) cooperate, share information and perform. This section looks at what team trust consists of, how it develops in dispersed teams, and what that means for WBC.
Trust usually rests on three things: a person's integrity, their benevolence, and their ability (Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman 1995; Robbins and Judge 2023). Employees are more willing to rely on a colleague or manager they see as honest, as genuinely concerned for their wellbeing, and as competent at their work. A weakness in any one of these can undermine it. WBC staff might doubt whether another department will give them accurate information, or feel that a manager cares more about targets than about the people meeting them. Trust is also slow to build and easy to lose, so one communication failure or a single episode of perceived unfairness can damage a relationship that then takes a long time to repair (Greenberg and Baron 2008).
Hybrid and remote work make this harder. Distributed employees have fewer of the informal, in-person moments that normally let trust grow, so it is more difficult to sustain than in a co-located team. Trust also matters more for team performance in virtual settings than in face-to-face ones (Breuer, Hüffmeier, and Hertel 2016; Cascio and Shurygailo 2003). To get work done before close relationships have formed, dispersed teams often rely on "swift trust," a provisional trust based on shared roles, professional norms and common goals rather than personal familiarity (Meyerson, Weick, and Kramer 1996; Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004). For WBC, the practical implication is that clear roles, dependable processes and consistent leadership can do much of the work that face-to-face contact would otherwise do.
Trust also underpins psychological safety, the belief that an employee can raise a concern, admit a mistake or challenge a decision without fear of punishment (Edmondson 1999). When trust is low, staff in a heavily regulated bank may stay quiet about errors or operational risks, which narrows problem-solving and widens compliance exposure. Leadership is decisive here. Leaders who act consistently, communicate openly and treat people fairly create the conditions for honest discussion and genuine teamwork (Yukl and Gardner 2020). Building trust therefore supports WBC's operational performance, risk management and resilience, since a high-trust culture tends to produce faster decisions, better collaboration and employees who stay engaged.
These dynamics affect WBC day to day. When employees trust one another, information moves faster and decisions are made without constant second-guessing, which matters in a bank that has to be quick and accurate at the same time. When trust is low, people double-check, withhold information or route it through slower formal channels, and coordination between functions such as compliance, risk and customer service starts to slow (Robbins and Judge 2023). Because communication and the trust beneath it influence motivation, collaboration and performance (Yukl and Gardner 2020), a trust deficit has concrete costs: slower service, a weaker customer experience and higher operating expenses.
The longer-term picture is similar. Sustainable performance depends on keeping capable people and keeping them engaged, and trust runs through both. In remote, high-pressure conditions, weak trust and the isolation common to virtual work are associated with more stress, lower satisfaction and a stronger intention to leave (Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004). For WBC, trust that stayed low over time would mean higher turnover, loss of institutional knowledge, and a workforce less willing to raise the risks the bank most needs to hear about. A high-trust culture supports the stable, transparent and accountable environment that sustainable organisations depend on.
This connects directly to the United Nations Global Compact, and to Principle 10, which calls on businesses to work against corruption in all its forms (United Nations Global Compact 2024). The link runs through psychological safety. Anti-corruption depends on employees being willing to report misconduct, errors and operational risk, and they do that only when they trust that speaking up will not cost them. Westpac's own history shows what is at stake: in 2020 the bank agreed to a record A$1.3 billion penalty for more than 23 million breaches of anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing law (AUSTRAC 2020), failures of monitoring and escalation that have since shaped its focus on conduct and risk culture (Westpac Banking Corporation 2024). Where trust is weak, staff stay silent, problems go unreported, and unethical behaviour has more room to take hold. Strengthening trust makes transparency and ethical conduct far more likely, and keeps WBC closer to Principle 10.
Several measures could help WBC strengthen trust and stay aligned with this principle. One is leadership development focused on consistency, fairness and open communication, so that managers model the behaviour trust is built on (Yukl and Gardner 2020). A second is clear, protected speak-up channels that give employees a safe way to raise concerns, reinforcing psychological safety and anti-corruption together. A third is firmer role clarity and reliable processes, which help swift trust hold across hybrid and dispersed teams (Cascio and Shurygailo 2003; Meyerson, Weick, and Kramer 1996). Regular cross-team and in-person contact would also help rebuild the informal interaction through which trust between people usually grows.














































3.c        Remote work challenges at WBC
According to Mcshane and Von Glinow (2024), Remote teams are teams in the organization where their members work through time, space and organizational confines and are connected through Information and communication technologies. These platforms provide teams with the environments necessary to help connect and reach organizational objectives. An article written by Peacock-Smith, Du, and Graham (2025), explores the case of  (Karlene Chandler v Westpac Banking Corporation 2025, 3115) where WBC had adopted a 2-days per week in office rule where employees are expected to be present at a corporate level office regardless of travel distance from their homes to these offices. In this case Karlene Chandler could not make the commute to the corporate office due to a 2-hour travel time and family commitments. This case highlights the prominent issue of distance in WBC’s remote work policy making it difficult for employees such as Karlene to maintain a good rapport at work but also have a work-life balance. An article produced by Muddit (2022), explores the negative impact of workplace bullying through virtual environments, virtual bullying can be present in various forms but is not explicit when it occurs as an individual is only able to make the connection through subtle hints. If WBC were to have an environment where bullying occurs even with remote teams this could lead to low individual morale, hinder the victim, cause internal conflict, lead to decreased productivity across teams and weaken organizational goals (Mehmood and Mushtaq 2020). Remote teams at WBC without a concrete foundation face the hurdle of lack of team cohesion and fostering a positive culture, work environment. As lack of cohesion leads to a barrier in helping employees engage and build interpersonal relationships and maintain them with colleagues as they would in normal day-to-day office settings (Asafu-Adjaye 2025). Work-family conflict was an issue at WBC, when it came to their hybrid policy. WFC is a growing concern for employees as it has an impact on their occupational stress and can lead to negative feelings at home. This dilemma of time-based conflict negatively impacted WBC reputation and reliability due to the fact that their policy was not inclusive and adaptable to employee lifestyles ( Junça Silva and Lopes 2025). The company prioritises what they feel as good on paper but does not take into consideration the reality of the situation. As noted above we can determine that the company’s performance has not been negatively impacted but rather there have been obstacles throughout the years which have resulted in legal disputes, changing work dynamics and reinforcement strategies.  WBC’s issues with navigating remote teams and successful integration of hybrid work models can be seen as misaligned with principal 4 of the UN global impact. The principal mentions “the elimination of all forms of forced and compulsory labour.” (United Nations Global Compact 2024). WBC has to be open to feedback and flexibility when it comes to work arrangements if they want to be able to maintain high team cohesion and employee satisfaction.

3.d        WBC Collaboration and Team Cohesion across departments 
The Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) depends on an array of departments to cooperate and communicate to function optimally; these include Retail Banking, Business Banking, Technology, Risk Management, Compliance, Operations and Customer Service. Team dynamics theory posits that cohesion and collaboration are significant determinants of the quality of communication, trust, information sharing and organizational performance (Mathieu et al. 2008).
WBC utilizes various measures to foster working-togetherness such as: enterprise communication systems, agile way of working, multi-functional teams, and integrated risk management systems, etc. By utilizing these kinds of ways and mechanisms, departments can communicate information with each other smoothly and can collaborate together. "Frequent interaction also serves to build up trust and relationships among individuals." (Beal et al. 2003)

Working toward common organizational objectives also facilitates cooperation. WBC's objectives: (a) customer satisfaction; (b) digital transformation; (c) operational excellence and (d) compliance, require the coordination of different departments. Team cohesiveness is likely to grow when people perceive common goals and feel a shared sense of accomplishment.

These are all advantages of this approach however cohesion across functional boundaries is proving to be more difficult. The large size and scope of the WBC could perhaps create communication problems between specific teams, technology teams could have priorities related to innovation and risk & compliance teams have a focus on control & regulation. This vein theory states that a shared mental model is required to ensure a team works efficiently together (Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006).
Hybrid and remote work arrangements have an impact on cohesion too. Virtual settings, where there is less communication and relationship building opportunities between colleagues, can lower trust and cohesion (Allen, Golden, and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015).
Despite the challenges, WBC is effective at building cohesion via leadership action, cross-departmental teams, team building exercises and common goals related to performance. These actions effectively overcome departmental barriers and develop better relationships between departments. Cohesive teams communicate well and get results (Salas, Sims and Burke 2005).
On the whole WBC uses effective systems to support collaboration and team cohesion, but organisational complexity and the move towards hybrid working needs focus on the communication, trust and common goals element to support long term performance.
This challenge also bears on WBC's alignment with the United Nations Global Compact. Principle 10, which calls on business to work against corruption through transparency and accountability, depends on departments collaborating openly rather than withholding information across silos (United Nations Global Compact 2024). Poor cross-departmental cohesion can let risks and misconduct go unescalated, weakening that alignment. To strengthen cohesion and stay consistent with this principle, WBC could expand cross-functional project teams, hold regular inter-departmental forums, and reward collaborative rather than purely siloed performance.

5.  Conclusion (Faiaz)
This report examined the organisational behaviour challenges affecting team performance at Westpac, looking closely at how communication, trust, remote working and overall cohesion shape the way the team operates. The analysis made clear that these issues are connected rather than separate. Weak communication made it harder for members to build trust in one another, and the move to remote work added distance that further reduced cohesion and a shared sense of purpose. Each problem fed into the next, which is why the team struggled to function as a unit.
Taken together, the four challenges point to a single underlying need, which is a workplace where people feel safe to speak openly and confident that their contributions matter. The recommendations respond directly to that need. Workshops on psychological safety, regular team building, confidential channels for raising concerns and clearer ways of collaborating are all aimed at rebuilding trust and improving how information moves across the team.
If these recommendations are put into practice and reviewed over time, Westpac can expect stronger working relationships, more consistent communication and a team that copes better with remote and hybrid conditions. Acting on these behavioural factors is not only about fixing current problems, it is about building the conditions for the team to perform well in the long term. A team that communicates openly and trusts one another is far better placed to reach its goals.

	The person is seen as honest and principled, holding to values the other party accepts.
	Inconsistent messaging or perceived unfairness between departments.
	Benevolence
	The person is seen as genuinely caring about the other's wellbeing, beyond self-interest.
	A sense that managers prioritise targets over the people meeting them.
	Ability
	The person is seen as competent and skilled in the relevant domain.
	Doubt that another department can supply accurate, timely information.
	Trust builds slowly but collapses quickly: a weakness in any one foundation, or a single communication failure, can undermine it (Greenberg & Baron 2008). In dispersed teams, clear roles and dependable processes support “swift trust” before personal familiarity has formed (Meyerson, Weick & Kramer 1996).
