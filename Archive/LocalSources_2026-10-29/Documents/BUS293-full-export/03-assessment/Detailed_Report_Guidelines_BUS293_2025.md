**1.0 PURPOSE**
_"Success in winning the approval and support of others for your thinking is not just a product of the ideas themselves. Of equal importance will be the way you present your ideas."_

Business reports sell your thinking to both managers and academics, so it is in your best interests to digest and embrace this outline. The guidelines will serve you well throughout life, not just at Murdoch. Adopting them improves both the "packaging" of your ideas and the value of the ideas themselves. Additional standards, such as the **Chicago referencing system**, address fundamental academic requirements; any business report requires the writer to state sources in the main body (or appendices). **Note that your report does not require a 'purpose' section.**

**2.0 OUTLINE OF A REPORT FOR BUS293**
The contents of each specific element of a report are provided in Section 2.0 to assist students in preparing and structuring their major assessment.

**2.1 Cover Page** — should include all standard requirements:

1. Unit code and title.
2. Report title (Topic should be in large font).
3. Student name and number.
4. Workshop day and time.
5. Name of the workshop facilitator (tutor).
   Give thought to presentation. The second element (report title) is most important — largest font, centre aligned. Other elements can be presented as you choose, but the cover should look professional.

**2.2 Report Contents Page — 2.2.1 Characteristics**

- Should be titled "Contents".
- Comprehensive index showing the structure of the report and its various elements.
- Typically two to four levels of titles in the index (this document's TOC has three).
- Should be the last thing you add before printing.
  Writers must know how to build an index using MS Word (see Appendix 3.1).

**2.3 Executive Summary (ES) (Commercial format) – keep to 1 page only**
In the commercial world, the ES is the most important page. You must sell your thinking in one page because many managers don't have time to read the whole report. High-impact, every word chosen to sum up the "Big Picture" in one page. Typically written **last**, after the whole structure, content and scope are defined — though a draft written early can help define goals and be amended as core ideas emerge; finalise after the discussion and end sections are finished.
_Note:_ Justified alignment looks best (even margins both sides), but left alignment is accepted — be consistent throughout.

**2.3.1 The Three Main Elements of the Executive Summary**

1. **The topic and its significance:** introduces the issue and its importance to management; usually linked with the key issues.
2. **The findings (conclusions):** your high-level evaluation/informed opinion — always stay **third person** (No "I", "my", etc.); make clear what the key findings of your analysis are.
3. **The recommendations and their benefits:** solutions to the issue plus some benefits.
   The entire ES should be no more than **one page**. You do not need subsections here.

**2.3.2 Benefits of an Executive Summary (ES)**
Managers are busy — if you can't get the main messages across in a page you'll lose their attention. The ES acts as a stand-alone, powerful page that sells the report. It also improves reader memory retention by giving a broad understanding that creates a framework for interpreting the detail.

**2.4 Introduction** — ideally no more than **one page**, with **four subsections**:

1. **Topic:** introduce the topic and briefly elaborate on its significance. Do not provide findings; simply define the key purpose (which is to arrive at the findings).
2. **Limitations:** discuss scope and constraints — weaknesses or omissions, often due to topic complexity and limited info in the case (people often distort information for their own ends).
3. **Sources of Data:** state briefly what sources were obtained — secondary only (books, journals, papers, magazines, websites) or primary (e.g. direct interviews). _Note: primary data is NOT to be collected for this assignment._
4. **Report Organisation:** elaborate on the structure of the main body so the reader knows what to expect; comment on whether you'll provide recommendations and conclusions. **Do not** mention the Intro or ES here.

**2.5 Main Body of the Report — 2.5.1 Characteristics**

- **Third person, factual, objective and impersonal.** Present the evidence uncovered and draw attention to the most significant information.
- **Split into numerous, logical sections and sub/sections.** The sections of the question and the research may help structure the discussion.
- Sections/subsections should have titles that reflect content and organise information logically. In BUS293, you'll nearly always find it best to have titles in major discussion sections that reflect the key issues in the topic.
- **Do not** draw attention to the reader or yourself (third person). Just present facts with citations/references/sources.
- Define the core theories and concepts of organisational behaviour and how they contribute to the organisation's current performance, sustainability and **alignment to at least one of the Ten Principles of the United Nations Global Impact** (see 'Guideline for Success' in the BUS293 Unit Guide for expected coverage of sustainability).
- **The main discussion section is heavily referenced** — you're presenting researched information, so it must be heavily referenced.
  (Additional note p.6:) This is not a section for your opinion; present the data found to make logical conclusions or support the team's decisions using credible sources. Discussing possible solutions here makes your later recommendations/conclusions more credible. Ensure the section is thoroughly researched and cited per the **Chicago method**.

**2.5.2 Tips on when to reference**

- If it is not your idea — cite your source (Chicago format). _Note: for a comprehensive list of Chicago examples, see the Murdoch Library referencing guides._
- If it's not clear from the wording that you're still presenting facts from a previously cited source — cite it again.
- Do not leave it till the end of the paragraph to cite. Cite as soon as the idea is used (generally at the end of a sentence).
- When using the same reference several times in a row, if it's not apparent you're still on that source, reduce citation frequency by using the author's name in the discussion (e.g. "Vernon Smith (2002) notes how …") early in the first sentence.
- If quoting word for word, put the quote in "inverted commas" and cite a page number if available. Pagination/number will suffice for online databases.

**2.6 Recommendations Section**
Ideally you've already discussed potential remedies/solutions (referenced) in the discussion section; here, outline the recommendations arrived at from your research. Can be numbered bullet-point form, but at minimum briefly discuss the benefit(s), or a paragraph per recommendation each with a sub-heading. You may have many recommendations; **the team needs to choose the most relevant five recommendations.** These should be detailed and include benefits (not just state the recommendation), so each is explicitly **linked to business needs** — a critical component of business report writing. Recommendations must be useful and practical for managers. Generally you do not need to reference here (solutions already discussed/referenced earlier; note references if not). Remember to stay **3rd person**.

**2.7 Conclusion – one page only**
Provide your core high-level findings in no more than a page (3rd person). Draw together the researched facts and your recommendations to demonstrate a well-thought-out plan of attack. Should be a powerful section ending on a high note or a note of urgency to generate reader support — touch on benefits of the proposed course of action or consequences of not acting. Generally **not heavily referenced** (done in the discussion), but **if you introduce new information you must reference it here** (new info in the conclusion warrants scrutiny). Raise any critical qualifications to your argument here to frame your ideas appropriately.

**2.8 Reference List**
An alphabetical list of all sources used in the report. Must comply with Chicago referencing standards (see Murdoch Library): https://libguides.murdoch.edu.au/Chicago

**2.9 Appendices – optional**

- See the example appendix at the end (titled "Building a Table of Contents") — in a section called "Appendices", each appendix under its own subheading (with name and number). Citing sources of data here is a **must** if it's not your own analysis/research.
- **Do not** simply copy and paste large amounts of discussion into an appendix. Appendices should use tables, graphs, figures and interesting formatting to aid comprehension.
- Remember to use appendices to keep under the **3000-word** size limit (the word count includes all text within the discussion section).

**3.0 APPENDICES**
**3.1 Appendix 1: Building a Table of Contents with Microsoft (MS) Word** (Updated 2012 – later versions of Word may differ)
Word has an automatic index builder; this and the formatting of this document should guide you.

- Before using it, be familiar with the **Style** function in the **Home Tab** (to select "heading 1", "heading 2", etc. — the TOC hierarchy). Select the text for the heading/subheading first, then apply the style formatting.
- To preview the hierarchy of your style headings, use the **Document Map / Navigation Pane** function in the **View tab** (check/uncheck to hide/show). Useful for tracking sections in a large document.
- MS Word's index builder is accessed from the **References Tab** (click "Table of Contents"). Typically the **last** thing you add, on its own page. Insert the cursor on the page where you want the index before accessing this feature.
  _Note:_ If you don't like the default heading format, change it using the **Format Painter** (the button with the paintbrush, found via the Home tab).

**Side box — "Customizing a Heading Style With the Format Painter Tool":** The Format Painter copies the format of any selected text onto text you select after clicking it — useful for keeping formatting consistent. Steps:

1. After creating a heading and changing its font size/format, select the heading text and click the Format Painter button (a paintbrush appears next to the cursor).
2. Move the cursor to the left of the heading you wish to format similarly.
3. As you move from the heading to the left margin, the cursor changes to an arrow — left-click. The same style is applied to that line. If it doesn't work, repeat and at step three select the text you wish to format instead. Trouble usually comes from not selecting text correctly — drag the paintbrush/cursor over the text holding the left mouse button, and keep practising.

**Key requirements at a glance:**

- **Report structure (in order):** Cover Page → Contents → Executive Summary (1 page) → Introduction (1 page, 4 subsections) → Main Body/Discussion → Recommendations → Conclusion (1 page) → Reference List → Appendices (optional).
- **Word limit:** 3000 words — **discussion section only** (excludes ES, intro, recommendations, conclusion).
- **Voice:** third person throughout (no "I"/"my").
- **Executive Summary 3 elements:** topic & significance; findings; recommendations & benefits.
- **Introduction 4 subsections:** Topic; Limitations; Sources of Data (secondary only — no primary research); Report Organisation.
- **Recommendations:** choose the **5 most relevant**, detailed, with benefits, linked to business needs.
- **Referencing:** Chicago style; cite as soon as an idea is used (not at paragraph end); quotes in inverted commas with page numbers; minimum (per Unit Guide) **20 credible references**; main body heavily referenced.
- **Content requirement specific to BUS293:** define core OB theories/concepts and link to the organisation's performance, sustainability, and **alignment to at least one of the Ten Principles of the UN Global Compact** ("Global Impact" as written in the doc).
- **Appendices:** use tables/graphs/figures (not pasted discussion); cite sources; use to stay under the word limit.
