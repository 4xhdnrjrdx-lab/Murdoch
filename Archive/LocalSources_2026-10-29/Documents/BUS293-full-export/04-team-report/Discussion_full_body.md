3.a Communication problems between teams (Bobur)

Communication issues between teams have been identified as a critical organisational issue for Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC), which is one of the top financial institutions on the ASX200 index of listed Australian firms. Efficient communication is crucial in banking as departments like Technology, Risk, Compliance, Customer Service, Operations, and Retail Banking often operate and depend heavily on each other. Team dynamics research has indicated that the level of communication within a team will impact a team’s level of trust, cohesion, coordination and decision-making abilities (Mathieu et al 2008). If communication systems are not properly implemented, the result is often fragmented or misinterpreted information which hinders performance of an organization and increases operational risk.

One of the key communication issues for WBC is how to keep information consistent across different departments that have contrasting perspectives and objectives. The objective of Technology team members tends to be more about innovative new products and digital transformations, whereas risk and compliance teams are driven more by governance, regulation, and the avoidance of risks. The differing objectives of various departments are a contributing factor to the lack of cooperation and communication barriers. As suggested in the team mental model theory, higher team performance can be achieved when members share similar understandings about the team's goals, their roles, and tasks (Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006). If departments have differing expectations and assumptions, this will more than likely lead to misunderstanding and lack of coordination.

It also relates to team trust and cohesion. Team trust is employees' positive beliefs about whether or not colleagues can be relied upon and are knowledgeable. Trust between teammates has been found to be vital in effective teamwork, and the basis for an information-sharing environment and the willingness to work together (Edmondson 1999). Breakdowns in communication may also reduce the level of trust between departments if employees believe that information is incomplete, contradictory or simply slow in its transmission. Cohesion may also be impacted on by insufficient interaction with teammates outside of immediate working groups. Team Dynamics discusses how member interaction and shared success impact on team cohesion; in a large organization such as WBC, departmental barriers can inhibit this and also reduce the collaborative spirit between groups.

Furthermore, remote and hybrid working conditions can exaggerate the issues with communication. Studies into remote teams have shown how virtual environments may limit informal communication, interpersonal bonds, and opportunities for informal knowledge sharing (Allen, Golden, and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015). Although WBC has invested in technology for digital communication,virtual communication cannot replace face-to-face interaction. Evaluation apprehension, and the need to conform, are both discussed in Team Dynamics as influences which inhibit effective communication. This is the apprehension which individuals have of being judged by managers or peers; this means employees may be reluctant to express problems, contradict decisions or present alternative solutions, leading to innovation stifled and poor decision-making enhanced.

Poor communication in WBC has consequences for organizational performance. Slow transmission of communication will affect the speed at which decisions are made, quality of services provided and cause inefficiencies between different departments. In the highly competitive banking industry this may also have a negative impact on customer perception of services provided and the company's image. Numerous studies prove that effective communication is linked positively to team efficiency, performance and employee satisfaction (Salas, Sims and Burke, 2005). Therefore, poor communication at WBC may negatively impact strategic objectives.

This challenge also has significant consequences for sustainability of the organization. In the long term WBC is dependent on positive relationships with its stakeholders, compliance with regulations and ethical decisions. Poor communication can contribute to employee stress, lower levels of job satisfaction, and affect turnover rates negatively. It will also increase the risk factors for the organization, regarding operational and compliance risks. This problem is linked to principle 10 of the United Nations Global Compact: it focuses on promoting integrity, accountability and anti-corruption actions. Effective communication will help ensure that employees comply with the rules, feel safe when reporting problems and take decisions that are morally correct (United Nations Global Compact, 2024). Therefore, poor communication should not be perceived only as a performance but also as a sustainability issue.

Recommendations

WBC should improve inter-departmental collaboration through setting regular meetings, inter-departmental project teams and performance metrics across the departments. Through increased interaction employee teams build trusting relationships and shared mental models that allow for better coordination (Mathieu et al. 2008; Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006).

The company should invest in developing their leaders through leadership training in psychological safety and open communication. By developing the psychological safety within teams employees will feel safer to share concerns and raise concerns, share information and come up with solutions. The employee will feel safer in expressing opinions and feelings without fear of being criticized (Edmondson 1999). Psychological safety helps reduce evaluation apprehension and increase the quality of the decisions made by the team.

WBC needs to improve their communication systems for teams that work hybrid or remotely. Using more structured protocols for communication, incorporating virtual collaboration technologies, and holding frequent virtual team engagement activities help develop trusting relationships, reduce misconceptions among employees who work remotely and develop shared mental models (Allen, Golden and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015).

Finally, WBC needs to expand team building activities that encourage interaction and boost team cohesion. Research into Team Dynamics suggests that through interaction employees build strong bonds through their shared goals and collective success, in turn, increasing team cohesion (Beal et al. 2003; Salas, Sims, and Burke 2005).

3.b Team trust (Faiaz)

While communication problems and team trust are closely linked, trust is worth treating as a dynamic in its own right, one that shapes how teams at Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) cooperate, share information and ultimately perform. Having already established the connection between communication and trust, this section turns to what team trust is actually made of, how it takes hold in dispersed teams, and what that means for WBC.

Trust is rarely a single thing. It tends to rest on three foundations: integrity, benevolence and ability (Robbins and Judge 2023). People are ready to rely on a colleague or manager they see as honest and principled, as genuinely interested in their wellbeing, and as good at the job. The catch is that a shortfall in any one of these is enough to erode trust. WBC employees might doubt whether another department can be counted on for accurate information, or sense that a manager cares more about hitting targets than about the people who have to hit them. And because trust accumulates slowly but collapses fast, a single communication failure or a moment of perceived unfairness can undo a great deal that is then hard to win back.

Hybrid and remote working make all of this more precarious. Distributed employees simply have fewer of the casual, in-person moments through which trust normally grows, which makes it both more important and harder to sustain than in a team sharing one office (Cascio and Shurygailo 2003). To get work done before close relationships have had time to form, dispersed teams often fall back on “swift trust”, a provisional trust extended on the basis of shared roles, professional norms and common goals rather than personal familiarity (Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004). For WBC, the practical lesson is that clear roles, dependable processes and consistent leadership do much of the work that face-to-face contact would otherwise do.

Trust also sits underneath psychological safety: the confidence that you can raise a concern, own a mistake or push back on a decision without fear of repercussions. When trust is thin, staff in a tightly regulated bank may stay quiet about errors or operational risks, which narrows problem-solving and quietly widens compliance exposure. Leadership is decisive here. Leaders who behave consistently, communicate openly and treat people fairly create the conditions in which honest conversation and real teamwork become possible (Yukl and Gardner 2020). Strengthening trust therefore feeds directly into WBC’s operational performance, risk management and long-term resilience, since a high-trust culture makes for quicker decisions, stronger collaboration and more engaged employees.

These trust dynamics feed straight into how WBC performs from one day to the next. When people trust each other, information moves faster and decisions get made without endless second-guessing, no small thing in a bank where speed and accuracy both have to hold at once. Where trust is low, the reverse takes over: staff double-check, hold things back, or push information through slower formal channels, and coordination between departments like compliance, risk and customer service starts to drag (Robbins and Judge 2023). Since communication and the trust beneath it bear directly on motivation, collaboration and performance (Yukl and Gardner 2020), a trust deficit isn’t some soft cultural footnote. It surfaces as slower service, a weaker customer experience and higher operating costs.

The longer view matters just as much. Sustainable performance rests on holding on to good people and keeping them engaged, and trust runs through both. In remote and high-pressure settings especially, thin trust and the isolation that tends to come with virtual work go hand in hand with more stress, less satisfaction and a stronger urge to leave (Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004). For WBC, trust that stays low over time would mean higher turnover, institutional knowledge walking out the door, and a workforce less inclined to raise the very risks the bank most needs to hear about. A high-trust culture does the opposite, underpinning the stable, transparent and accountable environment that sustainable organisations depend on.

This ties directly to the United Nations Global Compact, and to Principle 10 in particular, which asks businesses to work against corruption in all its forms (United Nations Global Compact 2024). The connection runs through psychological safety. Anti-corruption only works if employees are willing to speak up about misconduct, mistakes and operational risk, and people speak up only when they trust that doing so won’t cost them. Where trust is weak, staff stay quiet, problems go unreported, and the space for unethical behaviour quietly opens up. A bank that never builds trust risks slipping out of step with Principle 10; one that works at it makes transparency and ethical conduct far more likely.

A few strategies could help WBC strengthen trust and stay aligned with this principle. The first is leadership development aimed at consistency, fairness and open communication, so that managers actually model the behaviour trust is built on (Yukl and Gardner 2020). The second is clear, protected speak-up channels that give employees a safe way to raise concerns, reinforcing psychological safety and anti-corruption at the same time. The third is firmer role clarity and dependable processes, which support swift trust across hybrid and dispersed teams and take some of the uncertainty out of working apart (Cascio and Shurygailo 2003; Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004). Last, regular cross-team and in-person contact would help rebuild the informal interaction through which trust between people tends to grow on its own.

3.c Remote work challenges at WBC (Owethu)

According to Mcshane and Von Glinow (2024), Remote teams are teams in the organization where their members work through time, space and organizational confines and are connected through Information and communication technologies. These platforms provide teams with the environments necessary to help connect and reach organizational objectives. An article written by Peacock-Smith, Du, and Graham (2025), explores the case of  (Karlene Chandler v Westpac Banking Corporation 2025, 3115) where WBC had adopted a 2-days per week in office rule where employees are expected to be present at a corporate level office regardless of travel distance from their homes to these offices. In this case Karlene Chandler could not make the commute to the corporate office due to a 2-hour travel time and family commitments. This case highlights the prominent issue of distance in WBC’s remote work policy making it difficult for employees such as Karlene to maintain a good rapport at work but also have a work-life balance. An article produced by Muddit (2022), explores the negative impact of workplace bullying through virtual environments, virtual bullying can be present in various forms but is not explicit when it occurs as an individual is only able to make the connection through subtle hints. If WBC were to have an environment where bullying occurs even with remote teams this could lead to low individual morale, hinder the victim, cause internal conflict, lead to decreased productivity across teams and weaken organizational goals (Mehmood and Mushtaq 2020). Remote teams at WBC without a concrete foundation face the hurdle of lack of team cohesion and fostering a positive culture, work environment. As lack of cohesion leads to a barrier in helping employees engage and build interpersonal relationships and maintain them with colleagues as they would in normal day-to-day office settings (Asafu-Adjaye 2025). Work-family conflict was an issue at WBC, when it came to their hybrid policy. WFC is a growing concern for employees as it has an impact on their occupational stress and can lead to negative feelings at home. This dilemma of time-based conflict negatively impacted WBC reputation and reliability due to the fact that their policy was not inclusive and adaptable to employee lifestyles ( Junça Silva and Lopes 2025). The company prioritises what they feel as good on paper but does not take into consideration the reality of the situation. As noted above we can determine that the company’s performance has not been negatively impacted but rather there have been obstacles throughout the years which have resulted in legal disputes, changing work dynamics and reinforcement strategies.  WBC’s issues with navigating remote teams and successful integration of hybrid work models can be seen as misaligned with principal 4 of the UN global impact. The principal mentions “the elimination of all forms of forced and compulsory labour.” (United Nations Global Compact, n.d.). WBC has to be open to feedback and flexibility when it comes to work arrangements if they want to be able to maintain high team cohesion and employee satisfaction.

3.d WBC Collaboration and Team Cohesion across departments (Bobur)

The Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) is a major player within the ASX200 financial organizations in Australia. WBC's business is a co-operative between divisions such as: Retail Banking, Business Banking, Technology, Risk Management, Compliance, Operations, and Customer Services. As banking is a highly co-operative business structure it is necessary that staff work together by communicating information, co-ordinating action and supporting the objectives of the organization. Team dynamics theory indicates that teamwork cohesion and collaboration are important predictors of the effectiveness of an organization as it will be an indicator of good communication, trust, knowledge sharing and job performance (Mathieu et al 2008). As such a significant measure of WBC's organizational effectiveness will depend on the way it fosters collaboration and teamwork cohesion across departments.

Team cohesion can be defined as the extent to which individual employees are drawn to their team and are eager to stay in that group. As presented in the Team Dynamics material, various factors lead to the cohesion of a group: the frequency of interaction among members of the group, the success of the group, a clear common goal, the size of the group, and facing obstacles. In general, the higher the level of cohesion, the higher is the level of individual commitment, communication and performance. But it might also be that a high level of cohesion will result in stronger pressures to conform and less criticism within the group. Team cohesion is valuable for WBC because all employees from various departments might have specific know-how that needs to be incorporated and utilized during working together for organizational objectives.

There are several systems set up in WBC that foster the collaboration between various departments: organization-wide communication systems, flexible working environment (agile working environment), cross functional teams, and integrated risk management. These systems help members of the organization to collaborate together, by sharing information and coordinating each other's tasks. High frequency of interaction between members is one of the most powerful predictors of team cohesion as it strengthens interpersonal relationships, trust among team members (Beal et al. 2003). The support for employees' collaboration provided by WBC can be traced from its system.

WBC's commitment to common organisational goals is also conducive of coherence and team cohesion. WBC's strategic objectives, namely customer satisfaction, digital transformation, operational excellence and regulatory compliance, all require teams to work together, as opposed to in silo. According to the findings of Team Dynamics, team cohesion improves when members share common goals and achieve them as a team. As employees recognize and achieve common strategic objectives they have greater commitment towards their team, and ultimately, the organisation as a whole. This is a critical dynamic for large organisations, where employees can become alienated from strategic objectives due to the nature and size of the organisation.

Nevertheless, keeping teams coherent across departmental boundaries is difficult. The large size and organisational complexity of WBC could pose communication barriers across specialised teams. For instance, technology teams could value innovation and digital solutions whereas compliance and risk teams may value governance and compliance with the regulatory environment. Differences in priorities can lead to conflict, confusion and delayed decision-making. According to the principles of Team mental model theory, effective teamwork can only be maintained when teams possess an organizational mental model, comprising a shared understanding of organisational objectives, roles and processes (Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006).

Both hybrid and remote working pose other challenges to team cohesion. Studies have shown that remote teams are less likely to establish trusting relationships and more likely to reduce interpersonal communication when contrasted to their co-located counterparts (Allen, Golden and Shockley 2015, Gilson et al. 2015).Although the WBC makes use of IT communication systems to ensure team collaboration, the nature of virtual communication can hinder both personal relationships and opportunistic sharing of information. According to the material on Team Dynamics, the member interaction is the source of cohesion; therefore, less face-to-face communication is a risk to team relations when other mechanisms of social interaction are not employed.

The WBC implements several strategies, however, to maintain the cohesiveness of the team. Leadership training fosters an atmosphere of trust, responsibility, and collaboration while cross functional projects break down departmental silos and promote shared understanding between employees. Team building activities, common goals for success, and achievement on past project work reinforce cohesion by bolstering individual identity and shared obligation. The evidence shows that high team cohesion correlates with the capacity of members to communicate openly and collaborate successfully to obtain successful work outcomes (Salas, Sims and Burke 2005)

In general, the systems in WBC are effective to enable team cohesion and cooperation across departments. The use of communication technologies, cross-functional teamwork, management leadership, and shared organizational goals all help employees to cooperate well with others and make organisations work well. Nonetheless, the complexity of the organization, heterogeneity of the department and the hybrid work format brings potential problems that have to be taken into account. Continuing to promote trust, communication and shared goals will contribute to improve team cohesion and organizational performance in the long term.
