1.   Executive Summary

As one of Australia’s major lenders and an ASX200 stock, Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) is an expansive, multi-business entity that operates through dynamic teams. In modern, complex organizations, teams must perform effectively across communication, trust, cooperation, and overall team efficacy. Moreover, as an economy that continually moves towards further digitization and a hybridized working landscape, effective inter-organizational communication becomes indispensable for overall success and long-term sustainability. This report analyses four primary team dynamics issues that impact Westpac Banking Corporation: communication between teams, team trust, and remote working.

Also, collaboration and team cohesiveness across departments. Communication issues are significant barriers in organizations with many departments operating with conflicting interests. Disparities between departments within Technology, Risk, Compliance, Operations, and Customer Service, for instance, create barriers to clear communication. Such barriers lead to misconceptions, delays in decision-making, and interruptions in overall information dissemination.

The report further considers the role of trust in promoting teamwork. Trust enables workers to share knowledge, work constructively, and participate in collaborative processes. Especially within hybrid and remote teams, trust is a vital commodity, as it serves as a substitute for direct employee contact and helps build informal relationships. The paper reveals how trust contributes to psychological safety, employee engagement, and organizational resilience.

Lastly, the paper addresses the effects of remote and hybrid work arrangements on employee well-being, communication quality, and team integration. In addition to the benefit of work-life balance, employees face a new challenge related to their connection to the organization and to other employees. Last of all, the paper evaluates teamwork and team integration across WBC's various divisions. In that regard, the implementation of communication technology, Integrated Systems, and the cross-functional teams set up to foster effective team collaboration failed because the organization is too complex.

The research, grounded in organizational behaviour principles and academic literature, highlights the strong relationships among communication, trust, hybrid/remote work, and team integration. To improve work-life balance and organizational efficiency, it is necessary to strengthen psychological safety processes, reinforce cross-divisional communication, and provide employees with more opportunities to build a sense of togetherness and trust in the organization. Also, a system that allows employees to provide the necessary feedback in a non-anonymous manner, coupled with regular surveys on employee satisfaction and organizational processes, needs to be put in place to ensure organizational sustainability.

Team dynamics refers to the observable and inferable behaviors and relational exchanges among team members that facilitate communication and interaction in the workplace (Collier, LaRocca, & Stoeber, 2012; Jones and Bartlett Publishers, 2010). It helps team members communicate, share knowledge and skills, feel valued as employees, and build trust that fosters cooperation (Jones and Bartlett Publishers, 2010). Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) is an Australian bank, a constituent of the ASX200, and one of the largest and most complex organizations in Australia; therefore, it relies on collaboration across departments, including Technology, Risk Management, Compliance, Operations, and Customer Service. The ability to communicate, trust, and cooperate has long been crucial to achieving overall organizational goals, growth, and sustainability. (Jones and Bartlett Publishers, 2010). In this report, 4 key challenges related to WBC team dynamics will be investigated to ensure the organization's sustainable operations.

3.   Identified Challenges

3.a  | Communication problems between teams

Communication problems are a significant issue for WBC, the largest financial company trading on the ASX200; in particular, due to the close interaction among departments such as Technology, Risk, Compliance, Operations, and Customer Service, as well as the close relationships within the company. Communication transmits information necessary for trust, cohesion, coordination, and decision-making, and it creates operational risk and reduces organizational performance when communication breaks down (Mathieu et al., 2008).

One of the main issues is effective communication, as each department has a different focus; for example, technology teams focus more on innovative or digital transformation objectives, whereas risk and compliance teams are more likely to focus on governance requirements. This divergence in understanding the main expectations is likely to lead to conflicts or communication issues. According to team mental model theory, performance will increase when everyone involved in a project shares a common understanding of the goals, roles, and processes (Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006).

Communication issues further impact trust and cohesion. Trust establishes what information needs to be shared, and efforts to work with others in collaborative activities, and is actually harmed by the breakdown in communication resulting from inconsistent and delayed information transfers, potentially damaging the positive interdepartmental relationships within the organization (Edmondson 1999). In a large organization like WBC, the different departments may inhibit cross-departmental contact and restrict opportunities to build cohesion through years of shared experience and achievement.

Communication becomes even more problematic in hybrid and telecommuting workplaces. Virtual work, for example, may reduce informal communication and knowledge transfer, making collaboration more complex (Allen, Golden, and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015). Even though WBC uses digital communication technologies, virtual communication cannot completely substitute for face-to-face communication.

Communication problems directly affect organizational performance. Information-sharing time lags may have slowed decision-making, reduced service delivery quality, and wasted resources. In a competitive banking industry, such an impact may cause poor customer satisfaction, organizational image, and reputation. Good communication practices are highly correlated with team effectiveness and employee commitment (Salas, Sims, and Burke, 2005).

Finally, communication challenges affect sustainability. Trust, adherence to regulation, and ethical decision-making among stakeholders are crucial to WBC's sustainability. Poor communication may contribute to increased stress and pressure on employees, greater operating risks, and regulatory violations. Such a conclusion also aligns with Principle 10 of the United Nations Global Compact, which encourages businesses to be transparent and accountable. In addition, we should also see communication as a matter of performance as well as of sustainability.

Potential recommendations

Improve cross-functional teamwork by setting shared goals and holding regular interdepartmental meetings (Mathieu et al. 2008).

Improve hybrid team communication by implementing explicit communication protocols and virtual collaboration tools (Allen, Golden, and Shockley 2015).

Expand team-building activities to boost cohesion, engagement, and performance (Beal et al. 2003).

3.b  | Team trust

Communication problems and team trust are closely connected, but trust deserves attention on its own. It shapes how teams at Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) cooperate, share information, and perform.

Trust usually rests on three things: a person's integrity, their benevolence, and their ability (Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman 1995; Robbins and Judge 2023). Employees are more willing to rely on a colleague or manager they see as honest, as genuinely concerned for their well-being, and as competent at their work. A weakness in any one of these can undermine it. WBC staff might doubt whether another department will provide accurate information or feel that a manager cares more about targets than about the people who meet them. Trust is also slow to build and easy to lose, so one communication failure or a single episode of perceived unfairness can damage a relationship that then takes a long time to repair (Greenberg and Baron 2008).

Hybrid and remote work make this harder. Distributed employees have fewer informal, in-person moments that normally foster trust, so it is more difficult to sustain than in a co-located team. Trust also matters more for team performance in virtual settings than in face-to-face ones (Breuer, Hüffmeier, and Hertel 2016; Cascio and Shurygailo 2003). To get work done before close relationships have formed, dispersed teams often rely on "swift trust," a provisional trust based on shared roles, professional norms, and common goals rather than personal familiarity (Meyerson, Weick, and Kramer 1996; Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004). For WBC, the practical implication is that clear roles, dependable processes, and consistent leadership can do much of the work that face-to-face contact would otherwise do.

Trust also underpins psychological safety, the belief that an employee can raise a concern, admit a mistake, or challenge a decision without fear of punishment (Edmondson 1999). When trust is low, staff at a heavily regulated bank may stay quiet about errors or operational risks, narrowing problem-solving and widening compliance exposure. Leadership is decisive here. Leaders who act consistently, communicate openly, and treat people fairly create the conditions for honest discussion and genuine teamwork (Yukl and Gardner 2020). Building trust, therefore, supports WBC's operational performance, risk management, and resilience.

These dynamics play out day to day and over time. When employees trust one another, information moves faster, and decisions are made without constant second-guessing; when trust is low, people double-check, withhold information, or route it through slower formal channels, and coordination between compliance, risk, and customer service slows (Robbins and Judge 2023). The costs are concrete: slower service, a weaker customer experience, and higher operating expenses. Sustained low trust causes longer-term damage, with more stress, lower satisfaction, and higher turnover in remote, high-pressure conditions, and a workforce less willing to raise the risks the bank most needs to hear about (Zakaria, Amelinckx, and Wilemon 2004).

It connects directly to the United Nations Global Compact and to Principle 10, which calls on businesses to work against corruption in all its forms (United Nations Global Compact 2024). The link runs through psychological safety. Anti-corruption depends on employees being willing to report misconduct, errors, and operational risk, and they do that only when they trust that speaking up will not cost them. Westpac's own history shows what is at stake. In 2020, the bank agreed to a record A$1.3 billion penalty for more than 23 million breaches of anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing law (AUSTRAC 2020), failures of monitoring and escalation that have since shaped its focus on conduct and risk culture (Westpac Banking Corporation 2024). Where trust is weak, staff stay silent, problems go unreported, and unethical behaviour has more room to take hold. Strengthening trust makes transparency and ethical conduct far more likely and keeps WBC closer to Principle 10.

Several measures could help WBC strengthen trust and stay aligned with this principle. Leadership development focused on consistency, fairness, and open communication would have managers model the behaviors that build trust (Yukl and Gardner 2020). Clear, protected speak-up channels give employees a safe way to raise concerns, reinforcing psychological safety and anti-corruption together. Firm role clarity and reliable processes help build swift trust across hybrid teams (Cascio and Shurygailo 2003; Meyerson, Weick, and Kramer 1996), while regular cross-team and in-person contact rebuilds the informal interactions through which trust usually grows.

3.c | Remote work challenges at WBC

According to McShane and Von Glinow (2024), Remote teams are teams in the organization whose members work across time, space, and organizational boundaries and are connected through Information and communication technologies. These platforms provide teams with the environments needed to connect and achieve organizational objectives. An article written by Peacock-Smith, Du, and Graham (2025) explores the case of (Karlene Chandler v Westpac Banking Corporation 2025, 3115) where WBC had adopted a 2-day per week in-office rule where employees are expected to be present at a corporate-level office regardless of travel distance from their homes to these offices. In this case, Karlene Chandler could not make the commute to the corporate office due to a 2-hour travel time and family commitments. This case highlights the prominent issue of distance in WBC's remote work policy, making it difficult for employees like Karlene to maintain good rapport at work while balancing work and life. An article by Muddit (2022) explores the negative impact of workplace bullying in virtual environments. Virtual bullying can take various forms but is not explicit, as an individual can only infer it from subtle hints. If WBC were to have an environment where bullying occurs, even within remote teams, this could lead to low individual morale, hinder the victim, cause internal conflict, decrease productivity across teams, and weaken organizational goals (Mehmood and Mushtaq 2020). Remote teams at WBC without a concrete foundation face the challenge of building team cohesion and fostering a positive culture and work environment. A lack of cohesion creates a barrier to helping employees engage, build interpersonal relationships, and maintain them with colleagues as they would in normal day-to-day office settings (Asafu-Adjaye 2025). Work-family conflict was an issue at WBC regarding its hybrid policy. WFC is a growing concern for employees, as it affects their occupational stress and can lead to negative feelings at home. This time-based conflict negatively affected WBC's reputation and reliability because its policy was neither inclusive nor adaptable to employees' lifestyles (Junça Silva and Lopes 2025). The company prioritizes what it feels is good on paper but does not consider the realities of the situation. As noted above, the company's performance has not been negatively affected; rather, over the years, obstacles have led to legal disputes, changing work dynamics, and the need for reinforcement strategies. WBC's issues with navigating remote teams and successfully integrating hybrid work models can be seen as misaligned with Principle 4 of the UN Global Compact. The principle states "the elimination of all forms of forced and compulsory labor." (United Nations Global Compact 2024). WBC has to be open to feedback and flexible with work arrangements to maintain high team cohesion and employee satisfaction.

3.d | WBC Collaboration and Team Cohesion across departments

The Westpac Banking Corporation (WBC) relies on an array of departments to collaborate and communicate effectively; these include Retail Banking, Business Banking, Technology, Risk Management, Compliance, Operations, and Customer Service. Team dynamics theory posits that cohesion and collaboration are significant determinants of communication quality, trust, information sharing, and organizational performance (Mathieu et al., 2008).

WBC utilizes various measures to foster collaboration, such as enterprise communication systems, agile ways of working, multi-functional teams, and integrated risk management systems. By using these mechanisms, departments can communicate information smoothly and collaborate. "Frequent interaction also serves to build up trust and relationships among individuals." (Beal et al. 2003)

Working toward common organizational objectives also facilitates cooperation. WBC's objectives: (a) customer satisfaction; (b) digital transformation; (c) operational excellence, and (d) compliance, require the coordination of different departments. Team cohesiveness is likely to grow when people perceive common goals and feel a shared sense of accomplishment.

These are the advantages of this approach; however, cohesion across functional boundaries is proving more difficult. The large size and scope of the WBC could create communication problems between specific teams: technology teams may prioritize innovation, while risk & compliance teams focus on control & regulation. This vein theory states that a shared mental model is required to ensure a team works efficiently together (Kozlowski and Ilgen 2006).

Hybrid and remote work arrangements also affect cohesion. Virtual settings, with fewer opportunities for communication and relationship building among colleagues, can lower trust and cohesion (Allen, Golden, and Shockley 2015; Gilson et al. 2015).

Despite the challenges, WBC is effective at building cohesion through leadership action, cross-departmental teams, team-building exercises, and common performance goals. These actions effectively overcome departmental barriers and develop better relationships between departments. Cohesive teams communicate well and achieve results (Salas, Sims, and Burke, 2005).

On the whole, WBC uses effective systems to support collaboration and team cohesion. However, organizational complexity and the move towards hybrid working require a focus on communication, trust, and common goals to support long-term performance.

This challenge also bears on WBC's alignment with the United Nations Global Compact. Principle 10, which calls on businesses to combat corruption through transparency and accountability, depends on departments collaborating openly rather than withholding information within isolated departments (United Nations Global Compact 2024). Poor cross-departmental cohesion can allow risks and misconduct to go unaddressed, therefore weakening alignment. To strengthen cohesion and stay consistent with this principle, WBC could expand cross-functional project teams, hold regular inter-departmental forums, and reward collaborative rather than purely siloed performance.

5. Conclusion

This report examined the organizational behavior challenges affecting team performance at Westpac, focusing on how communication, trust, remote work, and overall cohesion shape the team's operations. The analysis made clear that these issues are connected rather than separate. Weak communication made it harder for members to build trust in one another, and the move to remote work added distance, further reducing cohesion and a shared sense of purpose. Each problem fed into the next, which is why the team struggled to function as a unit.

Taken together, the four challenges point to a single underlying need: a workplace where people feel safe to speak openly and confident that their contributions matter. The recommendations respond directly to that need. Workshops on psychological safety, regular team building, confidential channels for raising concerns, and clearer ways of collaborating are all aimed at rebuilding trust and improving how information moves across the team.

If these recommendations are put into practice and reviewed over time, Westpac can expect stronger working relationships, more consistent communication, and a team that copes better with remote and hybrid conditions. Acting on these behavioural factors is not only about fixing current problems, but also about building the conditions for the team to perform well in the long term. A team that communicates openly and trusts one another is far better placed to reach its goals.
