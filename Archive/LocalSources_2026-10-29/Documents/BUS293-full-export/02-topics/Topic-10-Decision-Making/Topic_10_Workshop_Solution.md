# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 7)

## Question 1 - The rational choice model and why real decisions depart from it

The rational choice paradigm describes the "perfect" decision maker. It assumes we identify the problem clearly, list every alternative, evaluate each one against consistent goals, and then pick the option with the highest expected value. As a benchmark it is useful. As a description of how people actually decide, it falls apart, and for two main reasons. Nobody has perfect information or unlimited brainpower, so we keep running into bounded rationality. On top of that, emotions and existing mental models shape every stage whether we want them to or not.

What we really do is cut corners. Instead of maximising, people satisfice. They pick the first alternative that is "good enough" against some implicit standard rather than working out the best possible one. We also tend to evaluate options one at a time as they turn up, not all together, and we often settle on a quiet favourite early on and then bias the comparison so that favourite wins. The rational model is still worth knowing as an ideal. The realistic goal, though, is just to make the process a bit more systematic, not to pretend we ever reach pure rationality.

## Question 3 - The role of emotions and intuition

The idea that good decisions are made with cold logic and no emotion is just wrong. Emotions are built into the whole process. They help us notice that a problem exists in the first place, and they attach a quick "good" or "bad" feeling to each alternative. That feeling is genuine information the brain uses to form a preference. Take emotion out completely and a person struggles to decide anything at all.

Intuition is closely linked to this. It is the capacity to sense a problem or opportunity and to choose a course of action without conscious reasoning, and it works through two things. The first is pattern matching, where an experienced person's "gut feeling" is really stored knowledge recognising a familiar situation. The second is emotional signals that point us toward an action. The catch is that not every gut feeling is real intuition. Sometimes it is just a bias or a passing mood. Intuition is worth trusting when the person has real expertise in that area. In an unfamiliar situation, or for a beginner, slower logical analysis is the safer bet.

## Question 4 - Escalation of commitment

Decisions are not finished once they are made. They have to be evaluated, and this is where escalation of commitment becomes a problem. It is the tendency to keep pouring time and money into a failing course of action instead of cutting losses. Four things drive it. There is self-justification, because admitting we were wrong threatens our self-image, so we double down to prove we were right. There is self-enhancement, where overconfidence convinces us we can still turn things around. The prospect theory effect plays a part too, since a sure loss feels worse than the gamble of continuing, so people take the gamble. Last is sunk cost bias, where money already spent feels like a reason to keep going even though it is gone either way.

The fixes are practical. Set clear limits and "stop points" before the project starts, so there is an agreed line for pulling out. Separate the people who made the original decision from the people who later review it, because the original decision makers are too invested to judge it fairly. Watch for confirmation bias when reading the progress reports, since people see what they want to see. Honest feedback from someone outside the decision, or a devil's advocate, makes it much easier to walk away from a losing position.

## Question 7 - Creativity in decision making

Creativity is producing ideas that are both novel and useful, and it sits behind better problem solving and innovation. The chapter sets out a four-stage creative process. Preparation is building a clear understanding of the problem and gathering the relevant knowledge. Incubation is stepping away and letting the mind work on it in the background, which is when divergent thinking happens. Insight, or illumination, is the sudden flash of a possible answer. Because these flashes are fragile, they need to be written down quickly before they slip away. Verification is the unglamorous stage of testing and refining the idea to check it actually works.

People are more creative when they combine relevant expertise with a strong drive to experiment and the persistence to keep at a problem. The environment matters just as much. Creativity needs a supportive climate, enough autonomy, goals that stretch people without crushing them, and managers who do not punish early failure. Simple techniques such as redefining the problem, associative play, and cross-pollinating ideas between different teams all help a group come up with more original solutions.

# Activity 2: Case Study - "Dogged by the wrong problem" (Chapter 7, p.268)

## Question 1 - The stage of decision making, and how the change affected later stages

The case is mainly about the first stage of the rational decision process, problem identification. For years animal shelters had mis-identified the problem. They assumed that owners who surrendered dogs simply did not want them anymore, so they framed it as a re-homing problem and put their resources into getting surrendered dogs re-adopted. They were, as the title says, dogged by the wrong problem. The breakthrough was re-defining it. As Lori Weise of Downtown Dog Rescue puts it, "owner surrenders are not a people problem... they are a poverty problem," and where behaviour is the trigger the real issue is usually the owner's lack of basic training. The problem was reframed from "owners don't want their pets" to "owners love their pets but cannot afford to keep them, or do not know how to manage their behaviour."

Because problem identification shapes everything that follows, changing it changed every later stage. When generating alternatives, instead of only finding new owners, shelters created keep-the-pet options such as financial support, temporary lodging, free veterinary care and short behaviour-training programmes. When choosing among them, ACC's trained counsellors now assess each owner's real situation in a private, non-judgemental conversation and pick a customised solution, including deciding which pets genuinely are better off rehomed. Implementation brought new processes too, with social-worker-trained admission counsellors, private offices and funded veterinary programmes in low-income areas. The outcomes then beat the forecast. ACC had predicted 150 owners would keep their pets over 18 months, but intake fell by more than 90 pets a month and surrenders dropped about 50% in the funded areas. So problem identification turns out to be the most important stage. Getting it wrong here came from solution-focused thinking that defined the problem around the existing re-adoption solution, and that wastes resources on a symptom while the real cause continues.

## Question 2 - The role of creativity

Creativity, producing ideas that are both novel and useful, runs through the whole turnaround. Once the problem was reframed, the field generated genuinely new solutions. There was the non-judgemental counselling model, with counsellors trained by licensed social workers and private offices so owners feel safe enough to explain the real situation. There was also a diverse, customised set of interventions, from financial aid and temporary lodging to behaviour training and free vet care. Downtown Dog Rescue in Los Angeles was the creative pioneer, and ACC in New York cross-pollinated by adapting that model. That is a recognised creativity technique of borrowing and recombining ideas across organisations. The reframing itself is the chapter's "redefine the problem" technique. Matthew Bershadker of the ASPCA describes the insight stage almost word for word: "it's almost as if a few years back a massive light bulb went off... we stopped thinking about how to get animals out of shelters and started thinking about how to keep animals from coming into shelters." Supportive conditions helped as well, with organisations open to change and New York Community Trust funding giving them the resources to experiment.

# Activity 3: Class Exercise - Employee Involvement Incidents (Chapter 7, p.269)

For each scenario we pick the best of the five levels of involvement. These run from (1) decide alone, to (2) receive information from individuals, (3) consult with individuals, (4) consult with the team, and (5) facilitate the team's decision. We judged each against the four contingencies: how structured the decision is, where the decision knowledge sits, how much employee commitment is needed, and the risk of conflict (whether employees share the organisation's goals and whether they are likely to agree with each other).

## Scenario 1 - The Productivity Dividend Decision

Recommended level: **consult with the team (level 4).** The decision is complex and non-routine (cutting costs by 3% across work that spans many trades), and the knowledge is dispersed. As an engineer you do not hold all of it, and even the first-line supervisors do not know every aspect, so wide input improves the quality of the decision. You also need employee commitment for the savings to actually stick. What stops you going all the way to facilitation is the conflict contingency. The union fears the initiative will cut jobs and raise workloads over time, so goals are not fully aligned, and TD-group staff may overlook the knock-on costs in other departments because they have little contact with them. So gather the team's ideas but keep the final decision yourself. Less involvement would miss the technical knowledge and the buy-in. Full delegation is risky too, because the employees' interests and the agency-wide view do not line up.

## Scenario 2 - The Sugar-Substitute Research Decision

Recommended level: **facilitate the team's decision (level 5), dropping to consult the team (level 4) if needed.** This is the strongest case for high involvement. The decision is novel and unstructured (no rules or precedents for funding a product the company will license but not use, and unknown demand). It is beyond your own technical expertise while some of your researchers know the chemistry, and you need their commitment to do the work. The deciding factor is that you believe the researchers are committed to the company's interests, so goals are compatible. That is exactly the profile for handing the decision to the team. The one brake is the limited R&D budget and the other scientists already competing for resources, which raises the chance of disagreement. If that competition looks likely to stall a team decision, step back to consulting the team and make the final funding call yourself.

## Scenario 3 - The Coast Guard Cutter Decision

Recommended level: **decide alone (level 1).** This is an emergency under extreme time pressure with a storm building. The choice is a fairly clear stay-or-go, and you hold the key information (the latest weather advisory, the navigation picture, all the crash data you gathered). The crew has no superior knowledge here, and as conscientious Coast Guard members they will carry out the order either way, so commitment to implement is not the issue. The bigger point is that the crew would be divided on whether to stay or leave, so involving them would create conflict and burn time you do not have. You might quickly pull a specific fact from a crew member, edging toward level 2, but the call is yours. Higher involvement would be actively harmful given the time pressure and the split crew.

## Scenario 4 - The Social Media Policy Decision

Recommended level: **consult with the team (level 4).** The decision is novel, since the agency has no policy and no precedent to follow. You personally know little about social media while your direct reports hold the relevant knowledge, so their input is essential. You also need their commitment to make the policy work. Goals are aligned, because you believe all of their views are in the agency's best interests, and that pushes toward high involvement. The reason not to fully delegate is that the reports disagree strongly among themselves, some firmly opposed and others very supportive, so a team left to decide alone could deadlock. Consulting the team surfaces both sides and the expertise while you make the final call. Deciding alone would be worse, because you lack both the knowledge and the buy-in.
