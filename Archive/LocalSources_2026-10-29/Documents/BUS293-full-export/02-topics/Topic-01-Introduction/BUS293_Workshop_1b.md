**СЛАЙД 15 — Chapter 1, Question 1**
A friend suggest that organisational behaviour courses are useful only to people who will enter management careers. Discuss the accuracy of your friends statement.

**СЛАЙД 16 — Ch1 Q1 (LO 1-2)**

- OB is useful for anyone who works in an organisation
- They will need to work as an individual, in groups and teams, or in an organisation with people at all levels and from other countries.
- Everyone need to understand how people think and feel and what they do in and around organisations.
- OB explains employee behaviour, decisions, perceptions and emotional responses at multiple levels AND how organisations interact with the external environment.
- This knowledge and understanding can help everyone get things done while conducting business.

_Speaker notes (Слайд 16):_ This chapter explains that although technical skills and knowledge are important in many occupations, the skills and knowledge that employers tend to rank above anything else are the topics associated with organisational behaviour, such as problem-solving, teamwork, communicating, and adaptability/initiative. In addition to helping students acquire these valuable skills and knowledge foundations, organisational behaviour helps students to adopt better personal theories to understand, predict, and influence organisational events. By adopting better personal theories, we are able to make more accurate sense of what goes on around us and are better able to coordinate our work objectives with coworkers and other organisational stakeholders. In other words, OB knowledge helps us to get things done more effectively in organisations. The above benefits of OB lead to a key principle in this book, namely that OB is for everyone who works in and around organisations. Although managers may require OB knowledge more intensively, people in all jobs ultimately require the skills that employers seek out, and people need to develop more accurate personal theories of why events and behaviour occur in organisational settings.

**СЛАЙД 17 — Chapter 1, Question 5**
Most companies actively hire and retain employees with diverse backgrounds, particular race, ethnicity, gender, disability, and sexual orientation. Based on this ongoing effort, many claim their organisation is an inclusive workplace. Explain why the existence of a diverse workforce differs from an inclusive workplace.

**СЛАЙД 18 — Ch1 Q5 (LO 1-4)**

- There is surface level diversity (observable and overt differences) and deep level diversity (such as differences in beliefs, values and attitudes)
- Employing a diverse workforce isn't enough to have an inclusive workplace.
- An inclusive workplace values people of all identities and allows them to be fully themselves while contributing to the organisation.
- An inclusive workplace gives diverse groups voice through formal structures such as diversity councils and in everyday processes such as in teams and casual gatherings and representation

_Speaker notes (Слайд 18):_ Workforce diversity differs from an inclusive workforce. Diversity refers to the characteristics (typically surface-level, but potentially also deep-level) of employees, whereas an inclusive workforce refers to how well the organisation values diversity and allows people of all identities to be fully themselves while contributing to the organisation. This distinction is evident when people in some groups are not given the opportunity to fully participate in the organisation due to their diversity characteristics. It also occurs when people of a particular group (ethnicity, sexual orientation, etc.) are members of the organisation, but their self-concept needs to be suppressed to actively participate. In other words, diversity refers only to the demographics or more subtle characteristics of the organisation. An inclusive workplace is an ongoing process that enables people of all backgrounds to feel psychologically safe, engaged, valued, authentic, listened to, and respected. For example, an inclusive workplace monitors organisational systems to ensure that they do not unfairly differentiate or discriminate against people with some forms of diversity.

**СЛАЙД 19 — Chapter 1, Question 8**
Automatic and several other businesses (e.g., Emisoft, Gitlab) are completely remote (distributed) companies. Everyone who works for these firms performs their jobs from homes and cafes. In your opinion will distributed companies become more common in the future? Why or Why not? Would you prefer working in a remote company with no physical location, maybe just an occasional gathering of staff at a conference venue setting? Or would you prefer working with co-workers face-to-face most days at a company site?

**СЛАЙД 20 — Ch1 Q8 (LO 1-4)**

- Remote work potentially benefits both employers and employees.
- Not suitable for all roles.
- Employees – potentially better work life integration but when appropriate space is unavailable at home it can be difficult.
- Enables employees to continue working during incidents such as bad weather or pandemic.
- Research indicates general higher productivity. Likely due to less stress, travel time and travel costs.
- Lower real estate costs for employers

_Speaker notes (Слайд 20):_ The viability of completely remote organisations touches on the issues at Yahoo and other companies that reduced or completely withdrew employee opportunities for remote work. But there is also plenty of writing about the success of Automattic and a few other completely remote companies. Some job and organisational features that are important for remote work are even more critical for entirely remote organisations. Things to consider: Do remote firms require leadership skills that are difficult to develop. Do people need more social integration than occurs in remote firms or remote work. Does the average employee perform better under guidance (or close watch) of a "manager" rather than working autonomously from home. Are remote companies more viable in some knowledge work businesses than in others. Consider how employees interact with customers and other external stakeholders. Consider how the confidentiality of information used by staff might limit the viability of remote work and organisations. Do financial arrangements—from salaries to financial benefits—get too complex in completely remote companies, particularly where employees are located in several countries (or would global remote firms simply shift to contract-based workers). Can remote companies overcome or manage the potentially heightened conflict and misunderstanding that tends to occur when staff rely on leaner communication channels (text messaging, online chat, etc.). Regarding preferences for working in remote organisations, a variety of personal characteristics and conditions are likely to be considered. Personal characteristics associated with personality, values, past experiences, and so forth may include: High self-motivation; High self-organisation; High need for autonomy; Good skill with information technology; Able to fulfill their social needs outside work.

**СЛАЙД 24 — Class Exercise: IT ALL MAKES SENSE (ответы)**

1. A happy worker is a productive worker. — True, to some extent. Research indicates job satisfaction has a moderately strong association with job [performance]. See Chapter 4.
2. A decision maker's effectiveness increases with the number of choices… — FALSE. At some point as alternatives increase, the decision maker becomes less efficient and effective in choosing among them. See Chapter 7.
3. Organisations are more effective when they minimise conflict… — FALSE. Dominant OB research indicates constructive (task-oriented) conflict is often beneficial (e.g. in decision making). See Chapter 11.
4. Employees have more power with many close friends than acquaintances. — FALSE. Social network research: people with many acquaintances tend to have more career success and related outcomes than those with mainly close friends. See Chapter 10.
5. Companies are more successful with strong corporate cultures. — FALSE. There is a weak relationship between corporate culture strength and organisational performance. See Chapter 14.
6. Employees perform better without stress. — FALSE. Some level of stress is essential for life; we need a certain level to energise us. See Chapter 4.
7. The best way to change people/orgs is by pinpointing the source of current problems. — FALSE. Emerging knowledge suggests a positive rather than problem-focused approach may be more effective in many/most circumstances. See Chapter 5.
8. Female leaders involve employees in decisions more than male leaders. — [Answer: TRUE.] Men and women are mostly similar in leadership styles; female leaders involve employees in decisions to a greater degree. Generally female leaders are more participative. See Chapter 12.
9. The best decisions are made without emotion. — FALSE. All decisions involve and require emotion; without it people can't recognise problems/opportunities or make choices. See Chapter 7.
10. If employees feel paid unfairly, nothing other than changing pay reduces injustice. — FALSE. Underpaid employees might reduce injustice by working less, taking nonmonetary resources, or quitting. See Chapter 5.

_Speaker notes (Слайд 24):_ [Расширенные пояснения к каждому пункту.] 1) TRUE, to some extent — latest research: job satisfaction has a moderately strong association with job performance (correlation ~0.30); see Chapter 4. 2) FALSE — when alternatives become very large, decision makers reduce motivation to decide and may avoid the decision; they process a finite amount of information; see Chapter 7. 3) FALSE — constructive (task-oriented) conflict often beneficial, relationship conflict has negative consequences; but constructive conflict can generate relationship conflict (hard to separate); Chapter 11. 4) FALSE — "weak ties" (acquaintances) give less redundant social capital, unique information (expert power), unique connections/opportunities (referent power), wider visibility; strong ties give faster but redundant resources; Chapter 10. 5) FALSE — "it depends"; weak relationship between culture strength and performance for three reasons: strong culture problematic when values inconsistent with environment; can blind employees to other perspectives; suppresses dissenting values important for future change; Chapter 14. 6) FALSE — some stress essential to energise us; problem is stress beyond the beneficial level; Chapter 4. 7) FALSE — dominant model identifies problem before solutions; emerging knowledge favours a positive approach; strengths-based coaching/feedback (Chapter 5); focusing on weaknesses creates defensiveness; appreciative inquiry introduced in Chapter 15. 8) TRUE — debate about whether men/women lead differently; mostly similar (end of Chapter 12), one exception: female leaders involve employees in decisions more; generally more participative. 9) FALSE — all decisions involve and require emotion; people form emotionally anchored preferences before consciously thinking; rational evaluation must be processed by emotional centre(s) to make a choice (Chapter 7; noted in Chapters 3, 4, 5); distinct from "getting emotional." 10) FALSE — people play "mind games" to avoid overreward inequity; underrewarded employees may work less, take nonmonetary resources, change the comparison other, or leave the field (quit); injustice is affected by procedures as much as distribution — reduced by allowing appeals, considering all information, treating people with respect, and giving voice; Chapter 5.

**СЛАЙД 25 — Case Study Questions (раздел)**
McShane and Von Glinow. (2024) — WYE & VALVE PLUMBING: A MARS DIAGNOSTIC EXERCISE (Page 32).
Футер: Cassandra Spencer · BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour · Topic One.

**СЛАЙД 26 — Case Study Question**
Diagnose W&V's customer service issues and discuss specific ways the retailer can improve customer service at the company. The discussion should explain whether and in what ways each MARS element can guide better customer service. All four elements of the MARS model should be considered.
_(Фреймворк: MARS model = Motivation, Ability, Role perceptions, Situational factors.)_

**СЛАЙД 27 — Case Study: M for Motivation**
Diagnosis: Lack of motivation by some (most?) of W&V staff seems a major cause of poor customer service. Staff generally do not actively volunteer information or recommendations even though customers value it. Some low motivation could stem from situational factors (difficult physical layout reduces motivation to retrieve parts). Role perceptions could be another reason (staff unaware it's important). But lack of motivation seems a factor beyond these — likely employees don't care about customer satisfaction (know it's their duty but don't engage). Possible counter-motivational influences such as status-like behaviour over DIY clients, or norms where staff aren't encouraged to improve service. No information on how staff are financially rewarded, though targeted performance rewards aren't always necessary. Overall, motivation is a strong explanation for poor service.
Recommendations: (1) Alter store norms so customer service is continually highlighted (beyond role perceptions — like a corporate-culture value; employees eventually engage in desired behaviours or seek work elsewhere). (2) More carefully recruit/hire people whose values and natural customer-service skills exceed current staff (motivation is influenced by personality/values, hard to change). (3) Incentivise employees through bonuses based on customer-service skills and feedback — treat cautiously, as financial rewards can have unintended consequences and may discourage non-rewarded activities.

**СЛАЙД 28 — Case Study: A for Ability**
Diagnosis: Many staff have industry experience and receive regular training on new products/techniques, so a large percentage can answer almost all DIY queries and most tradesperson questions (at least knowing existence/whereabouts of specialised parts). Overall, some specific staff may lack sufficient ability for specific issues (especially serving tradespeople), but ability is NOT a major factor in the service issues W&V is trying to resolve.
Recommendations: Although most staff have sufficient ability, identify specific staff uncomfortable with low-level plumbing knowledge and/or W&V's products. These people might receive better training or be moved to secondary roles such as (re)stocking shelves and cashiering.

**СЛАЙД 29 — Case Study: R for Role Perceptions**
Diagnosis: Knowledge/skills don't translate into awareness of required work behaviours — staff may have poor role perceptions about appropriate behaviour toward clients. They can answer questions but show a lack of initiative to answer respectfully. Employees assume customers know what parts/tools they need — further evidence of poor role perceptions (inaccurate perceptions of customer needs). Some staff may not realise they need to be more proactive and provide supplies that take more effort to retrieve. May require further investigation but likely means staff need customer-service training to improve role perceptions. Some staff may be ill-suited to retail work as they don't view serving customers politely as part of their role.
Recommendations: Introduce customer-service training that makes staff aware of specific role behaviours needed; include behaviour modelling and sessions where staff hear candid customer feedback. Emphasise proactive, respectful behaviour (effect of lost accounts, how competitors excelled in customer experience). The learning process should help staff realise specific behaviours (proactively approaching customers, showing empathy and friendliness, etc.) are important to their jobs and company success.

**СЛАЙД 30 — Case Study: S for Situational Factors**
Diagnosis: Staff are particularly inhospitable when asked for specialised parts only staff can retrieve; retrieving requires effort. Thousands of parts, many retrievable only by staff behind the main customer desk. May require further investigation, but suggests the layout of supplies hampers good service — challenge locating specific parts, cumbersome physical area, or many similar parts to bring and restock. The physical layout may be an impediment to effective customer service.
Recommendations: Investigate store layout for more efficient display/restocking. Alternatively, divide staff so some retrieve/restock specialised behind-counter parts, while those most knowledgeable about trade practices and with strong service presentation skills work in the front area. Behind-counter staff become more proficient at retrieving/restocking even if not plumbing experts.
