# Activity 2: Team Activity (Survival on the Moon)

This is a team exercise. Each member first ranks the 15 items on their own, the team then works out a consensus ranking, and both sets of rankings get compared against the expert (NASA) ranking and scored.

What it shows is that teams usually beat the average individual score, thanks to synergy and the division of labour. A group can pool what its members know and get further together than any one person would alone. The catch is that this only happens when people genuinely share information and reach a real consensus. If the team just defers to whoever sounds most confident, the advantage disappears.

# Activity 3: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 1, p.31)

## Question 1

A friend suggests that organisational behaviour courses are useful only to people who will enter management careers. Discuss the accuracy of this statement.

We do not think the statement is accurate. Organisational behaviour (OB) is useful for anyone who works in and around organisations, not just managers. Everyone works as an individual, in groups and teams, and within an organisation alongside people at every level and from different countries. So everyone needs some understanding of how people think, feel and behave at work.

OB explains employee behaviour, decisions, perceptions and emotional responses at several levels, and it explains how organisations interact with their external environment. Managers might draw on this knowledge more intensively, but the skills OB develops (problem solving, teamwork, communication and adaptability) are exactly the ones employers rank most highly for all employees. That is why OB helps everyone, not only managers, get things done more effectively at work.

## Question 5

Explain why the existence of a diverse workforce differs from an inclusive workplace.

Diversity is about the characteristics of employees. Those can be surface-level, meaning observable differences such as race, gender or age, or deep-level, meaning differences in beliefs, values and attitudes. Hiring people with diverse backgrounds, on its own, does not create an inclusive workplace.

Inclusion is a different thing. It is about how well the organisation values that diversity and lets people of all identities be fully themselves while still contributing to the organisation. An inclusive workplace is an ongoing process that gives diverse groups a genuine voice. That happens through formal structures such as diversity councils and representation, and through everyday processes such as teams and casual gatherings. The aim is for people to feel psychologically safe, valued, listened to and respected, rather than having to suppress their self-concept just to take part.

## Question 8

Will completely distributed (remote) companies become more common in the future? Which would you prefer, a fully remote company or working face-to-face at a company site?

Distributed companies will probably become more common for a lot of knowledge-work roles, though remote work clearly does not suit every job. The upside cuts both ways. For employees it offers better work-life integration and lets work carry on through disruptions like bad weather or a pandemic, and research generally shows higher productivity because there is less stress and less travel time and cost. Employers, for their part, save on real estate.

There are real limits, though. Remote work is hard when employees do not have suitable space at home, and it brings its own challenges: leadership at a distance, social integration, keeping information confidential, and the extra conflict and misunderstanding that creep in through leaner communication channels. Whether a particular person suits remote work comes down to personal characteristics such as self-motivation, self-organisation, a need for autonomy, decent IT skills, and being able to meet social needs outside work.

Personally, I would go for a hybrid arrangement. It keeps the flexibility and productivity of remote work while still giving you the face-to-face contact that helps communication, relationships and a sense of belonging at work.

# Activity 4: Class Exercise - “It All Makes Sense” (Chapter 1, p.34)

For each statement, here is the answer and the chapter where it is explained.

**1. A happy worker is a productive worker. True, to some extent.** Job satisfaction has a moderately strong association with job performance, around 0.30. (Chapter 4)

**2. A decision maker’s effectiveness increases with the number of alternatives available. False.** Past a certain point, more alternatives actually reduce both the motivation to decide and the quality of the choice. (Chapter 7)

**3. Organisations are more effective when they minimise conflict. False.** Constructive, task-oriented conflict is often a good thing, for example in decision making. (Chapter 11)

**4. Employees have more power with many close friends than many acquaintances. False.** Weak ties (acquaintances) tend to bring more unique information, connections and visibility, and that is linked to greater career success. (Chapter 10)

**5. Companies are more successful when they have strong corporate cultures. False.** The relationship between culture strength and performance is only weak, and strong cultures can blind employees and suppress useful dissent. (Chapter 14)

**6. Employees perform better without stress. False.** We actually need some stress to energise us. The problem is stress beyond the beneficial level. (Chapter 4)

**7. The best way to change people and organisations is by pinpointing the source of current problems. False.** A positive, strengths-based approach is often more effective than zeroing in on problems. (Chapter 5)

**8. Female leaders involve employees in decisions to a greater degree than male leaders. True.** Men and women lead in mostly similar ways, but female leaders are generally more participative. (Chapter 12)

**9. The best decisions are made without emotion. False.** All decisions involve and require emotion. Without it, people cannot recognise problems or make choices at all. (Chapter 7)

**10. If employees feel they are paid unfairly, nothing other than changing their pay will reduce the feeling of injustice. False.** Underpaid employees may also reduce effort, take nonmonetary resources, change their comparison, or quit. Fair procedures and giving people a voice also reduce feelings of injustice. (Chapter 5)

# Activity 5: Case Study - Wye & Valve Plumbing (MARS Diagnostic, Chapter 1, p.32)

The poor customer service at Wye & Valve (W&V) can be diagnosed using the four elements of the MARS model: Motivation, Ability, Role perceptions and Situational factors. We worked through each one in turn.

## M - Motivation (a major cause)

Diagnosis: a lack of motivation looks like a major cause here. Staff generally do not volunteer information or recommendations, even though customers value exactly that, which suggests many of them simply do not care about customer satisfaction.

Recommendations: make customer service an ongoing store norm and cultural value, and recruit people whose values and natural service orientation are stronger than the current staff. Service-linked bonuses are worth considering too, but use them cautiously, since financial rewards can have unintended effects.

## A - Ability (not a major factor)

Diagnosis: ability is not really a major factor. Most staff have industry experience and get regular product training, so they are able to answer most DIY and many trade questions.

Recommendations: identify the few staff who do lack the necessary knowledge and give them further training, or move them into secondary roles such as restocking shelves or cashiering.

## R - Role Perceptions (significant)

Diagnosis: the staff have the knowledge but show poor role perceptions. They lack initiative, are not always respectful, and assume customers already know which parts and tools they need. They do not seem to view proactive, polite service as part of the job.

Recommendations: run customer-service training using behaviour modelling and candid customer feedback, so staff come to understand that proactively approaching customers, showing empathy and being respectful are genuine parts of their role, and matter to the company’s success.

## S - Situational Factors (contributing)

Diagnosis: staff are at their most unhelpful when asked for specialised parts that only they can retrieve from behind the main desk, because fetching them takes effort. That points to the store layout being an impediment to good service.

Recommendations: look into the layout for more efficient display and restocking. Alternatively, split the staff so that some handle behind-counter retrieval and restocking while the most knowledgeable and service-skilled staff work the front area serving customers.

**Conclusion:** The customer-service problems at W&V come down mainly to Motivation and Role perceptions, with Situational (layout) factors contributing, while Ability is largely adequate.
