Hi, and welcome to BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour. This unit provides an
overview of the different dynamics of individual and group behaviour in organisations
and explores, organisational theories and management processes. The first topic is an
introduction to organisational behaviour, which will define organisational behaviour and
explain its importance, introduce four perspectives of organisational effectiveness, and
discuss challenges and changes to the field of organisational behaviour.
Organisations have existed since the beginning of time and are defined as “people
working together to achieve a common goal or purpose” and they are not just for-profit
businesses. Throughout history, craftspeople and merchants have formed guilds to
achieve common purposes. Large-scale irrigation projects in Egypt and Mesopotamia,
and the building of ancient temples, required the organisation and coordination of
massive numbers of people. And organisations continue to exist because of the synergy
created through this effective division of labour – through humans being able to achieve
more as a team than as individuals.
Organisational Behaviour is the study of what people, as individuals and groups, think,
feel, and do in and around organisations. It is examined on three levels, the individual
level, the work group level, and the organisational level. The purpose is to apply this
knowledge to improve the organisations performance through the management of
human behaviour.
The effectiveness, or performance of the organisation, was traditionally gauged on how
well it achieved its stated goals or objectives. However, if a company achieved goals or
sales targets that were set too low, or weren’t relevant to the current industry, economic,
or social environment, this still wouldn’t result in success. Now, organisations are seen as
effective if they have a good fit with their external environment, are a learning
organisation, have efficient and adaptive internal subsystems, and satisfy their
stakeholders.
The open systems perspective is the foremost method of thinking about organisations
and their interactions with the external environment. It describes the organisation as a
complex organism that uses inputs from the external environment, like raw materials,
human resources, information, financial resources, and equipment, and transforms them
through all the interdependent organisational subsystems, into outputs that are then put
back into the external environment. These outputs can include products or services,
shareholder dividends, community support and services, and waste and pollution. Some
organisational outputs, such as dividends, services, and community support, are valued
by the external environment, but others, like pollution and employee layoffs, are seen as
less desirable, and throughout this process those evaluations are received as feedback by
the organisation. Organisations also receive feedback about the availability of inputs such
as skilled labour shortages, or changing population demographics, and through this
combined feedback have the opportunity to adjust their subsystems, and thus the
behaviours in the organisation, to ‘fit’ and continue to be effective in the changing
external environment. The other perspectives on organisational effectiveness can be
explained in relation to the open systems perspective.
An input that is occasionally looked over in the open systems perspective is knowledge.
The organisational learning perspective suggests the effectiveness of an organisation
depends on the organisations ability to acquire, share, use, and store valuable knowledge.
Knowledge is seen as a resource, or asset, called intellectual capital, and it exists in three
forms.
Human capital is the stock of knowledge, skills, and abilities among employees that
provide economic value to the organisation. It is valuable and rare, as employees are
essential for organisational survival and success, and talented people are difficult to find
and replicate. Human capital cannot easily be replaced by technology and is thus a
competitive advantage and a huge risk, as when people leave the organisation their
knowledge goes with them.
Structural capital is what would remain if every employee left the organisation and is
embedded in organisations systems and structures. It includes documentation of work
procedures, physical layout of the production line, and finished products that can be
reverse engineered.
Finally, relationship capital is the value of an organisations’ relationships with its
suppliers, customers, and other stakeholders. This includes the goodwill and brand image
of the organisation.
An organisations intellectual capital develops and is maintained through the four
organisational learning processes of Acquiring knowledge, Sharing knowledge, Using
knowledge, and Storing knowledge.
The High-Performance Work Practices perspective of organisational effectiveness looks
closely at the transformational subsystems of the open systems perspective and suggests
four work practices that improve effectiveness. Although each of these practices will
improve effectiveness, they have a stronger effect when bundled together. They are
employee involvement in decision making, employee autonomy, employee development,
and rewards valued by employees linked with performance and skill development. These
practices improve organisational effectiveness by improving individual employee
performance, increasing organisational adaptability to changing external environments,
and improving employee motivation and job satisfaction.
The stakeholder perspective of organisational effectiveness expands on the external
environment aspect of the open systems perspective. It identifies specific people and
groups that affect, or are affected by, the organisations objectives and actions, and
recognises that stakeholder relations are dynamic. This perspective incorporates values,
ethics, and corporate social responsibility, and in general, organisations are more
effective when they satisfy stakeholder needs and expectations.
The increasing focus on corporate social responsibility is one of the current challenges
facing organisations. The concept of corporate social responsibility is that an
organisation has a responsibility to the society in which it operates, and it should be
sustainable, not just as a going concern in continuing to profit, but also in ensuring that it
meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to
meet their own needs. Managers are therefore challenged to ensure that all their inputs,
social, environmental, and financial are used in a way that all stakeholders receive value
and that the resources required by future generations are not exploited.
Technological change is another challenge facing organisations. It has always been a
disruptive force in organisations and society, boosting productivity but displacing
employees and sometimes making entire occupational groups obsolete. Other
technologies have altered our relationships and patterns of behaviour with co-workers
and stakeholders, like the introduction of the internet, smartphones, email, social media,
and data analytics. These new technologies have both positive and negative impacts on
organisations and the people within them, facilitating ease in communication and
networking, yet tethering people to their jobs for longer hours, reducing work-life
balance, and increasing techno-stress.
Globalisation is the increasing interconnectedness to other countries and cultures
through liberalisation of trade and immigration policies and the ability to communicate
using new forms of technology. It benefits organisations through access to larger and new
markets, lower costs of production, and greater access to knowledge and innovation. It
can also have less positive impacts and has been attributed to work intensification, and
the reduction of job security and work-life balance in developed countries. It increases
the complexity of teamwork, diversity, values, organisational structure, leadership, and
other subsystems that impact behaviour in the organisation.
The nature of work is also changing due to globalisation and technology, with knowledge
work increasing. This makes knowledge management and brain drain – where employees
leave jobs and take their knowledge with them – a new area managers need to pay
attention to.
The composition of the workforce is shifting as well, with increasing cultural diversity
through globalisation which impacts leadership, motivation, and decision making. The
population is aging and this changing demographic could mean labour shortages, loss of
organisational memory, and generational differences in terms of values, skills, loyalty,
occupational mobility, and technical skills. Increasing gender equality, with more women
in the workforce, and in senior positions is also altering organisational membership
demographics, as well as breaking down the traditional idea that some jobs are gender
specific.
Employment relationships, and the psychological contract, are another challenging
aspect as flexible work arrangements become standard along with remote work. There is
also the growing casualisation of roles, which promotes flexibility for the organisation
but decreasing job security for employees, and encourages a gig economy. The emerging
gig economy, where people independently contract or freelance for multiple
organisations, also offers increased flexibility for workers, but is criticised as workers do
not receive sick leave, annual leave, or parental leave, and have very little job security.
These challenges change the way managers and organisations need to be, think, and act
in order to succeed and perform effectively. We study organisational behaviour to
understand the dynamics of why things happen in organisations, and to know how to
predict, and manage these events through adjusting the behaviours in and of the
organisation. As organisational behaviour practices are powerful predictors of an
organisations survival and success they are therefore important for both managers and
employees to know and understand.
