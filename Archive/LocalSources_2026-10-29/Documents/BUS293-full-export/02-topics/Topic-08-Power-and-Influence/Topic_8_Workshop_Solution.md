# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 10)

## Question 2 - Building power when you hold little formal authority

A lot of people assume power comes from your job title, but the chapter shows that is only part of the picture. Power is the capacity to influence others. It exists whenever someone perceives they depend on you for something they value, so even without formal authority you can hold real power if people need what you bring.

The five sources split into position power (legitimate, reward and coercive) and personal power (expert and referent). Someone in a junior or trainee role has very little position power, so the practical route is to build personal power. Expert power is the strongest lever here. If you develop knowledge or skills others value, and especially if you become the person who can manage uncertainties for the team, people respond to that expertise much as they respond to authority. Referent power is the other one to invest in. When colleagues like you, trust you and identify with you, they cooperate without being told to. The norm of reciprocity helps too, since doing genuine favours creates an informal expectation that people will help you back.

Having a source of power is not enough on its own. It only becomes real power through the four contingencies, and three of them are worth pushing on:

* **Substitutability.** Make yourself hard to replace by controlling knowledge or tasks that others cannot easily get elsewhere. Being non-substitutable is what turns expertise into leverage.
* **Centrality.** Position yourself so that a lot of people depend on you and feel the effect quickly if you are not there.
* **Visibility.** Let your competence be seen. It is the same reason professionals hang their degrees and certificates on the office wall.

The fourth contingency, discretion, is a useful warning. Front-line supervisors often look powerless because organisational rules give them little freedom to actually use the power they have. The point, then, is not just to collect sources of power. You have to operate where you have room to use them.

## Question 5 - Hard and soft influence tactics, and which are acceptable

It helps to separate power from influence. Power is only the capacity to influence. Influence is the actual behaviour that tries to change someone's attitude or behaviour, and it runs in all directions, upward to managers as much as downward to staff.

The chapter lists eight tactics. Five are hard tactics supported by position power: silent authority, assertiveness, information control, coalition formation and upward appeal. Three are soft tactics supported by personal power: persuasion, impression management (which includes ingratiation and building a personal brand), and exchange.

Soft tactics are generally seen as more acceptable than hard ones. Friendly persuasion and a bit of subtle ingratiation sit far better with people than assertiveness or going over their head with an upward appeal. Persuasion is usually the most effective for career success, particularly when the person is seen as credible, does not personally profit from the outcome, and acknowledges the other side's arguments. There is no single best tactic though. The right choice depends on three things: your own power base, whether the target is above, below or at your level, and the personal, organisational and cultural values in play. Silent authority, for example, is the most common form of influence in high power distance cultures, where simply complying with a manager's request is expected.

Two cautions are worth flagging. Extreme assertiveness can tip into workplace bullying, and excessive ingratiation reads as insincere and self-serving, so it only works in small doses and at lower levels.

## Question 6 - Organisational politics: what drives it and how to keep it in check

Organisational politics is the use of influence tactics that others see as self-serving, benefiting the individual at the expense of others and sometimes against the organisation's interests. It is not automatically a bad thing. Politics can be positive when it does not harm others and actually supports things like fair decision making, conflict resolution and maintaining relationships. The negative version is what diverts resources away from the organisation, and that is what lowers job satisfaction, commitment and morale and creates retention problems.

Politics tends to flare up under two conditions in particular: when decisions are ambiguous and involve allocating scarce resources, and when the organisation tolerates or rewards political behaviour. People with a high need for personal power are also more inclined to play this way.

Management can reduce dysfunctional politics rather than pretend it away. The main levers are clear rules for how resources are allocated, a free flow of information so people are not fighting in the dark, and using education and involvement when the organisation goes through change. Beyond that, team norms and a culture that discourages backstabbing matter, and so do leaders who model organisational citizenship instead of showing everyone that political savvy is how you get ahead. One more point: influence is read more positively when the person has high political skill, meaning they are socially astute, good at networking and come across as sincere.

## Question 8 - How social networks give people power

Power does not only come from formal roles. It also flows through social networks and the social capital they create. The two main sources of this kind of power are referent power, because people in your network identify with and trust you, and legitimate power through the norm of reciprocity, where favours owed turn into influence.

The interesting part is which connections matter most, and this is where centrality comes back in. Having weak ties across many different networks can be more valuable than a few strong ties within a similar group, because weak ties reach into places your close circle cannot and bring back information and contacts you would not otherwise get. The practical lesson follows from that: build a broad and diverse network rather than just deepening a handful of close relationships.

The flip side is that these networks are a natural part of organisations but can become a real barrier for anyone not already connected. The "boys clubs" that form in some managerial areas are the obvious example. They show why building a wide network early matters, especially for people who start outside the inner circle.

# Activity 2: Case Study - 'Resonus Corporation' (Chapter 10)

## Question 1 - Sources and contingencies of power among the executives and departments

The striking thing about Resonus is how far the real power sits from the organisation chart.

* **Doc Kalandry (research director)** holds the most power even though the chart puts him under Choy. His sources are personal: strong **expert power** as the "living genius" behind the company's successful products, and **referent power** from the quirky, enthusiastic style everyone loves, Hunt most of all. On top of that he has informal **legitimate power**, because Hunt actively supports Doc reporting straight to the CEO. All four contingencies work in his favour. He is **non-substitutable** (genius innovators cannot just be replaced), highly **central** because research drives the products and every design change ripples outward, and very **visible** through celebrated products and an over-the-top personality. He also has the **discretion** to keep launching last-minute changes.
* **Bill Hunt (CEO)** has the formal **legitimate, reward and coercive** power of a 15-year chief executive, plus **referent power** as the keeper of the founder's "family spirit." His own bias matters: he started in research and still favours that group, which is part of why Doc's informal power survives.
* **Jacqui Blanc (production director)** has formal legitimate power, but her real influence comes from credibility. By uncovering and cleaning up the earlier fraud she earned Hunt's trust, which is why he tolerates her formal style in an informal culture. Production also has **centrality** over output, since it controls what actually gets manufactured and in what order.
* **Frank Choy (director of engineering) and the ESD** are the weak point. On paper Choy oversees both ESD and research, but his position power is hollow because Doc ignores him and Hunt backs that. He is new (eight months), so he has little expert or referent power, and the role itself is structurally fragile, with the last two directors fired or gone inside 18 months. His **discretion is low** as well: he technically has authority but no freedom to use it, because saying no to Hunt or Doc risks his job. ESD is treated as highly **substitutable** and low status, getting the blame and little credit. The research engineers, not ESD, are the ones production trusts.

Power at Resonus flows to expertise, the CEO's favour and credibility, not to formal authority. That leaves Choy responsible for outcomes he cannot control.

## Question 2 - Influence tactics, and are they organisational politics?

Several tactics are visible. Doc uses **upward appeal**, taking his nano-processor idea straight to Hunt and bypassing Choy, backed by **persuasion** and his infectious enthusiasm (a form of impression management). Hunt leans on **silent authority**, both in urging everyone to "remain civil" and in asking Choy and Blanc to "show commitment," which is hard to refuse coming from the CEO. Blanc uses **assertiveness** and a conditional **exchange**, agreeing to deliver only if ESD provides accurate specs on time and the sales director stops overpromising. Choy mostly **yields and avoids**, swallowing his protest to avoid his predecessor's fate. The sales director uses a blunt political tactic, **implying Choy's incompetence** to deflect attention from his own wild delivery promises.

Whether these count as organisational politics depends on whether they are self-serving at others' or the organisation's expense. The sales director's blaming is clearly political, scapegoating Choy to protect himself. Doc's habit of pushing changes he "won't let go of," right up against production deadlines, is also political in effect: it serves his own enthusiasm while ESD and production absorb the cost in hundreds of change orders and delays. Hunt's ongoing favouritism toward research entrenches this. Blanc's conditions and insistence on guidelines are a different matter. They are not really politics, because they protect the organisation (quality, and a response to real fraud) rather than her personal interest. The case is fertile ground for politics precisely because accountability is ambiguous, time and production priority are scarce, and the culture tolerates the informal power games.

## Question 3 - Consultant's recommendation on the power dynamics

Resonus has one problem underneath the rest: a mismatch between authority and accountability. ESD is blamed for delays it cannot prevent, because the real power lies with research and the CEO. My recommendations target that.

* **Realign authority with accountability.** Either route research's work formally through engineering so Choy has genuine say over hand-offs, or stop holding ESD accountable for delays caused by late design changes. Responsibility without power is the structural fault here.
* **Introduce proper change control.** Doc's last-minute revisions are the actual cause of the delays. The firm needs a design-freeze and clear cut-off points before production begins, rather than endorsing changes three weeks out. This curbs the spec churn without killing innovation.
* **Manage Doc's power rather than remove it.** He is the innovation engine, so the aim is to channel his ideas through a feasibility and manufacturability check, and Hunt must stop reinforcing the informal bypass that lets research override engineering.
* **Raise ESD's standing and fix the politics.** Give ESD credit and visibility, route production's design-fault queries through the proper channel, set clear rules for production priority, improve information flow between the groups, and have Hunt model fairness instead of favouritism. The sales director's overpromising and scapegoating need addressing directly too.

Resonus does not need to lose its genius or its collegial culture. What has to change is Hunt letting collegiality mean that no one can say no to Doc while the engineering director is set up to fail.

# Activity 3: Team Exercise - Mapping our power and influence

Each of us identified our main source of power within the group and the influence tactics we tend to rely on, then we compared notes. We wanted to see how power actually works in a small team rather than just defining it.

Most of our power turned out to be personal rather than positional. The members others listened to were the ones with expert power on a particular part of the report, or referent power because people trusted and liked working with them. Nobody was leaning on formal authority, since there was none to lean on. On tactics, we mostly used soft ones. Persuasion and exchange ("I'll take this section if you take that one") came up far more than anything assertive, which fits the idea that soft tactics are more acceptable between peers at the same level.

One thing we only spotted afterwards was centrality. The member who held the shared documents and kept track of deadlines had quiet influence simply because everyone depended on them to keep things moving.

The exercise made it clear that even a team with no manager has a real power structure, and that it is built mostly on expertise, trust and who others depend on.
