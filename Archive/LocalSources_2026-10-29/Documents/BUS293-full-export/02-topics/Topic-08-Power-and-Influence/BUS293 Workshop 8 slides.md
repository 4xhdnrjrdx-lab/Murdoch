## Slide 5 — Chapter 10 Question 2

Until recently, a mining company's data resided in the department responsible for that information. Property data were on the computers in land administration, hydrocarbon data were in the well administration group, maps were found in the map department, and so on. The executive team concluded this arrangement was dysfunctional, so the CEO announced that all information would become widely accessible on a central server system. If someone needs a colour map, for example, they can retrieve it from the central server without going through the map department. Rather than welcome the change, employees in several departments complained, offering several arguments why other groups should not have direct access to their data files. Some departments tried to opt out of the centralized server system.
Using the model of sources and contingencies of power, explain why some groups opposed the central server model of data access.
_(Note on slide: This incident actually occurred in a resources company and was a major challenge for those implementing the change.)_

## Slide 6 — Ch10 Q2 (1/2): Sources of Power

- **Legitimate power** (in particular control over information). Each group had unilateral control (monopoly) over the distribution of knowledge within their department. Employees elsewhere have legitimate power to request that information, and the department probably has an obligation to fulfil the request — but the departments still experience power through the requesting process. If someone asks in an inconsiderate way, the department might fulfil the request more slowly or incompletely.
- **Expert power** — because the information was housed in one group under the old system, they would likely have more detailed knowledge of that resource.

## Slide 7 — Ch10 Q2 (2/2): Contingencies of Power

- **Non-substitutability** — Under the old system, each department had a monopoly over the resource and possibly gained special knowledge on how to access it, making them almost non-substitutable. Under a central server, information is easily/quickly accessible by employees elsewhere, so department staff lack control and are more substitutable.
- **Centrality** — Under the old system department staff affected others; they have less immediate effect (or affect fewer employees) when people can retrieve the information without department assistance.
- **Visibility** — Bypassing a department means they are less connected to the data and therefore less visible.
- **Discretion** — Department staff previously had discretion over whether, how and when to release information. However, discretion is a relatively minor issue because the department ultimately had an obligation to distribute the knowledge.

## Slide 8 — Chapter 10 Question 5

You have been asked to form and lead a task force of students to recommend improvements to an important college process (such as course registration, parking and public transportation, or grade appeals). The committee consists of one student from each of eight disciplines at the college. These people have diverse backgrounds and experiences (since their education programs are in different departments or faculties). You are selected to lead because you are in your final year and have succeeded in other student leadership roles.
Among the eight influence tactics described in this chapter, which two or three would you apply the most to steer the committee (such as keeping discussion on track, minimizing dominant members, and pushing agreement on various issues)? Which influence tactics are unlikely to ever be used by you in this committee role?

## Slide 9 — Ch10 Q5 (1/2) _(slide mislabeled "Q4")_

The answer relates partly to personal preferences (people feel comfortable with tactics compatible with their personality and values). Here the student leader has only **moderate legitimate power**; committee members are likely volunteers, so there's little **reward or coercive power**; students tend to have fairly **low power distance** in many cultures; and leader and members probably want to depict themselves as independent, mature decision makers (not reliant on college administration's power). Suggested views on each tactic:

- **Silent Authority.** Probably weak — you have relatively little legitimate power, and members value their power/autonomy in the decision process (unwilling to defer).
- **Assertiveness.** Might occasionally be used in its mildest form (reminding members of the deadline and the need to work effectively together). Any attempt to bully or punish would be disastrous — members don't feel a strong need to remain on the task force under those conditions.
- **Information Control.** Unwise and probably unavailable — no evidence the leader controls information unavailable to members; if it existed, members would rebel against the "framing" manipulation when it became evident.
- **Coalition Formation.** Applies only if a few members took a position and the leader worked with them to convince others; a leader using this would be viewed as biased, whereas task force leaders need more neutrality where groups make decisions and have low power distance.

## Slide 10 — Ch10 Q5 (2/2) _(slide mislabeled "Q4")_

- **Upward Appeal.** Might be used if members act outside acceptable behaviour — warning offenders they'd be reported to the college, or reminding them of college policies/expectations and the task force's mandate. Risky, because student task forces want to display capacity to lead themselves; calling on college administration (symbolically or actually) would be viewed with disdain.
- **Persuasion.** By far the most commonly used tactic to steer the committee (remind members to examine information, consider arguments/evidence, remind dominant members that effective decisions involve everyone, etc.).
- **Impression Management.** Everyone engages in some forms all the time; the leader would apply some, possibly effective at managing member behaviour (how the leader dresses/presents affects perceived legitimacy/respect; ongoing positive views of members increase willingness to defer). **Ingratiation** (congratulating/thanking members for ideas) can boost the leader's persuasion — but only if sincere and logical.
- **Exchange.** Possible in some situations (agreeing to do something for support), but the leader has few resources to offer — maybe timing of meetings.

## Slide 11 — Chapter 10 Question 6

Consider a situation in which there is only one female member on a team of six people, and she is generally excluded from informal gatherings of the team. What kind of influence tactics can she use to address this situation?

## Slide 12 — Ch10 Q6

- Social networks generate **social capital** — the goodwill and resulting resources shared among members in a social network.
- A female team member in this situation is likely to miss opportunities and favours from team members (for both expressive and instrumental purposes), which is a limitation.
- It may be useful for her to understand the cultural situation first, because socializing with male colleagues is received very differently across cultures.
- She needs to approach the team leader to update them and request a mechanism whereby all work-related information is shared with her.
- She will need to be very watchful and diplomatic in all formal meetings.

## Slide 13 — Chapter 10 Question 8

A coworker suggests that the company where you both work would be much more effective if there were no organizational politics. What would you say to this person in reply?

## Slide 14 — Ch10 Q8

Organizational politics is well known for its negative consequences — associated with lower job satisfaction, organizational commitment, organizational citizenship, and task performance, and higher work-related stress and motivation to leave. Surveys report it as one of the major causes of work-related stress and a reason many employees prefer working away from the office. But it's essentially impossible to remove all organizational politics, for at least two reasons:

1. **Politics is a perception** observers have of an actor's influence tactics — that the behaviour occurs for personal gain at the perceived expense of others/the organization. People will assume organizational politics when adversely affected, even when the action benefits the organization and was intended to.
2. **Humans are sometimes self-serving** — occasionally protecting themselves even against the greater good. Some actions really are politics (applying an influence tactic for personal gain detrimental to others/the organization): e.g. employees/departments hoard resources when sharing would improve effectiveness; employees hide reputation-damaging information (filtering up the hierarchy) when fuller transmission would yield better decisions.

- Also, some employees have a natural tendency toward politics — those with a strong **need for personalized power** and those with **dark triad** personality traits.

## Slide 15 — Case Study Questions (section)

McShane and Von Glinow (2024) – Chapter 10 Case Study: 'Resonus Corporation'.

## Slide 16 — Case Study Question 1

What sources and contingencies of power existed among the executives and departments at Resonus?

## Slide 17 — Case Study Question 1 (answer)

Examine the power dynamics among the key players, particularly **Doc** and **Choy**. Key issue: why Choy and his team can't complete the engineering specification on time.

- **Doc Kalandry** wields tremendous power (wishes usually fulfilled) through **expert power** and **referent power**. Hunt (and others) recognize Doc as an authority on the product's technology; Hunt claims this expertise is key to company success. Doc's over-the-top enthusiasm/optimism are compelling (referent power), and Hunt identifies with Doc's department (Hunt's first job at Resonus).
- Doc has **all four contingencies** in his favour: **nonsubstitutable** (Hunt considers him unique/irreplaceable); high **discretion** (Hunt trusts him, lets him ignore deadlines and procedures); strong **visibility** (direct informal reporting to the CEO; quirky style / personal brand); considerable **centrality** (his changes/delays directly and immediately affect Choy and others; poor product development hurts the company in the market).
- **Choy** and his engineering services team have limited power. He's new (no referent power); he'd have expertise to secure a senior position, but it isn't mentioned, and the case notes perceptions that Choy's team lacks expertise ("We shouldn't have to prove our ability all the time, but we spend as much time defending ourselves as we do getting the job done."). The org chart shows Doc reports to Choy, but this **legitimate power doesn't exist in practice** because of Doc's informal reporting to CEO Hunt.
- On contingencies: Choy/team have **centrality** (their work affects production, sales, others) but this doesn't extend to Doc or his team (unaffected by Choy's work). Choy has **limited discretion** (can't dictate deadlines to Doc; must respond to whatever Doc provides; can't act without Doc's output). Choy has **visibility**, but it isn't visibility of power — he and his team are visible for _failing_ to meet deadlines (not the "visibility" in the power model).
- Overall, Doc has considerably more power, so his actions take priority and Choy's team can't achieve their objectives.

## Slide 18 — Case Study Question 2

What influence tactics were evident in this case study? Would you define any of these influence activities as organizational politics? Why or why not?

## Slide 19 — Case Study Question 2 (answer)

Influence-tactic information is more limited here.

- **Choy** takes no steps to influence others — he holds back, fearing the same fate as the previous two directors.
- **Doc** relies on **persuasion**, **silent authority** (his expertise), and possibly **impression management** to influence Hunt.
- **Hunt** relies mainly on **silent authority** (urging others to be more cooperative) but also used **assertiveness** when firing the previous director of engineering.
- The **sales director** also seemed to rely on **assertiveness** (becoming furious with Choy).

## Slide 20 — Case Study Question 3

Suppose you are a consultant invited to propose a solution to the organization's product-delay problems. What would you recommend, particularly regarding power dynamics among the executives and departments?

## Slide 21 — Case Study Question 3 (answer)

Most suggest Doc should report to Choy, not Hunt — i.e., Hunt should enforce the org chart (Doc as a direct report to Choy). Valuable for debate, but may not solve the underlying power dynamics:

- If Doc were required to report only to Choy, Doc might quit if Choy demanded strict schedules. More likely, Hunt would side with Doc if conflict became manifest (Hunt as final arbitrator). Given Hunt's desire for innovation at any cost and admiration for Doc (Doc's referent power over Hunt), Choy might give in to Doc's poor time management. Choy also has limited capacity to reject Doc's last-minute changes because Doc's group has more perceived expertise; public opinion would shift away from Choy if he used legitimate authority over Doc.
- **A more likely solution: a two-stage strategy.**
  1. **Persuasion** (initially subtle) on Hunt about the value of production deadlines — show costs of late completion, refer to collapse of other companies that released late/flawed products due to last-minute changes, have Hunt speak with CEOs who learned the value of schedule deadlines and the false benefits/high risks of last-minute changes. Choy should also subtly signal his own expertise (more power/influence, better persuasiveness).
  2. If persuasion fails, be **more assertive** and less avoidance-oriented in conflict handling — assert the importance of deadlines while using a problem-solving style to reconcile deadlines with Doc's need to add new technology. Interests may be perfectly opposing, so Choy may revert to a more forceful style; compromise might not work because Doc ignores middle-ground options.

## Slide 22 — Case Study Question 4 (extra discussion if time permits)

Analyze this case in terms of the sources of conflict and conflict-handling styles. Your answer should also briefly identify incidents of manifest conflict.

## Slide 23 — Case Study Question 4 (answer)

Clear evidence of conflict: Choy experiences conflict with Doc Kalandry and CEO Bill Hunt, even though it isn't manifest and Doc/Hunt may be unaware. Choy perceives Doc's actions interfere with engineering services completing specifications on time; Hunt is a partner in the conflict through his support for Doc.
**Structural sources of conflict at work:**

- **Incompatible goals** — Doc and Hunt want to push new technology into the product; Choy wants specifications ready on time. More tech changes → Choy's group less able to meet goals.
- **Differentiation** — Doc and Hunt value innovation, with low priority on timeliness/predictable scheduling.
- **Interdependence** — Choy's team depends on Doc for final product design — **sequential interdependence** (work passed from Doc's group to Choy's, not back and forth).
- **Scarce resources** — mainly **time**; Doc uses time Choy's team needs.
- **Ambiguous rules** — clear handover procedures exist but aren't followed; Hunt lets Doc ignore the schedule and discourages bureaucracy, preferring informal give-and-take, making rules less clear.
- **Communication** — less central, but managers aren't forming mutual understanding (Choy wants to raise concerns with Hunt but doesn't).
  Also illustrates the **avoiding style** of conflict, emphasized by CEO Bill Hunt ("I'm sure we can resolve these differences if we just learn to get along better"; "I spent too much time smoothing out arguments"). The problem: underlying causes aren't addressed, resulting in frustration, stress, and late deadlines.

## Slide 26 — Bank Consultants (answer)

- The three consulting groups have limited informal networking with each other → limited communication, collaboration, or information sharing.
- High networking within two groups (business services and information systems), but relatively low networking within the organizational consulting group (four distinct clusters apparent).
- A few individuals have considerable **centrality**, particularly the information systems group person at the bottom left of that group.
- Most of the organizational consulting group has fairly low power through networking (most powerful = person at far right of that group); the lowest power is among a few employees at the top far left of the information systems group.
- One person (possibly a newcomer) has no network linkage. If any cross-group collaboration exists, it's somewhat more between the business services and organizational consulting groups.

## Slide 27 — Social Network Exhibit 2: Retail Managers _(sociogram image)_

This diagram is a fictionalized version of the informal network among all district managers and regional directors of a national retail organization. Regional managers report to regional directors, who report to the vice-president. The executive team wants district managers to actively network with each other to share information, learn from each other, and develop cohesion within and across regions so they form a strong identity with their leadership roles and the organization.
Discussion questions:

1. What aspects of this diagram suggest that the network is not operating as effectively as possible?

## Slide 28 — Retail Managers (answer)

- Informal networking among district managers, the four regional directors, and the VP. The organization appears quite **hierarchical** — many employees interact mainly through formal reporting ties (e.g., in the top-right and bottom-left groups, two managers each network only with their boss). Regional directors communicate mainly with their boss (VP); only two regional managers have meaningful networking with other regional managers. Lack of networking from district managers to the VP further indicates a strong hierarchical culture. One exception individual also has several peer connections within and between regional groups.
- Networking among district managers is varied and generally low. The bottom-right group is well networked internally (several short connecting lines) but has minimal ties to other regions; coincidentally its regional director is the most networked with other directors (a savvy networker who encourages the same). The top-right group has minimal networking (low cohesion); one manager there has stronger connections to the adjacent top-left group (perhaps a recent transfer).
- **Regional directors** tend to be most powerful — information flows through them (**high betweenness**) to other regions. The bottom-right director seems most powerful (high **degree** = many connections; direct connection to two other directors = higher betweenness). **Least power**: district managers with only one link, invariably to their regional director.

## Slide 30 — Two Merged Firms (answer)

- Integration is far from successful — professionals interact mainly with peers from their same previous firm. Some cross-firm ties exist (possibly work-required) but are typically limited and weak. "Company 1" analysts are peripheral in clusters dominated by "Company 2" analysts, and vice versa.
- **Company 2 analysts are much better networkers** (more ties, short distances), likely from a stronger culture of networking and high social cohesion.
- A few very powerful individuals (near middle-centre and bottom-centre of the exhibit) — all former Company 2 staff. Company 1 staff universally have little **centrality** (few/long ties, limited betweenness) → least networking power.

## Key terms / frameworks / sources

- **Sources of power:** legitimate (incl. control over information), reward, coercive (position power); expert, referent (personal power).
- **Four contingencies of power:** substitutability/non-substitutability, centrality, visibility, discretion.
- **Eight influence tactics:** silent authority, assertiveness, information control, coalition formation, upward appeal (hard); persuasion, impression management (incl. ingratiation, personal brand), exchange (soft).
- **Organizational politics:** perception of self-serving influence; need for personalized power; dark triad traits; consequences (lower satisfaction/commitment/OCB/performance; higher stress/turnover intent).
- **Social capital & social networks:** centrality, degree, betweenness, weak vs strong ties, sociograms (network exhibits).
- **Conflict (Q4):** structural sources — incompatible goals, differentiation, interdependence (sequential), scarce resources, ambiguous rules, communication; conflict-handling styles (avoiding; problem-solving; forcing); manifest conflict.
- **Sources:** McShane & Von Glinow (2024) Ch.10 (p.382); case study "Resonus Corporation"; "Deciphering the Network" exercise (p.385).
- Power distance; norm of reciprocity (referenced in tactics).
