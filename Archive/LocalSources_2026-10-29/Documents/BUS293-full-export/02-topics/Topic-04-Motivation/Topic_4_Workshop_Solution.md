# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 5)

## Question 3 - Motivating game developers (intrinsic vs extrinsic)

Companies should design jobs that build intrinsic motivation first, then layer on extrinsic rewards in a way that does not undermine it. Intrinsic motivation comes from the work itself, especially when it fulfils the drives for competence and autonomy. That means developers need real autonomy (freedom from micromanagement and excessive rules) and tasks that are matched to their skills while still stretching them.

Extrinsic motivation takes three forms. There are clearly external rewards (pay, recognition), internal pressure that is still linked to external sources (not wanting to let the team down), and the internalised value of external sources (identifying with company values). We think the third is the most useful, because it feels internally sourced and is the least likely to reduce autonomy. To avoid undermining intrinsic motivation, extrinsic rewards should ideally be unexpected (a surprise bonus), low in value relative to the intrinsic reward, and not tightly contingent on specific behaviour (a fixed salary, for example). Companies should therefore be cautious about attaching performance bonuses to work that is already intrinsically motivating.

## Question 5 - Expectancy theory and developmental reviews

Frequent, strengths-based developmental reviews motivate more than traditional annual appraisals, and they do so across all three parts of expectancy theory. Timelier, more frequent feedback gives employees clearer information on whether their effort is actually producing performance, which strengthens the E-to-P expectancy. OB Mod and social cognitive theory reinforce this, since employees see the consequences of their own and their coworkers' behaviour sooner. Developmental conversations also help managers learn what each employee values, so they can clarify and offer outcomes with higher valence. On top of that, these reviews let managers spell out what outcomes actually follow performance, which builds clearer P-to-O expectancies. A strengths-based focus raises the E-to-P expectancy too (working to strengths rather than weaknesses) and points employees toward higher-valence work that fits their self-concept.

## Question 8 - 'Worth more than I'm paid' and 'better than average'

This reflects a non-rational self-enhancement tendency. Statistically not everyone can be above average, yet most people rate themselves better than average on skills such as leadership and interpersonal ability (think of the surveys where the large majority place themselves in the top tier). Because people overrate their own contribution, equity theory explains why they tend to feel underpaid relative to coworkers: they compare their (overrated) inputs against what others receive and perceive negative inequity. The same effect makes some motivational or corrective programs feel demoralising, because being told you are only average clashes with the inflated self-concept and produces frustration.

## Question 9 - Improving procedural and interactional justice

To improve procedural justice I would investigate whether decision makers are free of self-interest or favouritism, whether decisions use full and accurate information including counterarguments, whether the interests of all affected groups are actually heard, whether decisions follow ethical principles, whether criteria are applied consistently across people and over time, whether employees have a voice to present their case, and whether questionable decisions can be appealed to a higher authority.

For interactional justice I would look at something more personal. Are employees treated with respect and politeness, so their self-worth is preserved even when they miss out on a benefit? Do they receive well-justified explanations for decisions that affect them, along with honest, candid and timely information soon after the decision is made? Managers should frame those explanations from the employee's point of view, taking into account their assumptions and expectations about promotions and other allocations.

# Activity 2: Case Study - 'Cincinnati Super Subs' (Chapter 5)

## Question 1 - Symptoms that something has gone wrong

Several symptoms point to a problem. Food wastage is high, which drives up costs because food is being eaten and given away. Morale has dropped: employees are unhappy about the removal of free food and about management generally, and relations between staff and management have soured to the point where valued employees have quit. Productivity is lower as well, since inexperienced new staff and ongoing shortages cut output. Loyalty is very low, and the employees who remain stay mostly out of continuance commitment.

## Question 2 - Main causes

* **Lack of motivation to minimise food costs.** High wastage brought positive outcomes (enjoying free food, peer support, fulfilling social and status drives by giving food to friends, and redressing felt pay inequity), while keeping costs low brought almost no rewards and even some negative ones (peer criticism). The manager's dismissal threat had little effect, because some employees did not value the job and others doubted he could actually fire them (low P-to-O expectancy).
* **Team leaders lacked motivation and power.** Employees held more informal power (the threat of ostracism) than the team leaders' legitimate, reward and coercive power. The leaders gained no positive outcomes from reporting wastage, and risked losing friendships if they did, which expectancy theory and felt inequity explain neatly.
* **Ineffective use of punishment.** Punishment strained relations, only worked while the manager was physically present (so it was proximity-dependent), and caused disruption when employees quit, which pushed costs up further.

## Question 3 - Recommended actions

* **Link rewards to food-cost targets.** Offer a bonus for meeting monthly (or per-shift) food-wastage targets to strengthen P-to-O expectancy and reduce felt inequity. Bonuses suit employees who feel underpaid, though even token awards could get the process started.
* **Remove threats and punishment** (except for serious infractions) to begin repairing relations between employees and management.
* **Create positive social events** (promotions employees run, for instance) to build bonding and loyalty, with the manager seen as the source of those positive experiences.
* **Rethink the team-leader role.** A performance-based reward system reduces the need for supervision in the first place. If a power base is still needed, hire less socially connected supervisors, pay them more, and back them with a wastage bonus and reward and coercive power.

# Activity 3: Class Exercise - The Learning (Reinforcement) Exercise

This is a live in-class exercise. Three volunteers are each 'taught' to move an object using a different reinforcement strategy. Volunteer 1 gets punishment only ('no!' or 'boo', silence when correct), Volunteer 2 gets positive reinforcement only (clapping when they move the right way, silence when wrong), and Volunteer 3 gets a combination of both.

The expected learning point is straightforward. Positive reinforcement, and the combination, guides the volunteer toward the target far more quickly and with far less frustration than punishment alone. Punishment tells the person what not to do but never what to do, it produces negative emotion toward the source, and it is simply less effective. That is exactly why managers should rely mainly on positive reinforcement and use punishment sparingly.
