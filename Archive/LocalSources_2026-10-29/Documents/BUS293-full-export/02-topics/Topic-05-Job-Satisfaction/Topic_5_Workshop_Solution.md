# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapters 4 & 6)

## Chapter 4, Question 3 - Emotional labour for college instructors

Emotional labour gets hard when a job means dealing with people a lot, showing a range of emotions, and following strict display rules. A college instructor runs into this all the time. They have to stay calm but firm when a student asks for an extension yet again. They have to look genuinely keen in the last hour of class after already teaching for six hours. And they need to seem interested and respectful when someone asks a really basic question. That said, I think emergency call-centre operators have it worse. Their emotional events are far more extreme, they have to stay calm and console people, and they cope with the frustration of life-threatening situations. Those demands are generally more intense than anything an instructor faces.

## Chapter 4, Question 5 - Building the four components of emotional intelligence

For awareness of one's own emotions, coaching helps: trainees reflect on and describe what they felt during an observed event, then get peer feedback. This works a bit like the Johari Window, where feedback enlarges the open, self-aware area. The daily version is simple, just pause to label what you are feeling and why.

Managing your own emotions can be built through training events that force you to regulate emotion, such as handling a difficult customer, with coach or peer feedback afterwards. On a daily basis, ask coworkers how you came across emotionally once a meeting wraps up.

Awareness of others' emotions improves with practice reading emotions from facial expressions in images or clips, followed by discussion, plus genuine dialogue with people from other social groups to build empathy. Day to day, observe and describe how others seem to be feeling, and have relaxed, nondirective chats.

Finally, managing others' emotions is best developed through real-world activities or role plays where the trainee engages another party (a customer or an actor) and the coach and peers debrief on how well that person's emotions were handled.

## Chapter 6, Question 1 - Gainsharing vs profit sharing for four warehouses

I would go with a gainsharing plan in each of the four regional facilities. Gainsharing rewards a team for cutting costs and improving labour efficiency in its own work process, while profit sharing spreads a slice of corporate profits across a broad group. These warehouse employees have almost no sway over corporate profits, but they can genuinely move the needle on costs and efficiency in their own facility, and rewards land best when people actually control the outcomes being measured. Gainsharing is also team-based and targeted at the four facilities, which fits the brief: reward people inside those facilities rather than the whole organisation.

## Chapter 6, Question 4 - Five strategies for a performance-based reward system

**Link rewards to performance.** The system uses objective criteria (sales, returns, waste) and a bonus big enough to create positive emotions. The weakness is that store-level performance may weaken the individual effort-to-performance expectancy in larger stores.

**Ensure rewards are relevant.** Criteria need to sit within employees' control. Sales can be swayed by economic conditions or local demographics, so the company may need to adjust measures across stores to account for these external factors.

**Use team rewards for interdependent jobs.** A store-level reward fits here because staff are interdependent within a store but largely independent across stores. If some roles have low interdependence, the company can add individual-level criteria.

**Ensure rewards are valued.** A uniform lump-sum bonus leaves little room to tailor to individual preferences. One fix is to let employees convert part of the bonus into discounted goods or services.

**Check for unintended consequences.** The risks here are real. People may chase the measured outcomes while neglecting unmeasured ones like inventory and personal service, and some behaviours carry hidden costs, for example a reluctance to accept legitimate returns, which can harm long-term reputation. It is safer to pilot the scheme first. Gaming is unlikely given the objective data.

## Chapter 6, Question 5 - Job specialization in pizzerias

Busy pizzerias usually run fairly high specialization, with different people on dough, filling orders, unloading and cutting and boxing, the register, and delivery. That high specialization lifts efficiency: no time is lost switching tasks, skills develop faster (handy where shops lean on part-time or temporary staff), and people get matched to the task that suits them. Smaller shops tend to go the other way, with two people sharing most of the work. The contingencies behind the difference come down to shop size and volume plus technology. A conveyor oven supports two people loading and unloading, whereas a fixed oven may suit one person tracking cooking times. Some shops also rotate jobs through a shift to cut boredom and repetitive-strain injury.

## Chapter 6, Question 8 - Self-leadership to reduce supervisory costs

Self-leadership is the set of cognitive and behavioural strategies people use to motivate and direct themselves: setting their own goals, monitoring their behaviour, using positive self-talk, and rewarding themselves. It can take over some supervisory roles, which supports the city manager's aim, but it cannot cover everything. Managers are still needed for macro-level planning, dividing up work, and resolving interpersonal and interdepartmental conflict, and some employees just do not take to self-leadership easily. So the manager could reduce the number of supervisors without eliminating them, and at the very least free up supervisors' time as employees, once trained, take on more.

As a training module example, take personal goal setting. You teach the features of effective (SMARTER) goals, have participants write goals for their own job, and give feedback. A follow-up module then focuses on the daily habit, with a coach checking whether the employee set goals that day, how well they stayed focused, and how their goal-setting has improved.

# Activity 2: Case Study - ‘YakkaTech, Inc.’ (Chapter 6)

## Question 1 - Symptoms that something has gone wrong

There were several warning signs: poorer customer service, employee indifference to client problems, slow response times, and staff who lacked knowledge about each client. On top of that, staff turnover was high before the reward-system change, employee referrals were declining, and productivity dropped.

## Question 2 - Main causes

**Jobs have low motivational potential.** Run the work through the five core job characteristics and the picture is poor. Skill variety is moderate but turns monotonous. Task identity looks high per ticket yet is actually low, because the real "whole" task is the client's whole system. Task significance is low, since staff don't see their impact on clients. Autonomy is hemmed in by the ticket system. And job feedback is very low, because staff never learn whether their fix lasted. All of this predicts low meaningfulness, lower satisfaction, and lower effectiveness, which is exactly what the case shows.

**Poor alignment of money and rewards.** Raising pay and adding a vested profit-sharing plan did reduce turnover, but the design was misaligned. The rewards barely reflect individual performance in a firm this size, the bonus is delayed two years, and the system rewards individual membership even though the work is interdependent, with shared tickets and on-site teams.

**Continuance commitment ('golden handcuffs').** The vested plan makes leaving costly, which builds continuance commitment, and that tends to drag down job performance and satisfaction. Both got worse after it came in.

**EVLN.** The Exit, Voice, Loyalty, Neglect model captures the responses well: Exit (high turnover until the pay incentive), Voice (indirect complaints and survey results), little Loyalty, and Neglect (poor service, not passing on jobs).

## Question 3 - Recommended actions

**Redesign jobs through job enrichment.** Organise individuals, or better still teams, around clients so the same staff handle all of a client's IT problems. This lifts task identity and significance, sharpens client knowledge and feedback, and builds felt responsibility along with a psychological closeness to the client.

**Realign rewards.** Once jobs are redesigned around client teams, use a team-based reward tied to specific long-term client outcomes. Profit sharing could stay, but more proximate, performance-linked rewards are the better bet.

# Activity 3: Team Exercise - ‘Is Student Work Enriched?’

This exercise uses the Job Diagnostic Survey to estimate the motivating-potential score (MPS) of being a student. Each core characteristic is averaged from three survey questions, namely skill variety (SV), task identity (TI), task significance (TS), autonomy (A) and job feedback (JF), then combined as:

MPS = ((SV + TI + TS) / 3) × A × JF.

Each of us works out our own MPS from our survey answers, the team compares results, and we then benchmark them against the reference MPS scores for occupational groups (for example Managerial 156, Sales 146, US national average 128, Clerical 106). One discussion point worth raising is that student work often lands in the moderate range. Autonomy and feedback are usually the characteristics that swing the score most, up or down, and the exercise shows how the five characteristics combine to make work feel meaningful and motivating.
