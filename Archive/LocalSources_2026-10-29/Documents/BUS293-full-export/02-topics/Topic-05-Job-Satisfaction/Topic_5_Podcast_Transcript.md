Welcome back to BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour. Last time, we looked at the
conceptual foundations of employee motivation and the practical applications. Job satisfaction
can also be impacted by emotions, attitudes, and stress. In addition, intrinsic job satisfaction
can be improved through effective job design and flexible work arrangements. This topic
introduces various types of job design, and organisational strategies to manage stress and
improve individual satisfaction.
Job satisfaction represents a person’s evaluation of their job and work context, and
dissatisfaction with their job affects behaviour and results. There are four types of job
dissatisfaction consequences, which are similar to those experienced when there is inequity in
the workplace.
Exit – which is quitting or otherwise getting away from the dissatisfying situation.
Voice – which is attempting to change the dissatisfying situation
Loyalty – which is patiently waiting for the problem to sort itself out,
and Neglect – which is reducing work effort and performance.
Loyalty is the employee’s emotional attachment to, identification with and involvement in a
particular organisation. This contrasts with continuance commitment, which is a logical,
calculated bond with the organisation. Organisations can increase commitment through justice
and support, shared values, trust, organisational comprehension and employee involvement.
Emotions such as those that contribute to organisational loyalty, are physiological, behavioural
and psychological episodes experienced towards an object, person or event that create a state
of readiness. They are brief experiences, and influence behaviour without cognitive awareness
by predisposing us to either approach or avoid objects, people, or events based on the emotion
experienced. Emotions differ from attitudes, as while emotions are subconscious, fleeting
experiences, attitudes are judgements that are generally conscious, and stable over time.
Attitudes have traditionally been described as a purely rational process, but we now know that
emotions have an influence on behaviour that is equal to or greater than that of cognition. This
dual process is apparent when we experience an internal conflict between what logically seems
good or bad and what we emotionally feel is good or bad in a situation.
Emotional intelligence is the ability to perceive and express emotion, assimilate emotion in
thought, understand and reason with emotion, and regulate emotion in oneself and others. This
concept includes four components arranged in a hierarchy:
Self-awareness, which is perceiving and understanding your own emotions
Self-management, which is keeping disruptive emotions in check and displaying positive
emotions when required. This also includes generating and suppressing emotions.
Social awareness, which is perceiving and understanding the emotions of other people, this
includes having empathy, but also being organisationally aware and sensing office politics and
understanding social networks.
Finally, relationship management which is managing others’ emotions through consoling
someone who is sad, inspiring team members to complete a project, getting strangers to
comfortably work with you, and managing dysfunctional conflict between team members.
Emotional intelligence can be learned to some extent, particularly through personal coaching.
Jobs requiring a variety of emotions and more intense emotions, as well as in jobs where
interaction with clients is frequent and has a long duration, generally involves a large amout of
emotional labour. Emotional labour consists of the effort, planning and control needed to
express organisationally desired emotions during interpersonal transactions.
Cultures also differ on the norms of displaying or concealing a person’s true emotions.
Emotional dissonance occurs when required and true emotions are incompatible with each
other. Deep acting, which is visualising reality differently to produce emotions more consistent
with the required emotions, can minimise this dissonance, as can the practice of hiring people
with a natural tendency to display desired emotions.
Stress another somewhat subconscious adaptive response to a situation that is perceived as
challenging or threatening to a person’s wellbeing. It is generally seen as negative, called
distress, however some level of stress activates and motivates people to achieve their goals,
and this is known as eustress. The causes of stress include any environmental conditions that
place a physical or emotional demand on a person, and three stressors that have received
considerable attention are harassment and incivility, work overload, and low task control.
Interventions to manage work-related stress include removing the stressor, withdrawing from
the stressor, changing stress perceptions, controlling stress consequences and receiving social
support.
One of the main organisational strategies to remove stressors is the introduction of policies to
improve work-life balance. These include flexible work time, job sharing, telecommuting,
personal leave, child care support, and job crafting.
The design of a job is a situational factor and can significantly increase or decrease the stressors
of work overload and task control.
Job design is the process of assigning tasks to a job, including the interdependency of those
tasks with other jobs.
Job specialisation is standardising work procedures and employing people in clearly defined
and specialised tasks, for example jobs on an assembly line in car manufacturing. Advantages
include increased operating efficiency, low skill and low-cost labour, minimal training
requirements, and controlled production quality. However, specialised jobs lack challenge and
lead to boredom, and there are then costs in terms of higher rates of absenteeism and turnover,
and the need to pay higher wages for people to do these “unattractive” jobs. Increasingly, the
natural extension of job specialisation is automation, with machines replacing the employees
needed to complete these highly repetitive jobs.
Job enlargement emerged in the 1950s as an attempt to reduce the boredom associated with
simplified jobs. Employees were given an increased number of tasks, and this increased task
variety was presumed to reduce the disadvantages of job simplification and therefore increase
intrinsic motivation. However, job enlargement doesn’t necessarily increase responsibility, or
autonomy, it may simply just increase the number of mundane tasks an employee performs, so
there are limits as to how much it can satisfy an employee.
Job rotation is similar to job enlargement in that it increases task variety, but does this through
periodically shifting workers between jobs involving different tasks at a similar level. It has
the advantage of enabling younger employees to learn the ropes in an organisation by rotating
them through different areas. It also increases workforce flexibility, with the workforce more
adaptable to accommodate when employees are sick, on leave, or have left the organisation. It
has a similar disadvantage to job enlargement though, as periodically changing the mundane
tasks performed does not increase their intrinsic motivation potential, only increases the
horizontal loading, or breadth of jobs, by increasing the number of tasks performed. If job
rotation includes vertical loading, where job depth is increased through adding responsibilities
previously held by supervisors, then this can contribute to employee development and reduce
some of the previous disadvantages of job rotation. Vertical loading is a key part of job
enrichment.
Job enrichment is the practice of building motivating factors into job content. It focusses on
increasing vertical loading, rather than the horizontal loading which is seen in job rotation and
job enlargement. The job characteristics model sets out five core job characteristics required
for a job to be enriched. These are skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and
feedback. The core job characteristics then need to transform into employees feeling that the
work is meaningful, they are responsible for the outcomes, and knowledge of actual results of
the work. If this occurs, it should result in high intrinsic work motivation and satisfaction, high
quality work performance, and low absenteeism and turnover. However, the job characteristics
model also takes into account individual differences of growth-need strength, knowledge and
skill, and context satisfaction, and therefore the idea that different people will respond
differently to job enrichment. Two ways to enrich jobs are clustering tasks into natural groups
and establishing client relationships.
Job design is a major influence in empowerment, particularly autonomy, task identity, task
significance and job feedback. Empowerment is also supported at the organisational level
through a learning orientation culture, sufficient information and resources, and corporate
leaders who trust employees.
Money and other financial rewards make up a fundamental aspect of the employment
relationship and are another situational factor related to job satisfaction and performance.
Financial rewards also affect our needs, our emotions and our self-concept.
Organisations reward employees for a variety of things.
Membership-based rewards are those offered for simply being an employee of the organisation
such as fixed pay, benefits, and paid leave. They may attract job applicants, and seniority-based
rewards reduce turnover, however these reward objectives also tend to discourage turnover
among those with the lowest performance.
Job status based rewards include pay increases on promotion and position-level benefits. They
try to maintain internal equity and motivate employees to compete for promotions. However,
they tend to encourage a bureaucratic hierarchy, support status differences and motivate
employees to compete and hoard resources.
Competency-based rewards such as skill-based pay are becoming increasingly popular because
they encourage skill development. However, they tend to be subjectively measured and can
result in higher costs as employees spend more time learning new skills.
Task performance based rewards include commission based pay and profit sharing. These
motivate task performance, attract performance-oriented staff, and create an ownership culture.
However, they discourage creativity and tend to address symptoms, not underlying causes of
behaviour. Employees often perceive a weak connection between individual performance and
the organisational rewards such as profit sharing.
Reward effectiveness can be improved in several ways, which can be seen in relation to
expectancy theory principles. Organisational leaders should ensure that rewards are linked to
work performance, rewards are aligned with performance within the employee’s control, team
rewards are used where jobs are interdependent, rewards are valued by employees, rewards
have no unintended consequences and rewards are adapted to suit the cultural context in which
they are used.
Financial rewards and the design of the job have traditionally been considered as the main
elements of job satisfaction, however it is now known that emotions, attitudes, and stress can
have as much, if not more impact on how someone evaluates their job. In order to maximise
job satisfaction, organisations should focus on the factors they control, such as stressors, and
individuals should aim to be aware of their emotions and improve their emotional intelligence
and perceptions of the job itself.
