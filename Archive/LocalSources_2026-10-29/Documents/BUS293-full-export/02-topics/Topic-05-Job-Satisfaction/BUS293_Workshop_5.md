**SLIDE 5 — Chapter 4 Question 3**
A recent study reported that college instructors are frequently required to engage in emotional labour. Identify the situations in which emotional labour is required for this job. In your opinion, is emotional labour more troublesome for college instructors or for people working at emergency service call centres?

**SLIDE 6 — Ch4 Q3**
Three factors that provide challenges to **emotional labour**: frequent interaction with other people; need to display a variety of emotions; the organisation and job require strict display rules.

- A student asks for an assignment extension one too many times — the instructor must maintain a calm demeanour yet display firmness.
- The instructor has taught for six hours straight yet must maintain an image of fresh enthusiasm in the final hour.
- A student asks a profoundly silly question without realising it — the instructor must maintain interest and show respect for the query.
  Emergency operators face more extreme emotional events that test their ability to remain calm and console others, and experience more extreme frustration given the life-threatening nature of the situation for their clients.
  Reference: K. Tracy and S.J. Tracy, "Rudeness at 911," _Human Communication Research_ 25 (December 1998): 225–51.

**SLIDE 7 — Chapter 4 Question 5**
Recognizing and regulating emotions in ourselves and others is an important capability in the workplace. Looking at each of the four components of this concept, what special training and daily informal activities can help us to increase our emotional intelligence?

**SLIDE 8 — Ch4 Q5**
Reflect on how each quadrant of **emotional intelligence** can be bolstered through training.

- **Awareness of our own emotions.** Coaching can improve emotional self-awareness. Trainees reflect on their emotional experience during an observed event and describe it to the trainer; a group of trainees provides feedback to increase each other's awareness — like the **Johari Window**, where feedback increases the employee's open area (self-awareness).
- **Management of own emotions.** Training events requiring emotion regulation (e.g., handling a difficult customer) followed by coach/peer feedback on how well the trainee managed their emotions. Informal daily activities: asking coworkers to describe the employee's emotional presentation after a meeting.
- **Awareness of others' emotions.** Test the trainee's ability to "read" emotions from facial expressions (still images or short video clips) with group discussion; strengthen through meaningful dialogue with people in other social groups to understand their emotions (improve empathy). Informal activities: asking coworkers to describe an observed person's emotional state (e.g., watching a customer from a distance); nondirective casual chats to understand others' situations.
- **Management of others' emotions.** Best trained through real-world activities or acted role plays — the trainee engages with the other party (a customer or an actor) and the coach/fellow trainees debrief on how the target person's emotions were managed.

**SLIDE 9 — Chapter 6 Question 1**
As a consultant, you have been asked to recommend either a gainsharing plan or a profit-sharing plan for employees who work in the four regional distribution and warehousing facilities of a large retail organization. Which reward system would you recommend? Explain your answer.

**SLIDE 10 — Ch6 Q1**
**Gainsharing plans** are team rewards that motivate team members to reduce costs and increase labor efficiency in their work process. **Profit sharing** includes any arrangement where a designated group of employees receives a share of corporate profits.
A gainsharing plan in each of the four regional facilities would probably be more appropriate. The main reason: these employees would have a small effect on corporate profits but could significantly influence costs in their facility — rewards are more effective when employees have direct control over the outcomes measured. Also, gainsharing is team-based whereas profit sharing applies to all employees; the mandate is to introduce a reward system for people within the four facilities, not the entire organization.

**SLIDE 11 — Chapter 6 Question 4**
A national retail chain selling electronics and kitchen appliances wants to motivate employees to increase sales, reduce product returns (customer changed mind within 14 days), and reduce waste (less product packaging damage). Sales staff have different areas of knowledge (kitchen appliances, home Wi-Fi technology, home cleaning technology, etc.). Discuss each of the five strategies to improve reward effectiveness in the context of a performance-based reward system for this organization and each of its stores.

**SLIDE 12 — Ch6 Q4 (1/3) — the five strategies**

- **Link rewards to performance** — use objective performance criteria; ensure rewards are timely and significant enough to create positive emotions.
- **Ensure rewards are relevant** — reward people for performance within their control or influence; be ready to adjust performance measures for factors beyond employees' control.
- **Use team rewards for interdependent jobs** — better than individual rewards when employees work in highly interdependent jobs because individual performance is difficult to measure.
- **Ensure rewards are valued** — know your employees and what they need and want.
- **Check for unintended consequences** — use pilot projects to test impact and make changes before organisation-wide implementation.

**SLIDE 13 — Ch6 Q4 (2/3)**

- **Link rewards to performance.** The financial reward system seems to fulfill this — objective criteria, bonus possibly significant enough to create positive emotions. Weakness: "performance" is store-level, so the effort-to-performance expectancy may be weaker than purely individual systems, depending on store headcount.
- **Ensure rewards are relevant.** Performance criteria must be within employees' control/influence — generally true for all three factors, but sales may vary by economic conditions (regions) or local demographics (stores); the company might adjust measures across stores for these external factors.
- **Use team rewards for interdependent jobs.** The store-level system is compatible because employees have interdependence within each store and minimal interdependence across stores. But if an individual's performance has low interdependence with coworkers, the system needs more individual-level criteria.

**SLIDE 14 — Ch6 Q4 (3/3)**

- **Ensure rewards are valued.** The system distributes the same financial reward (likely a lump-sum bonus) to everyone, limiting adjustment to individual preferences; the company might offer to transfer some/all of the bonus for discounted goods/services below what employees would pay.
- **Watch out for unintended consequences.** Almost all performance-based financial rewards carry this risk. Two stand out for a store-level system: (1) focusing employees on measured outcomes and away from unmeasured ones (inventory, personal customer service, etc.); (2) encouraging behaviors with higher unmeasured costs but better measured outcomes (e.g., discouraging returns or being reluctant to accept returns with damaged packaging → better short-term scores but poor long-term customer reputation). Gaming the system is unlikely (objective info), though managers might shift costs/sales between fiscal periods. Cherry picking/cream skimming mainly applies to individual employee behavior, not the store-based plan.

**SLIDE 15 — Chapter 6 Question 5**
Most of us have watched pizzas being made while waiting in a pizzeria. What level of job specialization do you usually notice in these operations? Why does this high or low level of specialization exist? If some pizzerias have different levels of specialization than others, identify the contingencies that might explain these differences.

**SLIDE 16 — Ch6 Q5**
In busy pizza places, several people are assigned to specific tasks (one prepares dough; one or more fill orders; someone unloads, cuts, and boxes cooked pizzas; cash-register person usually unloads; others deliver). This relatively high **specialization** increases efficiency — time saved by not changing tasks, faster skill development (important where shops rely on students/temporary part-time staff), and assigning people to tasks matching their best skill (coordination for tossing dough, strength for cutting). Smaller shops may be run by two people sharing most tasks (except delivery). **Technology** plays a role: conveyor-type ovens allow two people to load/unload respectively; a fixed oven might work better with one person tracking cooking time. Jobs are generally specialized but there could be **job rotation** through the shift to minimize boredom and avoid repetitive strain injuries.

**SLIDE 17 — Chapter 6 Question 8**
The city manager of a large city government wants to reduce supervisory costs by encouraging employees to motivate and manage themselves much of the time. The manager has heard of self-leadership and believes it may be a key strategy to reduce the number of supervisors. Discuss the extent to which self-leadership practices among employees would support the city manager's objectives. Summarize the content of a training module that would improve any one of the self-leadership practices.

**SLIDE 18 — Ch6 Q8 (1/2)** _(slide mislabeled "Ch6 Q5 1/2")_
**Self-leadership** refers to specific cognitive and behavioral strategies to achieve personal goals and standards through self-direction and self-motivation. It is a replacement for some (not all) roles of a supervisor/leader: employees set their own goals, monitor and guide their behavior, motivate themselves through positive thinking (self-talk), and reward themselves (self-reinforcement). It does not completely replace supervisory roles — managers are still needed for macro-level planning, division of work, and resolving interpersonal/interdepartmental conflicts. Even under the best conditions, some employees don't easily apply self-leadership (effective with normal supervision but lacking the ability or personality). So the city manager might reduce the number of supervisors, but these issues limit how much/whether this can occur; at the very least, current supervisors would have more freed-up time on some tasks (goal-setting, motivating staff) as employees take on more through self-leadership training.

**SLIDE 19 — Ch6 Q8 (2/2)** — training modules per self-leadership practice

- **Personal goal setting.** Learn basic features of effective goals (SMARTER list, Chapter 5), write goals for their own job, and get feedback from trainer/manager. Another module focuses on the daily activity — a coach checks whether employees set goals that day, how well they stayed focused, and how they improved goal-setting.
- **Constructive thought strategies.** Similar to how David McClelland taught participants to develop a strong need for achievement, but here developing a positive "can-do" mindset: write positive stories about near-future selves, speak about themselves positively (with feedback when too self-critical), or work with a coach repeating they can be the best for a role. Another module covers **mental imagery** — think through (and write down) task steps, recognize what could go wrong, identify strategies to avoid/overcome problems (a form of scenario planning for individuals — see Chapter 7).
- **Designing natural rewards.** Practice natural rewards — trading off tasks, taking on more enjoyable tasks, changing how tasks are accomplished to make them more enjoyable, and reframing an unmotivating activity so it feels more motivating.
- **Self-monitoring.** Diagnostically review every way the current job gives feedback on specific tasks, then identify ways to make feedback easier to receive or, where lacking, strategies to generate it within resources.
- **Self-reinforcement.** Write out possible self-reinforcers at work and describe occasions they were applied; share examples (especially in similar jobs); a coaching module has a coach speak about a task and what reinforcer they'll apply on completion/milestone. The idea: understand self-reinforcement, find examples in their job/past behavior, and maintain vigilance of the process in daily work.

**SLIDE 21 — Case Study Question 1**
What symptom(s) in this case suggest that something has gone wrong?

**SLIDE 22 — Case Study Question 1 (answer)**
Several symptoms: poorer customer service; employee indifference to client problems; slow response; lack of staff knowledge about each client; high staff turnover (prior to reward system change); declining employee referrals; lower productivity.

**SLIDE 23 — Case Study Question 2**
What are the main causes of these symptoms?

**SLIDE 24 — Case Study Question 2 (1/2): Jobs Have Low Motivational Potential**
Analyze using the five core job characteristics:

- **Skill variety** — moderate (each ticket has some task variety), but work gets monotonous over time and departmental organization narrows the range of tasks.
- **Task identity** — seems high (staff complete an entire ticket), but the "whole task" is managing the client's entire system/subsystem; the ticket system results in fairly low task identity.
- **Task significance** — quite low; staff don't know how their work affects the client, and small tasks (low task identity) make significance less apparent.
- **Autonomy** — limited information, but the ticket system restricts autonomy (staff are fed precise jobs rather than arranging their own timing/variety).
- **Job feedback** — very little; staff don't know whether actions produced a long-term or brief solution (recurring problems become new tickets sent randomly to others), and limited direct client interaction reduces feedback.
  Diagnosis predicts low meaningfulness (verified in employee comments about feeling disconnected and not caring about client issues), lower job satisfaction, and lower work effectiveness — all apparent here.

**SLIDE 25 — Case Study Question 2 (2/2)**

- **Poor Alignment of Money and Rewards.** YakkaTech raised pay to among the highest in the industry (assuming higher morale, lower turnover, lower hiring costs, higher productivity) and introduced a **vested profit-sharing plan** (bonus paid only if the employee stays two years after it's awarded). These reduced turnover (both tied to continued employment) but were misaligned: the pay interventions were only remotely associated with individual performance (the profit-sharing bonus barely reflects individual performance in a company this size, and isn't paid until two years later), and the system relies on individual membership while the jobs have interdependence (on-site team, shared tickets across departments).
- **Continuance Commitment from Golden Handcuffs.** The vested plan makes leaving more costly (quit or sacked for cause = lose up to two years of bonus), creating **continuance commitment**, which tends to produce lower job performance and lower job satisfaction — both occurred after the plan was introduced.
- **Exit–Voice–Loyalty–Neglect (EVLN).** Here EVLN describes rather than explains: Exit (high turnover until pay incentive introduced), Voice (indirect complaints to management, survey results), Loyalty (not much evidence), Neglect (poor service, not passing on jobs).

**SLIDE 26 — Case Study Question 3**
What actions should YakkaTech executives take to correct these problems?

**SLIDE 27 — Case Study Question 3 (answer)**

- Reorganize jobs to increase motivational potential and improve coordination for interdependent work. **Job enrichment** around establishing client relationships suits this — individuals or (more likely) teams organized around clients, so clients approach the same staff for all IT problems. Staff feel greater task significance and task identity, understand client needs better, get better feedback on intervention effectiveness, and feel more responsibility (greater psychological closeness to the client).
- Revise reward and recognition practices to align with the work and desired attitudes/behaviors. If jobs are redesigned around teams and clients, a team-based reward system could be considered (specific people identified with specific long-term client successes/failures). Profit sharing might remain, but more proximate rewards are preferred.

**Calculating the motivating-potential score.** _Scoring core job characteristics:_ Use the following calculations to estimate the motivating-potential score for the job of being a student, using your answers from the job diagnostic survey completed earlier.

- Skill variety (SV) = (Question 3 + 6 + 9) / 3
- Task identity (TI) = (Question 2 + 7 + 12) / 3
- Task significance (TS) = (Question 4 + 10 + 15) / 3
- Autonomy (A) = (Question 1 + 11 + 14) / 3
- Job feedback (JF) = (Question 5 + 8 + 13) / 3
  **Calculating MPS:** MPS = ((SV + TI + TS) / 3) × A × JF. Note that skill variety, task identity, and task significance are averaged before being multiplied by autonomy and job feedback.
