# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 13)

## Question 3 - Span of control in modern manufacturing

The old administrative theorists favoured a narrow span of control. They assumed direct supervision was the main way to coordinate work. Today's top manufacturers can run a much wider span, and they manage it because they coordinate through other mechanisms instead of a manager watching over each worker. Standardised work processes, self-directed teams, information systems and well-trained, multiskilled staff do a lot of the coordinating that a supervisor used to do. One manager can therefore be responsible for far more people without losing control.

A flatter structure also brings real benefits, which is part of why firms move to it. There are fewer middle managers, so overheads drop and more of the payroll goes into people who actually produce the goods. A wider span forces less close supervision. That in turn gives teams more autonomy and a real say in decisions, and that tends to suit the high-performance practices many manufacturers now use.

A narrow span still makes sense in some cases. Where the workforce is low skilled and the tasks are not yet standardised, closer supervision is needed to keep quality and safety acceptable. It also fits a workforce high in power distance, because those employees expect and accept close direction. They would not read tight supervision as a sign that management does not trust them.

## Question 4 - Determining the degree of decentralisation

As organisations grow and their environments become more complex, they usually have to decentralise. Once senior executives can no longer process every decision themselves without becoming a bottleneck, the sensible response is to push authority down to the people who hold the relevant information. The tricky part is judging how far to go, and the contingencies of organisational design give us the main things to weigh up.

The external environment matters first. Decentralisation suits complex environments, where the people closest to the situation have the information to make better calls. In a genuinely hostile environment, though, it is often better to centralise temporarily so the firm can respond quickly and decisively. Organisational size pulls the same way: larger organisations, with more specialised jobs and more elaborate coordinating mechanisms, generally need to decentralise so the top is not overloaded. Technology counts too. Where the work involves many exceptions to standard procedure (high variety) and the outputs are hard to standardise (low analysability), decentralised decision making works better.

All of the above are then filtered through strategy. Johnson & Johnson, for example, runs a decentralised divisional structure precisely because its strategy is to develop and deliver a wide spread of health products across the world. So there is no single correct level. The company has to read its environment, size, technology and strategy together and decentralise to match.

## Question 5 - Coordination mechanisms in mechanistic and organic structures

There are three coordinating mechanisms: informal communication, formal hierarchy, and standardisation. Mechanistic and organic forms lean on different ones.

A mechanistic structure has high formalisation and centralisation, a narrow span of control, and rigidly defined tasks. With decision making concentrated at the top and behaviour tied down by rules, coordination runs mainly through the formal hierarchy and standardisation. Managers direct work through their authority, and standard processes and outputs keep everyone aligned.

An organic structure works differently. It has a wide span of control, decentralised decisions and little formalisation, so there are fewer rules and less supervision to fall back on. People coordinate far more through informal communication, sharing information directly and building common mental models to synchronise their work. This is why organic forms depend on employees having well-developed roles and expertise. They have to coordinate themselves rather than wait to be told.

## Question 7 - Working in a matrix structure (employee perspective)

For employees, a well-run matrix structure has clear upsides. It improves communication across the project and the functional side of the business, and it gives some flexibility to balance workload with other members of a project team. People also get to interact and coordinate with others in their own technical speciality, which keeps their expertise sharp. Because a matrix demands strong communication and interpersonal skills, employees often pick up better training and development through working in it.

The main drawback is the two-boss problem. Reporting to both a functional manager and a project manager means dealing with two sets of expectations that may pull in different directions, and that can raise stress, conflict and organisational politics. The structure is also relatively fluid compared with a clear bureaucratic chain of command. Some employees find that ambiguity hard to adjust to, since it is not always obvious who has the final say on priorities.

# Activity 2: Case Study - 'Sloan & Wu, Inc' (Chapter 13)

## Question 1 - The proposed changes in terms of the four elements of structure

The change to **span of control** goes in two directions. At district level the span narrows: district managers now look after about 10 stores rather than 15, so they can work more closely with each store manager. At regional level the span widens sharply. The firm drops from five regions to three, removes the assistant regional manager layer, and expands from 30 to 45 districts, so each regional manager now oversees roughly 15 district managers instead of a handful.

On **centralisation / decentralisation**, when Sloan & Wu acquired the three regional chains it centralised a lot of activity, especially merchandising and marketing, which is why stock ended up in the wrong places (winter coats in Florida). The new structure clearly decentralises, handing merchandising and marketing decisions to district and store managers so they can tailor the offer to local customers. The CEO describes it as a "devolved" structure.

**Formalisation** moves both ways. It falls in some respects, because district and store managers gain autonomy and more flexible processes for dealing with vendors. But it rises in others. New technology tracks what is in stock at each store and records the brands, sizes and colours that local customers prefer, and managers must work within that system feedback, which is itself a form of formalisation.

For **departmentalisation**, the visible change is mainly in the number of units at each level: 45 districts across three regions (East, South, West) instead of 30 across five. Most of the other changes, such as the decentralised power and the shift of merchandising and marketing staff out to the districts, would not actually show up on the organisation chart.

## Question 2 - Contingencies suggesting the structure is appropriate or not

* **Dynamic vs stable.** Customer preferences vary by area and shift fairly quickly, which is why merchandising and marketing have been pushed down to the local level. But a lot of the business is stable. How customers shop, buy online and return goods is fairly predictable over the medium term, as are vendor processes. So Sloan & Wu needs a structure sitting between organic and mechanistic, and decentralising with somewhat less formalisation at the local level fits that.
* **Complex vs simple.** A national department-store chain with many products and diverse customers operates in a moderately complex environment, and the more complex the environment the more decentralisation helps. The new structure moves in that direction.
* **Diverse vs integrated.** The firm is diverse, mainly geographically. A full divisional structure aligned to that diversity is probably not warranted, since the real variation is at district level (45 geographic divisions would be unworkable) and customers are not all that different across regions for items like kitchen appliances. Decentralisation should be enough without a divisional structure.
* **Hostile vs munificent.** There is no sign of severe resource scarcity or extreme competition. The firm lost some market share through over-standardised buying but remains competitive, so hostility is not really a driving contingency here.
* **Organisational size.** It is a large firm, so it leans on standardisation (inventory systems) and formal authority through the district managers as coordinating mechanisms. That is broadly appropriate for its size.
* **Technology.** Most staff perform tasks with fairly low variability and medium-to-high analysability, because buying behaviour is reasonably predictable. That points to a structure somewhat more mechanistic than organic, close to what the new design actually is.
* **Strategy.** The CEO signals a shift toward creativity and innovation rather than pure cost efficiency, which argues for a more organic structure. Whether the new design is organic enough is debatable, but it is moving the right way.

Taken together, the new structure looks reasonably well matched to Sloan & Wu's contingencies. The main question mark is whether it is organic enough for the stated strategy.

## Question 3 - Problems the firm might experience

The first risk is **inflated costs**. There are 50% more districts than before, and merchandising and marketing now happen more at district level, which probably needs more people overall than the old regional offices did. The narrower district span (around 10 stores each) also means more managers on the payroll, so costs could climb.

A second worry is **formal authority dressed up as empowerment**. There is a real chance that centralised head-office control is simply replaced by district managers controlling store managers more closely. If that happens, store-level staff are not really empowered. They are just supervised by someone nearer to them.

The third problem is **too much variability**. Decentralised decisions can create inconsistency across the chain. Localising the offer suits some products but not all, and customers visiting a store in another region may be put off by big differences in format or merchandising. Local autonomy also raises the chance of poor or even unethical decisions. For instance, district managers dealing directly with vendors could lead to questionable arrangements, and districts might end up competing against each other for stock and paying higher prices.

# Activity 3: Team Exercise - The Club Ed Exercise

The exercise asks the team to design and justify an organisational chart for Club Ed at four stages of its growth. We need to show how the most suitable structure changes as the business grows and diversifies.

* **Scenario 1, one new resort, ~75 staff.** A functional structure works best here, grouping employees around areas such as food and beverage, housekeeping, front office, maintenance and administration. A simple owner-supervised structure is hard to sustain with 75 employees, and a fully team-based form is more organic than this fairly routine, single-site operation needs. The contingencies point to something more mechanistic than organic.
* **Scenario 2, 17 resorts across three regions.** Most teams move to a geographic divisional structure, with a regional manager over each region. It is worth questioning whether that regional layer is really needed at this size, since the resort managers could report fairly directly to the CEO or COO. If a divisional form is justified, it should be on geography mainly where the resorts genuinely differ by region or clientele (party-oriented, relaxed, family-focused).
* **Scenario 3, 30 resorts plus a charter airline.** By 30 resorts a geographic divisional structure generally makes sense, though again the span of control and the coordinating mechanisms should be examined before assuming a regional layer is essential. The airline adds complexity. It can sit as a fairly independent division, since it carries both Club Ed guests and outside customers and needs little integration with the resorts. Some teams would instead run the resorts as a matrix organised by geography and function (food services, housekeeping and so on).
* **Scenario 4, 75 resorts, the airline and an acquired adventure-travel agency.** This is best handled as a product-based divisional structure with three products (resorts, airline and adventure travel), using a geographic divisional structure inside the resorts division and a functional structure for the other two. The key judgement is whether Club Ed should integrate the three to build synergies (using resort strengths to support adventure travel and the airline), or run them as largely independent businesses, in which case it effectively becomes a holding company.

**Discussion.** Club Ed's structure shifts from simple/functional toward divisional and then product-based as it grows, driven by increasing size, a more complex and diverse environment, and a strategy of expansion and diversification. It can become adaptive rather than purely reactive by building flexibility in. That means using more organic, team-based and information-sharing arrangements, scanning its markets, and shaping its own environment through its strategy rather than only responding to changes after they hit.
