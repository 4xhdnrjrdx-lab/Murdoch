**SLIDE 5 — Chapter 2 Question 2**
All candidates applying for a management trainee position are given a personality test that measures the five dimensions in the five-factor model. Which personality traits would you consider most important for this type of job? Explain your answer.

**SLIDE 6 — Ch2 Q2 (1/2)**
Each Big Five personality dimension is associated with various forms of behavior and performance. All five dimensions predict employee performance and related behaviors to some extent, but three probably apply best to managerial roles.
**Conscientiousness** (especially industriousness and dutifulness) is important because it is the best predictor of proficient task performance in almost every job group. Conscientious employees set higher personal goals, are more motivated, and have higher performance expectations than those with low conscientiousness. They also tend to have higher levels of organizational citizenship and are less likely to engage in counterproductive work behaviors.

**SLIDE 7 — Ch2 Q2 (2/2)**
**Extroversion** is the second best predictor of proficient task performance. Extraverts frame situations as challenges and are comfortable interacting with and influencing people. Extraversion is one of the strongest predictors of both adaptive and proactive performance. All of these factors—proficient, adaptive, proactive task performance, organizational citizenship, low counterproductive behavior, comfort in social and influence situations—make it important for managerial positions.
**Emotional stability** (lower neuroticism) is a significant predictor of adaptive task performance, relating to managing change. It is also associated (moderately or inconsistently) with task proficiency, organizational citizenship, and counterproductive behaviors. Emotionally stable people cope better with ambiguous situations, which applies to managers in more strategic roles.

**SLIDE 8 — Chapter 2 Question 6**
The CEO and two other executives at an automotive parts manufacturer were recently fired after being charged with fixing prices on several key automotive parts sold to the auto industry. Executives at competing manufacturers face the same charges for also participating in this collusion. Profit margins have come under intense pressure in the industry, which could cause one or more auto parts firms (possibly this company) to go bankrupt. When the wrongdoing was discovered, most employees involved in product pricing (but not implicated in price-fixing) were surprised. The executives were highly respected in their fields of expertise, so many staff members interpreted the unusual pricing decisions as a new strategy, not an illegal activity. Apply your knowledge of personal and ethical values and behavior to explain why the unethical activity may have occurred.

**SLIDE 9 — Ch2 Q6 (1/2)**
There are two parts to the answer: (a) ethical concepts related to the wrongdoing; (b) ethical concepts related to how others reacted to the unethical decisions.
**Ethical analysis of the wrongdoing**—These companies are experiencing lower profit margins and one or more firms could go bankrupt. At least short-term, executives could be under tremendous pressure from shareholders to maintain profit margins, motivating them to do so illegally. Some could be motivated by the prospect of running a business into bankruptcy, which is disastrous for an executive's résumé and would face strife/anger from employees who lose their jobs. These "situational" conditions are actually motivational influences—they do not require or prohibit the executives from engaging in wrongdoing.

**SLIDE 10 — Ch2 Q6 (2/2)**
**Ethical analysis of product pricing employees**—Product pricing employees were surprised when executives were charged with price-fixing, highlighting their low moral sensitivity. They failed to detect the moral issue, likely because they had less expertise regarding prescriptive norms and rules. They had core knowledge due to job expertise, but believed executive directives reflected even greater knowledge rather than illegal activity. (NOTE: This misunderstanding has actually occurred in some of the most serious corporate accounting fraud incidents.) Staff may also have had fairly low mindfulness of price-fixing risks in their day-to-day work; greater sensitivity might have led some to question the executive directives.

**SLIDE 11 — Chapter 2 Question 8**
People in a particular South American country have high power distance and high collectivism. What does this mean, and what are the implications when you (a senior executive) visit employees working for your company in that country?

**SLIDE 12 — Ch2 Q8**
In **high power distance** cultures, people tend to accept the power differential in their society, which extends to the workplace. I would expect employees to address me by my surname; I would not interpret this as aloof or unfriendly. Social interchange would be formal.
People from **high collectivism** cultures define themselves by group membership and value the well-being of people within those groups. It would encourage me to celebrate the achievements of everyone as a group. Any discussion would emphasize improving or maintaining group harmony and teamwork.

**SLIDE 13 — Chapter 3 Question 4**
Several years ago, senior executives at energy company CanOil wanted to acquire an exploration company (HBOG) owned by another energy company, AmOil. Rather than face a hostile takeover and unfavourable tax implications, CanOil's two top executives met with the CEO of AmOil to discuss a friendly exchange of stock. AmOil's chief executive was previously unaware of CanOil's plans, and as the meeting began, warned that he was there merely to listen. The CanOil executives were confident AmOil wanted to sell HBOG because energy legislation at the time made HBOG a poor investment for AmOil. AmOil's CEO remained silent for most of the meeting, which CanOil executives interpreted as implied agreement to proceed to buy AmOil stock on the market. But when CanOil launched the stock purchase a month later, AmOil's CEO was both surprised and outraged. He thought he had given them the cold shoulder, remaining silent to show disinterest. The misunderstanding nearly bankrupted CanOil because AmOil reacted by protecting its stock. What perceptual problem(s) occurred that led to this misunderstanding?

**SLIDE 14 — Ch3 Q4**
This incident describes true events in Dome Petroleum's acquisition of Conoco subsidiary Hudson's Bay Oil and Gas (HBOG). [See: J. Lyon, *The Rise and Fall of the House that Jack Built* (Toronto: McMillan, 1983).] HBOG's high acquisition price (due to Conoco's resistance strategy), other debts, increasing interest rates, and falling oil prices put Dome on the brink of bankruptcy. The company was later acquired by Amoco.
This is a classic example of the **false-consensus effect**. The CanOil executives interpreted the AmOil CEO's silence as tacit agreement, probably because they wanted to believe AmOil would support the stock swap. The plan was critical to the executives' personal goals and was a high-risk strategy relying on questionable accounting procedures—conditions likely to further add to the perceptual bias that the AmOil CEO was on side.

**SLIDE 15 — Chapter 3 Question 6**
During a diversity management session, a manager suggests that stereotypes are a necessary part of working with others. "I have to make assumptions about what's in the other person's head, and stereotypes help me do that," she explains. "It's better to rely on stereotypes than to enter a working relationship with someone from another culture without any idea of what they believe in!" Discuss the merits of and problems with the manager's statement.

**SLIDE 16 — Ch3 Q6**
This raises the fundamental dilemma that **stereotyping** is both a necessary and a troublesome activity in the perceptual process.
On the one hand, the manager is correct that stereotyping helps fill in missing information, such as non-observable characteristics that become apparent only after a long time. Stereotyping also helps organise information more efficiently by placing people into predetermined categories.
Although most stereotypes have some truth, there is plenty of error and distortion that could lead to employment discrimination. It is unlikely someone perfectly fits the stereotype category, and when we stereotype we tend to assign traits that are untrue. Stereotyping makes it difficult to alter beliefs about a person regarding traits that conflict with the stereotype.

**SLIDE 17 — Chapter 3 Question 8**
Self-awareness is increasingly recognised as an important ingredient for effective leadership. Suppose you are responsible for creating a leadership development program in a government organisation. What activities or processes would you introduce to help participants constructively develop better self-awareness of their personality, values and personal biases?

**SLIDE 18 — Ch3 Q8**
This lays the foundation for leadership development as well as improving perceptions about ourselves and others. The quick answer is for leadership trainees to receive more feedback about themselves, which can occur through novel experiences that provide feedback about their behaviour and performance. One example is an **assessment centre**, where observers monitor participant behaviours during exercises and later provide confidential feedback. A related activity is **360-degree feedback**, where trainees receive systematically compiled feedback from peers, subordinates and bosses, offering clarity about strengths and areas for improvement.

**SLIDE 19 — Case Study Questions (section)**
McShane and Von Glinow (2024) – Chapter 3 Case Study: 'Bridging the two worlds', page 110.

**SLIDE 20 — Case Study Question 1**
What symptoms in this case suggest that something has gone wrong?

**SLIDE 21 — Case Study Question 1 (answer)**
The main symptom at the end of the case is that John, a productive employee, was upset/dissatisfied with management's offer for him to take a management skills seminar. He was ready to quit and leave the company. He also formed an even stronger negative image of George and other managers. Another symptom is the ongoing negative attitudes that shop floor employees have of management.

**SLIDE 22 — Case Study Question 2**
Explain the causes of these symptoms using your knowledge of self-concept and perceptions concepts and theories. Analyze the case facts separately for each theory and refer to the symptom(s) that the theory explains from the facts.

**SLIDE 23 — Case Study Question 2 (1/2)**
**Stereotyping:** Shop floor employees rely on stereotypes of "management" to interpret management's actions without considering alternative reasoning. Look at the foundations of stereotyping—categorization, homogenization, and differentiation. Shop floor employees cluster (categorize) all managers and assign common attributes (homogenization). They distinguish the author of the case only after some personal interaction. They reveal a negative view of managers versus their own capabilities and ethics (differentiation). This stereotyping, particularly differentiation, partly explains why John is dissatisfied with the offer of management training. A similar analysis of managers' perceptions of employees could be done, though there is less information.
**Self-concept and social identity:** It can be inferred that John has a self-view firmly anchored in his shop floor role, apparent when he considers management training an insult. His self-concept shows through his social interaction with other shop floor employees and his status in that group—discussed via social identity theory. George may also be understood through his social identity with managers and self-concept as a supervisor, though we have less information.

**SLIDE 24 — Case Study Question 2 (2/2)**
**Contact hypothesis:** Although introduced as a way to improve perceptions (a solution), it can diagnose why John and George misunderstand each other. Both have very limited direct contact—they work in separate physical spheres with limited direct communication. George's communication is formal (written), conveying status differences and power. They have no interaction through common equal-status activities. With the previous analyses, the contact hypothesis explains why George and John have low empathy—they don't understand each other's intentions or anticipated feelings.
**Communication:** George's use of memos is an ineffective communication channel that amplifies the misunderstanding. Management and shop floor employees also differ in jargon (from swearing to technical jargon), reinforcing distinct social identities.

**SLIDE 25 — Case Study Question 3**
What would you recommend to the manager and company overall to improve the situation (reduce or remove the symptoms) in the future? Provide recommendations for both immediate and longer-term actions.

**SLIDE 26 — Case Study Question 3 (answer)**
One of the clearest solutions is for management and shop floor employees to engage in **structured contact** and, ultimately, some form of meaningful interaction. A professional facilitator might get John and George together and explain the reasons for George's request. Care must be taken to present this as a reward, not a sign of dissatisfaction. Other actions: asking John to attend weekly management meetings or other management social functions, in line with future promotion.
The immediate action is to correct John's misperception of the management training program. The case author might take this communication role since he seems better at communicating underlying meaning than George. If George can communicate his true intent, a face-to-face explanation would be beneficial.

**SLIDE 29 — Bonus Team Exercise: Case 1**
An ethical dilemma at the forefront since the pandemic sent most employees into remote work. On one hand, the company wants to monitor employee behavior (as on premises) so customer data isn't compromised and employees maintain customer service standards and efficiency. On the other hand, monitoring in a home setting invades the individual's privacy in their personal domain. A separate work area at home might reduce this somewhat, but not everyone has a large enough home/compatible area for a separate workspace. Some monitoring methods are more precise and continuous than what employees typically experience at the office. The company's suggested benefits seem contrived—that webcams will help training, team building, and social interaction—and the "opt in" condition ignores the company's power in the employment relationship.

**SLIDE 30 — Bonus Team Exercise: Case 2**
Occurred at a small industrial services business in the UK (see: A. Levy, "Teenage Office Worker Sacked for Moaning on Facebook About Her 'Totally Boring' Job," _Daily Mail_, February 26, 2009). Several similar cases involve Facebook, blogs, and social media where employees write negative comments about employers. Parallels: (a) a Starbucks employee fired for complaining about his boss's decision; (b) a government marketing employee in northern Canada fired for posting artistic photos of garbage in the snow. Some say the teenage Facebook complainer should be dismissed for lacking commitment to and appreciation of the job. The owner explained: "We were looking for a long-term relationship with Miss Swann as we do with all our staff. Her display of disrespect and dissatisfaction undermined the relationship and made it untenable." Others say the owner's actions were unethical because (a) he was snooping on the employee's private communication (though open for others to read), (b) her statements may have accurately reflected the work, (c) no evidence her performance was undermined by her statements/attitude, and (d) she did not name the company.

**SLIDE 31 — Bonus Team Exercise: Case 3**
Describes the health incentive program at Whole Foods, which led to considerable controversy. One main concern is the incentive is unfair because a person's weight (plus blood pressure and cholesterol, also considered) is to some degree beyond their control due to heredity or metabolism. A human rights expert pointed out incentives should apply only to conditions over which people have considerable control. Human rights advocates claim the program is unfair discrimination because obesity is a disability (at least in some circumstances). Another concern: it relies on BMI, a century-old measure criticized for poor measurement of obesity. CEO John Mackey acknowledged BMI's limitations but dismissed the issue, saying measures "do have the virtues of being relatively good, easy to measure and not too expensive to monitor." (See C. Tobias, "Whole Foods Controversy," _Canadian Business_, May 2, 2011; L. Sloane, "Why Whole Foods Wellness Program Hurts Employees: An Open Letter," _Psyched in San Francisco_, December 14, 2015.)

**SLIDE 32 — Bonus Team Exercise: Case 4**
Refers to actions by Hewlett Packard (HP). (See D. Pringle and S. Stecklow, "Electronics With Borders," _Wall Street Journal_, January 17, 2005, B1.) Though the sense of freedom to purchase globally may dominate, some agree companies are buffeted by currency fluctuations so much they cannot adapt quickly to price changes and shifting supplies. E.g., a large buyer of HP printer ink in Europe might ship much of it to the US if the Euro rises against the USD, causing a shortage in Europe. Others argue this supply shift is a small portion of supply in most regions, so HP's actions are unfair. In terms of **moral intensity**, few people are affected by HP's restrictions and it has low proximity (not nearby), so moral intensity is low. If you believe there is no (or minimal) moral dilemma, why was the story on the front business page of a leading newspaper?
**Update:** HP recently introduced printers that issue failure warnings when nonbranded ink cartridges are used, even though the non-HP products work. HP claims required use of HP-branded ink (costing more than luxury perfume per quart/liter) protects its intellectual property. Rejection of budget inks occurred after a firmware update, sometimes long after consumers purchased the printer and had been using generic cartridges (see Z. Kleinman, "HP Printers Start Rejecting Budget Ink Cartridges," BBC News, September 19, 2016, http://www.bbc.com/news/technology-37408173).

HP printers start rejecting budget ink cartridges
Published: 19 September 2016
Large numbers of HP printer owners found their printers stopped recognising unofficial printer ink cartridges on 13 September.
Dutch printer ink vendor 123inkt said it had received more than 1,000 complaints in one day. HP said that during its last firmware update, settings had been changed so HP printers would communicate with only HP-chipped cartridges. It also said some devices already had the functionality built-in.
"The purpose of this update is to protect HP's innovations and intellectual property," it said in a statement. It has angered some of its customers as HP branded cartridges are notably more expensive than unofficial brands.
'Cartridge problem'
Reported error messages include "cartridge problem", "one or more cartridges are missing or damaged" or "older generation cartridge". One contributor to HP's support forum said the firmware had been updated "without my permission" and an error message now said the ink cartridge was damaged. "I use it daily for work and now am in trouble," he wrote. "Others must have the same problem. Is there a way to stop HP from doing this to people who have bought their equipment?"
Affected Models and Compatibility
The HP OfficeJet, OfficeJet Pro and OfficeJet Pro X printers are affected. Chipped cartridges filled with alternative ink would still work, HP told the BBC. It said: "These printers will continue to work with refilled or remanufactured cartridges with an original HP security chip. Other cartridges may not function." "In many cases, this functionality was installed in the HP printer and in some cases it has been implemented as part of an update to the printer's firmware," it added.
'(Un)intended result'
123inkt said: "Printer manufacturers regularly execute firmware-updates, claiming that they improve the operation of the printer or solve security issues. The (un)intended result usually is that the operation of lower price private label cartridges is disturbed and error messages are triggered."
