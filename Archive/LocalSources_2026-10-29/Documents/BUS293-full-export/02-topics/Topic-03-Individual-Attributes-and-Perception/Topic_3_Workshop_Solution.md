# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapters 2 & 3)

## Chapter 2, Question 2 - Big Five traits for a management trainee

Three of the Big Five dimensions matter most for a management role. Conscientiousness comes first. It is the best predictor of proficient task performance in almost every job group, and conscientious people set higher goals, are more motivated, show more organisational citizenship and commit fewer counterproductive behaviours. Extraversion is the second best predictor, and it is one of the strongest predictors of adaptive and proactive performance. Extraverts frame situations as challenges and are comfortable interacting with and influencing people, which suits management well. Emotional stability (low neuroticism) predicts adaptive performance too. It helps managers cope with ambiguity and change, and that is important in strategic roles.

## Chapter 2, Question 6 - Why the price-fixing occurred

We think the wrongdoing comes down to situational pressures acting as motivational influences. With falling profit margins and the threat of bankruptcy, the executives were likely under intense pressure from shareholders to maintain margins, and bankruptcy would have damaged their own careers. These conditions motivated the illegal collusion, even though they did not require it.

The reaction of the pricing employees reflects low moral sensitivity. They failed to detect the moral and legal issue because they had less expertise in the relevant rules, and they assumed that directives from highly respected executives reflected superior knowledge rather than illegal activity. Because they were not mindful of price-fixing risks, they did not question what they were told. Greater sensitivity might have prompted some of them to challenge the directives.

## Chapter 2, Question 8 - High power distance and high collectivism

High power distance means people accept an unequal distribution of power, and this carries into the workplace. As a visiting senior executive I should expect employees to address me formally (for example by surname) and to keep social interaction formal, and I should not misread this as aloofness. High collectivism means people define themselves by group membership and value group well-being and harmony. So I should celebrate achievements as a group rather than singling out individuals, and frame discussions around maintaining group harmony and teamwork.

## Chapter 3, Question 4 - Perceptual problem in the CanOil/AmOil case

The false-consensus effect is at work here. The CanOil executives interpreted the AmOil CEO’s silence as tacit agreement, largely because they wanted to believe AmOil would support the stock swap. The plan was critical to their personal goals and was a high-risk strategy, and that desire biased their perception even further. They read the silence as agreement when it was actually intended as disinterest.

## Chapter 3, Question 6 - Are stereotypes a necessary part of working with others?

Stereotyping is both necessary and troublesome. The manager is partly right. Stereotyping helps fill in missing, non-observable information and organises information efficiently by placing people into categories, which is a natural energy-saving process. The problem is that, although most stereotypes contain some truth, they also carry significant error and distortion that can lead to employment discrimination. People rarely fit a stereotype perfectly, we tend to assign untrue traits, and once a stereotype has formed it is hard to change even when a person clearly does not fit it. So stereotyping cannot be avoided entirely, but relying on it the way the manager suggests is risky and should be checked against real information about the individual.

## Chapter 3, Question 8 - Developing self-awareness in a leadership program

The key is to give participants more structured feedback about themselves through novel experiences. I would introduce an assessment centre, where trained observers watch participants during exercises and later give confidential feedback on their behaviour and performance. I would also use 360-degree feedback, where participants receive systematically compiled feedback from peers, subordinates and bosses. That gives them clearer insight into their personality, values, biases, strengths and areas for improvement.

# Activity 2: Case Study - ‘Bridging the Two Worlds’ (Chapter 3)

## Question 1 - Symptoms that something has gone wrong

The main symptom is John. He is a productive employee, yet he became upset and dissatisfied when management offered him a place on a management skills seminar. He was ready to quit, and he formed an even stronger negative image of George and the other managers. A broader symptom is the ongoing negative attitude that shop-floor employees hold toward management generally.

## Question 2 - Causes (self-concept and perception theories)

A few perception and self-concept theories help explain what happened.

**Stereotyping.** Shop-floor employees stereotype ‘management’ and interpret managers’ actions without considering alternatives. They categorise all managers together, homogenise them by assigning common attributes, and differentiate ‘us versus them’ by viewing managers negatively relative to their own capability and ethics. This differentiation partly explains why John takes the training offer as an insult.

**Self-concept and social identity.** John’s self-concept is firmly anchored in his shop-floor role and his social identity with that group, which is why he sees management training as an insult rather than an opportunity. George can be understood the same way, through his social identity as a manager.

**Contact hypothesis.** John and George have very limited direct contact. They work in separate physical areas with little equal-status interaction, and this lack of contact explains their low empathy, since neither understands the other’s intentions or feelings.

**Communication.** George relies on memos, an ineffective channel that amplifies the misunderstanding, and the two groups use very different language (technical jargon, swearing), which reinforces their separate social identities.

## Question 3 - Recommendations (immediate and longer-term)

Immediate: correct John’s misperception of the training offer by clearly presenting it as a reward and a step toward promotion, not a sign of dissatisfaction. The case author communicates underlying meaning better than George does, so he could take on this role, ideally arranging a face-to-face explanation of George’s true intent, possibly with a professional facilitator.

Longer-term: build structured contact and meaningful interaction between management and shop-floor staff. For example, John could be invited to weekly management meetings and social functions in line with his future promotion. Increasing equal-status contact over time will reduce stereotyping and improve empathy and communication between the two groups.

# Activity 3: Bonus Team Exercise - Ethics Dilemma Vignettes

For each case the team evaluated how ethical the company’s action was, using the three ethics principles (utilitarian / end-result, duty-based / individual rights, social-contract / distributive justice) and the perceived moral intensity of the issue.

## Case 1 - Webcam monitoring of remote employees

Largely unethical. A utilitarian view supports protecting customer data and service standards, but monitoring through webcams invades employees’ privacy in their own homes, which is a serious breach of individual rights (duty-based ethics). The claimed benefits (training, team building) seem contrived, and the ‘opt-in’ ignores the power imbalance in the employment relationship, so consent is not genuine. The intrusion into the personal domain gives this a high moral intensity, so the team judged it unethical.

## Case 2 - Employee dismissed for a Facebook complaint

Mostly unethical, though arguable. From a duty and relationship view the owner argues that the employee’s public disrespect undermined the working relationship. Even so, the employee did not name the company, there is no evidence her performance suffered, and the owner was effectively monitoring her private (if openly readable) communication. Given the limited actual harm, dismissal appears disproportionate. The team leaned toward unethical while still recognising the employer’s relationship concern.

## Case 3 - Whole Foods health-incentive program

Ethically problematic. The incentive partly rewards factors (weight, blood pressure, cholesterol) that are beyond a person’s full control due to heredity and metabolism, which raises distributive-justice concerns and possible unfair discrimination, since obesity can in some circumstances be a disability. It also relies on BMI, a flawed measure the CEO admitted is limited. Because it can unfairly disadvantage a definable group, the team judged it unfair and ethically questionable.

## Case 4 - HP regional purchase restrictions and ink cartridges

The regional purchasing restriction has low moral intensity. Few people are affected and the impact is distant (low proximity), so on its own it is a minor ethical issue. The later firmware update is more troubling. It made printers reject already-working non-HP cartridges, sometimes long after purchase, which harms consumers and is arguably deceptive, and it weakens HP’s intellectual-property justification. The team viewed the regional restriction as low intensity but the cartridge-blocking as ethically questionable.
