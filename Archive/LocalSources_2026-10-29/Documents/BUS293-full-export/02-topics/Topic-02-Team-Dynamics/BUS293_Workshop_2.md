_Speaker notes (Slide 2 / general team theory):_ Under the right conditions teams make better decisions, develop better products and services, and create a more motivated workforce. Individuals are more motivated when working in teams than when working alone because: they develop a bond with their team and are motivated to fulfil the goals of groups; they feel accountable to fellow team members, who monitor performance more closely than a traditional supervisor; they see team members become benchmarks of comparison, motivating them to work harder because of apprehension that their performance will be compared against others'. Two limitations of teams are: **process losses** — resources (including time and energy) expended towards team development and maintenance rather than the task; **social loafing** — this occurs when people exert less effort (and usually perform at a lower level) in teams than when working alone.

**SLIDE 5 — Chapter 8 Question 2**
Although teams have limitations, they can also be very effective. Identify three reasons why a team of six students potentially produce a better report for a major course assignment than the average quality of their reports completed individually. Detail the motivational benefits as one reason why teams are potentially better.

**SLIDE 6 — Ch8 Q2 (answer)**

- **Larger pool of expertise:** Teams will have a wider range of expertise/knowledge helping them to discover relevant information more quickly and easily than a student working alone.
- **More hands on deck:** Individuals cannot read through as much content as a team of six students. Although there are challenges of coordinating the work, ultimately a team report will be based on a larger volume of conceptual resources.
- **Potentially higher motivation to prepare the report:** Team members are potentially motivated more when working in teams than when working alone because: each student has high accountability to fellow team members; they feel accountable to fellow team members, who monitor performance more closely; they develop a bond with their team and are motivated to fulfil the goals of groups.

**SLIDE 7 — Chapter 8 Question 4**
Consider the six team roles summarized in 8.6 (page 297). Which two of these best describes your role tendency or role preference in a typical team at school or work. What factors do you think influence a person's role preferences?
ROLES:

- **Organiser** – Structures what the team is doing. Keeps track of team progress and achievements relative to goals and timelines.
- **Doer** – Accepts tasks, reliably completes work, and meets deadlines to ensure team success.
- **Challenger** – Pushes the team to explore all aspects of the situation, consider alternative assumptions, explanations and solutions. Debates, critiques and asks why.
- **Innovator** – Regularly generates new and creative ideas, strategies and approaches.
- **Team Builder** – Establishes norms, supports decisions, maintains a positive ambience. Calms members when stressed and motivates when discouraged.
- **Connector** – Boundary spanner who helps build bridges and connects the team with external stakeholders. Ensures good working relationships between the team and outsiders.

_Speaker notes (Slide 7):_ To discuss the factors influencing a person's role preferences, consider comparing each role with the five-factor personality model and with Schwartz's personal values circumplex (4 quadrants and 10 value categories), page 59.

**SLIDE 9 — Ch8 Q4 (2/2)**
Other team roles to consider:

- **Encourager** – These team members have a strong tendency to praise and support the ideas of other team members, thereby showing warmth and solidarity to the group.
- **Gatekeeper** – These team members have a strong tendency to encourage all team members to participate in the discussion.
- **Harmonizer** – These team members have a strong tendency to mediate intragroup conflicts and reduce tension.
- **Initiator** – These team members have a strong tendency to identify goals for the meeting, including ways to work on those goals.
- **Summarizer** – These team members have a strong tendency to keep track of what was said in the meeting (i.e., act as the team's memory).

**SLIDE 10 — Chapter 8 Question 5**
You have been given the task of forming an eight-member team of software engineers to secretly develop an important new component (feature) for the company's main software, which is used widely by financial advisors and their clients. Suppose that high team cohesion is critical for this team, whose members will be working on the team for up to six months. The company's software engineers are located in several cities in North America, as well as three locations in Europe and Asia. What actions would you take to create a software team that would quickly become highly cohesive, while still maintaining the company's standards of an inclusive workplace?

_Speaker notes (Slide 10):_ This team has a few important features to consider for developing cohesion. All members have similar occupational backgrounds (software engineering) but may differ in cultural backgrounds. This is a remote team in which members are located around the planet, so in-person gatherings would be costly. This team is a special group, completing a secret project. The team is also relatively small with eight members. Finally, the group will exist for only six months. We do not know if any members previously worked together. To answer this question, work through the six main influences on team cohesion and identify ways to apply those factors to improve cohesion.

**SLIDE 11 — Ch8 Q5 (1/2)**

- **Member similarity:** The team already has some advantage for cohesion because its members work in similar fields (software). However, there are cultural differences, which may slow down the team development process. This can be improved through activities that help team members understand each other, such as getting-to-know-you or my-user-manual events. Also, the team leader can organize social bonding sessions during which team members discover mutual life activities across cultures (e.g., hiking and soccer).
- **Team size:** The team is relatively small with eight members. This is a fixed feature of the team that can't be adjusted for the purpose of improving cohesion.
- **Member interaction:** This is likely the most important factor to speed up the team's cohesion. It can occur in a few ways. First, the team needs a routine of regular communication, and some of that communication should have high social presence, such as having video calls a few times each week. The team should also develop a routine of near-synchronous text/chat communication because synchronicity tends to improve collaboration and coordination. Second, the team should set aside time for social bonding events, such as 30-minute video gatherings to discuss nonwork ideas or amusements. Third, this remote team should kick-start its team development (including developing cohesion) by having an in-person gathering for a few days. These face-to-face sessions are perhaps the most important for accelerating team cohesion.

**SLIDE 12 — Ch8 Q5 (2/2)**

- **Somewhat difficult entry:** This team is secretly developing an important new component (feature) for the company's main software. As such, team members were likely specially selected for this project. The team leader should emphasize this point and remind team members that they have this special value and purpose to the organization.
- **Team success:** This team will have a series of completion stages as well as challenges that are conquered. Each of these occasions is a "success" along the journey toward final project completion. The team leader should ensure these events are celebrated, particularly that success is attributed to the overall team rather than to specific individuals.
- **External competition and challenges:** This company almost certainly has competitors, and the secret nature of the project highlights the competitive nature of the work. The team leader can strengthen cohesion by reminding members that other companies covet their work and are likely trying to develop a similar component. Project setbacks (difficult coding or related issues) can also be viewed as cohesion-building challenges overcome through the team's collective effort.

**SLIDE 13 — Chapter 8 Question 8**
The chief marketing officer of Sawgrass Widgets wants marketing and sales staff to identify new uses for its products. Which of the four team structures for creative decision making would you recommend? Describe and justify this process to Sawgrass' chief marketing officer.

**SLIDE 14 — Ch8 Q8 (answer)**
**Brainstorming**

- **Benefits:** Brainstorming is the most popular structure for encouraging creative ideas. It encourages divergent thinking while minimizing evaluation apprehension and other team dynamics problems. Brainstorming can bring benefits beyond the number of ideas produced. Participants interact and participate directly, increasing decision acceptance and team cohesiveness. Brainstorming rules tend to keep the team focused on the task. There is evidence that effective sessions provide valuable nonverbal communication that spreads enthusiasm; team members share feelings of optimism and excitement which may encourage a more creative climate. By involving clients in brainstorming sessions, these positive emotions may produce higher customer satisfaction than if people work alone.
- **Potential limitations:** Researchers warn that brainstorming does not completely remove evaluation apprehension; employees still know that others are silently evaluating the quality of their ideas. Production blocking and related time constraints can also prevent all ideas from being presented. Research also indicates that in some situations, individuals working alone produce more potential solutions to a problem than if they work together brainstorming.

**SLIDE 15 — Case Study Questions (section)**
McShane and Von Glinow. (2024) – Chapter 8 Case Study: 'The Shipping Industry Accounting Team'. Page 312.

**SLIDE 16 — Case Study Question 1**
What type of team was formed here? Was it necessary, in your opinion?

**SLIDE 17 — Case Study Question 1 (answer)**
The team created in this case was a remote team that consisted of members who operate across space, time, and organizational boundaries and are linked through information technologies to achieve organizational tasks. Whether it was necessary to form this team requires consideration of a few factors, some not explicit in the case. It seems reasonable for an organization to rely on experts in a specialization to put their heads together to form a strategy to improve business in their specialization. Without making too many assumptions, it is difficult to imagine how the firm could develop strategy without the collective involvement of these people. The key question isn't whether the team should have been formed — it's how the team should be structured. Perhaps a collocated team would be more effective; these people could have been brought together face-to-face for a few days to work through the main strategy issues.

**SLIDE 18 — Case Study Question 2**
Use the team effectiveness model and related information in this chapter to identify the strengths and weaknesses of this team's environment, design, and processes.

**SLIDE 19 — Case Study Question 2 (1/2)**

- **Team environment:** Several environmental concerns make it difficult for this team to operate effectively. They rely on email, which lacks the qualities (media richness) for communicating complex, ambiguous issues about corporate strategy. Given the lack of coordination, the organizational structure appears to support individual rather than team performance — reflected in statements that they could just as well work alone. Quality of leadership influences could be a factor.
- **Team tasks:** Forming a strategic plan to improve shipping accounting business is a potentially messy affair the members don't seem to engage in individually, let alone together very often. The task is novel, complex, and ambiguous — not ideal for remote teams, particularly ones far from the performing level of team development.
- **Team size/composition:** Team size seems fine; four people should be able to develop as a remote team relatively well. However, team composition is clearly a concern. While everyone has appropriate accounting expertise, there is a lack of motivation and competencies for remote team dynamics. The author and possibly others have little desire to work in a team environment, having worked alone for some time before the merger.

**SLIDE 20 — Case Study Question 2 (2/2)**
Reviewing this case using the **Five Cs model of team competencies**:

- **Cooperating** — Mixed evidence at best. Some cooperation with some members, but the author prefers to work alone. Limited willingness to be flexible.
- **Coordinating** — Limited competencies; little evidence anyone is sensitive to each other's circumstances or tries to make the team more efficient.
- **Communicating** — Team members lack skills in communicating through information technology. One member doesn't use email well. None has used conference calling much. More effective media not mentioned.
- **Comforting (psych support)** — No evidence anyone is supportive of others through the trials of teamwork; possibly some support between two people, implied rather than stated.
- **Conflict resolving** — Conflicts are very apparent, but no evidence anyone is working effectively to resolve them.
- **Team processes:** A glaring problem is that members have had almost no opportunity or resources to develop as a team. Effective remote teams typically meet face-to-face at some point (preferably early); this has not occurred here (except the author briefly met another member). The result: members don't understand each other, can't agree on anything beyond basic issues, and have very low cohesiveness.

**SLIDE 21 — Case Study Question 3**
Assuming that these four people must continue to work as a team, recommend ways to improve the team's effectiveness.

**SLIDE 22 — Case Study Question 3 (answer)**
[Callout box:] Several things can improve the effectiveness of this remote team. The wisdom of relying on a remote team for such an ambiguous and complex problem should be questioned. A multiday retreat for these four people and perhaps other senior executives might have been wiser and, in the long run, less costly.

- One recommendation is for the team members to meet face-to-face to at least begin the strategy plan and possibly participate in some team-building activity. This personal contact may improve team dynamics by moving the team development process further along and building some level of cohesiveness.
- Another recommendation is to develop the skills in these employees (and possibly others) regarding communicating through information technology as well as working in teams. They need to become better versed in a variety of communication channels so they can choose information technology that matches the complexity of this and other tasks.
