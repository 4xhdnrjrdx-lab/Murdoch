# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 8)

## Question 2

*Identify three reasons why a team of six students could potentially produce a better report than the average quality of their reports completed individually, including the motivational benefits.*

There are three main reasons I would point to. First, a team of six has a wider range of knowledge and skills than any single student, so it can locate relevant information more quickly and cover the topic more thoroughly. That larger pool of expertise is hard for one person to match.

Second, there are simply more hands on deck. One student cannot read and process as much material as six can. The work does need coordinating, sure, but the report is ultimately built on a much larger volume of conceptual resources.

The third reason is motivational. Members are often more motivated in teams than alone. Each student feels accountable to the others, fellow members monitor performance more closely than a supervisor would, and over time members develop a bond and want to fulfil the team's goals.

## Question 4

*Consider the six team roles (Exhibit 8.6). Which two best describe your role preference, and what factors influence a person's role preferences?*

In most team settings my two strongest role tendencies are the Organiser and the Doer. As an Organiser I tend to structure what the team is doing and keep track of progress against goals and deadlines. As a Doer I reliably take on tasks and complete them on time to help ensure the team succeeds.

A person's role preferences are influenced by their personality and values, along with past experience and skills. Using the Five-Factor (Big Five) model, my preference for the Organiser and Doer roles reflects high conscientiousness, meaning being organised and dependable. Using Schwartz's values model, these roles align with achievement and a degree of conservation (valuing order and reliability). By contrast, the Innovator role tends to suit people high in openness to experience and openness to change, while the Team Builder role suits people high in agreeableness. Prior experience in similar teams and a person's confidence in particular skills also shape which role they gravitate toward.

## Question 5

*How would you quickly build high cohesion in an eight-member, secret, remote software team spread across several countries, while maintaining an inclusive workplace?*

I would work through the main influences on team cohesion and apply each to this team.

The members already share a similar occupational background (software engineering), which helps cohesion, but they differ culturally. To work with that member similarity, I would run getting-to-know-you and 'my user manual' sessions plus social bonding activities that surface shared interests across cultures, which also supports inclusion. On team size, eight members is relatively small and is a fixed feature of this project, so that is already favourable for cohesion.

Member interaction is the most important lever for a remote team. I would establish a routine of frequent communication with high social presence (regular video calls), near-synchronous chat for coordination, short social video gatherings, and ideally an initial in-person meeting for a few days to kick-start development.

A few other levers matter too:

* **Somewhat difficult entry.** Members were specially selected for a confidential, important project. I would emphasise this special status and purpose so members feel they belong to an elite group.
* **Team success.** I would break the project into milestones, celebrate each completed stage, and attribute success to the whole team rather than to individuals.
* **External competition and challenges.** I would remind the team that competitors are likely working on a similar feature, and frame setbacks as challenges the team overcomes together, which strengthens cohesion.

## Question 8

*Which of the four team structures for creative decision making would you recommend to Sawgrass' chief marketing officer for identifying new uses for its products?*

I would recommend brainstorming. It is the most popular structure for encouraging creative ideas because it promotes divergent thinking while reducing evaluation apprehension and other team-dynamics problems. Beyond the number of ideas generated, members participate directly, which increases decision acceptance and cohesiveness, and the energy and enthusiasm in a good session can create a more creative climate. Involving clients can even raise customer satisfaction.

The CMO should also be aware of the limitations. Brainstorming does not fully remove evaluation apprehension, since members know others are silently judging their ideas, and production blocking and time limits can stop all ideas being heard. In some cases individuals working alone actually generate more ideas than a group. These problems can be managed by combining brainstorming with individual idea generation beforehand and enforcing the 'no criticism' rule.

# Activity 2: Case Study - The Shipping Industry Accounting Team (Chapter 8)

## Question 1 - What type of team was formed, and was it necessary?

This was a remote (virtual) team. The members operate across space, time and organisational boundaries and are linked through information technology to achieve organisational tasks. It seems reasonable to bring together experts in the same specialisation to form a strategy, and it is hard to see how the firm could develop the strategy without their collective involvement, so forming a team was justified. The real question is not whether to form the team but how to structure it. A collocated team brought together face-to-face for a few days may well have been more effective.

## Question 2 - Strengths and weaknesses of the team's environment, design and processes

The team environment is weak. The team relies on email, which lacks the media richness needed for complex, ambiguous strategy issues. The organisational structure rewards individual rather than team performance, and leadership support appears limited. The team task is a weak fit as well: forming a strategic plan is novel, complex and ambiguous, hardly ideal work for a remote team, especially one far from the 'performing' stage.

Team size and composition are more mixed. Four members is a workable size, but composition is a concern. They all have accounting expertise, yet several lack the motivation and competencies for remote teamwork, having worked alone before the merger.

Reviewing the team against the Five C's of team competencies, every area looks weak:

* **Cooperating.** Some cooperation exists, but the author prefers to work alone and shows limited flexibility.
* **Coordinating.** Little evidence members align their work or try to make the team efficient.
* **Communicating.** Members lack skill with the communication technologies (one barely uses email; little use of conference calls).
* **Comforting.** Little evidence of members supporting each other through the difficulties of teamwork.
* **Conflict resolving.** Conflict is clearly present but no one is resolving it effectively.

Team processes are the glaring problem. The members have had almost no opportunity to develop as a team, have never met face-to-face, do not understand each other, and have very low cohesiveness.

## Question 3 - Recommendations to improve effectiveness

My first recommendation is to meet face-to-face. Bring the four members together for a short retreat to begin the strategy plan and take part in team-building. Personal contact moves the team along the development process and builds cohesion.

The team also needs to develop teamwork and technology skills. I would train members in working in teams and in using a range of communication channels, so they can match the richness of the medium to the complexity of the task.

Finally, the organisation should question the structure. Given how ambiguous and complex the task is, it should reconsider relying on a purely remote team for it. A face-to-face working session for these (and perhaps other senior) people may be wiser and cheaper in the long run.
