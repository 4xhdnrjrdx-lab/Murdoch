Welcome back to BUS293, Organisational Theory and Behaviour. As mentioned in the
previous topic, working together is a fundamental aspect of the human experience and
workplace teams are increasingly perceived as necessary for success. This topic
introduces the team effectiveness model, team processes, team decision making, and
emerging team types.
Firstly, although similar, there are some differences between teams and groups. Teams
are a type of group, where the members have complementary skills and work together as
a unit to achieve a common purpose associated with organisational objectives for which
the collectively hold themselves accountable. Teams perceive themselves as a social
entity within an organisation. Informal groups are NOT formally designated as a part of
the organisation and they emerge unofficially. These groups include friendship and
interest groups, and they help people accomplish their work, satisfy their individual
needs, reduce stress, and therefore improve employee wellbeing. The main determining
factor that separate teams from just being a group, is the shared commitment of members
towards achieving organisational objectives.
Teams have both advantages and disadvantages. Under the right conditions, teams make
better decisions, better products and services, and a more motivated and engaged
workforce than individuals working alone. Teams can quickly share information and
coordinate tasks, which speeds up processes and increases breadth of knowledge,
consequently increasing quality of customer service. People are also more motivated
when working in teams, as there is a social identity to uphold, increased accountability to
team members who monitor performance more closely than a supervisor, and the
tendency to compare performance to other team members.
Despite these advantages, there are also disadvantages in the forms of process losses
encountered by teams. Coordination process losses include the time and energy taken to
develop and maintain the team. Teams need to develop understanding of their goals,
determine strategies, negotiate roles and rules of conduct, and resolve disagreements, to
ensure the team functions effectively. Process losses increase as team size increases and
are amplified when a new member joins the team, as time and energy must be used to
accommodate and integrate the new member. Motivational process loss is seen mostly in
social loafing. Social loafing, or freeloading, is the tendency of people to not work as hard
in a team as they would individually. Social loafing is reduced when the task is interesting,
when the team’s objective is important, and when team members value team
membership.
There are a few main types of teams in the workplace. Self-directed teams are small teams
who have been empowered to manage themselves and the work they do on a day-to-day
basis. They are very project-focussed, completing an entire piece of work requiring
several interdependent tasks and usually develop their own goals. They have substantial
autonomy, operate with fewer layers than traditional teams, and the position of
supervisor is no longer needed. Self-directed teams are increasing in popularity and
research indicates they have increased productivity and job satisfaction.
Multicultural teams are also increasing as organisations become increasingly global and
their members become more diverse. The heterogeneity of multicultural teams increases
their creativity and performance; however it is important to develop norms that allow
the team to reach consensus. Cultural differences in norms about power, status, and
authority, communicating, and decision making may cause tension and frustration
between team members, however these can be managed through adaptation, variation of
team structure, or managerial intervention in setting ground rules for interaction.
Virtual teams are a response to the increasing degree of separation between team
members created by new forms of organisational structure, advances in technologies, and
globalisation. They could be separated by different floors in the same building, different
base organisations, or even different countries. Members of virtual teams require strong
communication technology skills and strong self-leadership skills, as well as access to
multiple communication channels, increased task structure, and face-to-face meetings
early in the team development process.
A team is effective when it benefits the organisation through task performance, benefits
its members through satisfying members needs, and survives long enough to accomplish
its purpose. Ensuring the right conditions for team effectiveness is therefore important,
and the team effectiveness model represents the conditions that can influence team
effectiveness.
The organisational and team environments either support or inhibit the team’s ability to
function and achieve its objectives. Team members work together more effectively when
organisationally there is team-based rewards, supportive leadership, structural support,
suitable information systems, and physical space for teamwork.
The design of the team also contributes to the team’s effectiveness. Task characteristics
are the first element of team design and consist of task complexity, task structure, and
task interdependence. Complex tasks require the skills and knowledge of more than one
person, and thus teams are more suited as the work can be divided into more specialised
roles. It then follows that well structured work is easier to coordinate in teams, in contrast
to ambiguous tasks. However, teams can perform ambiguous tasks with well-structured
roles. Finally, task interdependence, the way in which members must share information,
materials, or expertise, in order to do their jobs, impacts team effectiveness, and the
higher the level of interdependence, the greater need for coordination and use of teams.
If the members do not directly interact with each other and are only indirectly affected
by the others’ actions, this is pooled, or low, interdependence. In this case, very little
attention is needed to the management of team dynamics.
If teams are designed in such a way that the outputs of one member become inputs for
another member, this is sequential, or medium, interdependence. The second member
depends on the first and cannot perform effectively if the first member is not performing
effectively. In this situation there is more potential for problems in team dynamics, which
creates a need for greater coordination and attention to team functioning.
The third interdependence, reciprocal, or high interdependence - occurs when each
member has outputs that are inputs to the other member. This increases the complexity
of team dynamics as there will be frequent and varied interactions between the members
on a daily basis. Any problems will spill over to other points of contact, and so teams need
to not only pay attention, but have skills in creating and maintaining effective team
dynamics and relationships.
The second element of team design is team size. The most effective number of team
members is debated, however it must be large enough to provide the necessary
competencies and resources to perform the work, yet small enough to allow efficient
coordination and meaningful involvement of each member. Smaller teams have less
process losses, are more engaged because they have a greater influence on the teams
norms and goals, experience more mutual trust and perceptions of support, and feel more
responsible for the team’s success and failure.
The final element of team design is team composition. The members capability to work
well in a team, or their team member competency, affect both task performance and team
maintenance. Team member competency is summarised in the five C’s: Cooperating,
Coordinating, Communicating, Comforting, and Conflict resolving. Team diversity is the
second aspect of team composition, with diverse teams making better decisions than
homogenous teams. Diverse teams also have a broader range of abilities and skills.
However, increased team diversity poses challenges as diverse teams tend to take longer
to bond and thus progress towards becoming a high-performance team. They also have
increased challenges in communication and motivation to coordinate. Team roles are the
third aspect of team composition, and are the set of behaviours a member is expected to
perform because they hold a certain position in a team and/or organisation. Roles can
either be formally assigned, or informally assumed. Finally, transactive memory is
knowing who knows what, or more simply put, allocating the right tasks and roles to the
most suited members.
Once the team is effectively designed, or formed, it must then then follow effective team
states and processes.
Norms are the informal rules and shared expectations that teams establish to regulate
the behaviours of their members. They can become deeply anchored, so the best way to
avoid norms that are detrimental to the team is to establish positive norms when the team
is first formed, for example by establishing a code of conduct which sets out basic
principles and rules governing team norms and behaviours.
Team cohesion is the degree to which members are attracted to and motived to remain a
part of the team. It strongly influences the extent to which members of a team conform to
team norms, and thus when team performance norms are positive, high cohesiveness has
positive effects for the organisation, but when team performance norms are negative,
high cohesiveness has detrimental effects on the organisation. The factors that improve
team cohesion include higher member similarity, smaller team size, increased member
interaction, difficulty of entry, team success, and external competition and challenges.
Team efficacy is the collective belief among team members of the team’s ability to
successfully complete the task, and as a general rule, the higher the team efficacy, the
higher the performance.
The final team state is team trust. Trust is both cognitive and emotional, based on
perceptions of competence and social identity. It is usually high in team formation, and
then events of poor individual performance, or other violations of trust, decrease the
level. A lower level of trust results in team members being less forgiving and less
cooperative, which undermines team and organisational effectiveness.
Team processes are both internal and external and are the interactions and activities,
generally classified as team work or task work behaviours, or team boundary spanning,
that the team engage in as they work towards their goal.
In the process of becoming an effective work unit, teams go through stages of team
development. Tuckman’s model of team development shows systematic stages that
teams go through to become a cohesive, effective team. Initially there is the forming stage,
where members discover team expectations and test he boundaries of behaviour. They
then progress to the storming stage where there is interpersonal conflict and negotiation
of roles, goals, and norms. As these roles, goals, and norms are established, the team
moves to the norming stage, where a sense of cohesion is established and there is a
consensus around objectives and behaviours. The team is then able to perform, as
members have learned to efficiently coordinate and resolve conflicts. For high-
performance teams, there is high level of cooperation, trust, commitment to group
objectives, and identification with the team. Once the team objective is complete, teams’
transition to the adjourning stage, and members shift their focus to relationships, away
from the task. Although Tuckman’s model depicts team development very well, it is not a
perfect representation of the process, and different teams will develop differently. There
may be inertia, revisiting of stages, or skipping of stages, as the team progresses through
their own individual experience.
Team development can also be accelerated through team building. Team building uses
formal activities to improve the development and functioning of a team. Experiential
activities include games that help to build cooperation, competition, and trust.
Challenging viewpoints approaches helps team members to break down unhelpful
viewpoints that are impeding team performance through using techniques like the
‘devil’s advocate’ process, or redefining roles within the team. Reaching consensus
approaches like brainstorming reduce members fear of criticism or failure and result in
enthusiasm, involvement, and a free flow of ideas. These team building techniques can be
conducted in a range of settings, from formal retreats, to regular meetings in a vein of
continual improvement, or outdoor experiences, however ongoing reflection of the teams
work experiences and a willingness to experiment is generally more effective.
By ensuring that the organisational and team environment, team design, team states, and
team processes are all optimised for team performance, teams are much more likely to
be effective.
Teams are expected to make decisions, and under certain conditions do better at this than
individuals alone. However, there are some constraints that reduce the effectiveness of
team decision making. Time constraints such as production blocking, where only one
team member can speak at a time, undermines idea generation. Evaluation apprehension,
where people are reluctant to mention ideas in case they will be judged or seen as silly,
also reduces idea generation. Pressure to conform causes team members to not speak out
if they disagree with an idea, which could lead to the team making a suboptimal decision.
Overconfidence can also reduce the quality of decision made as overconfident teams are
less vigilant when making decisions and are less likely to seek out outside information.
Finally, groupthink is when highly cohesive groups focus on consensus rather than
decision quality. It limits both idea generation and idea quality, and is usually present
when pressure to conform and overconfidence are also factors.
To improve effective decision making, teams can use the following techniques to reduce
the constraints. Brainstorming helps to increase idea generation and minimise evaluation
apprehension, however it is still impacted by production blocking and pressure to
conform.
Brainwriting, which is a version of brainstorming where team members write ideas on
cards and place them in the centre of the table, helps remove production blocking, and
arguably produces more and better quality ideas than brainstorming.
Electronic brainstorming, where brainwriting is done anonymously using technology,
again improves on the benefits of brainwriting, and minimises production blocking,
evaluation apprehension, and pressure to confirm, however it tends to be too structured
and technology bound. Finally, nominal group technique is another variation of
brainwriting conducted in three stages. Firstly, members individually and silently
document their ideas, then they collectively describe these to the group without criticism,
and then again silently and independently the members evaluate the ideas presented.
This technique again improves on the benefits of brainstorming, however production
blocking and evaluation apprehension do still occur to some extent. Despite the
limitations, using techniques for team decision making can improve the effectiveness of
the decisions made.
Team work is essential, as research has shown that teams consistently out-perform
individuals or random groups,
but as this topic has discussed, they are not easy to create or manage.
In order to ensure teams have positive norms, desired team behaviours need to be
actively shaped and reinforced.
