Regency Grand Hotel

The  Regency  Grand  Hotel  is  a  five-star  hotel  in  Bangkok,  Thailand.  The  hotel  was
established  15  years  ago  by  a  local  consortium  of  investors  and  has  been  run
throughout this time by a Thai general manager. The Regency is one of Bangkok's
most upmarket hotels, and its 700 employees enjoy the prestige of working there. The
hotel provides good employee benefits, above-market-rate salary and job security. In
addition, a generous year-end bonus amounting to four months' salary is awarded to
employees, regardless of the hotel's overall performance during the year.
Recently,  the  Regency  was  sold  to  a  large  American  hotel  chain  that  was  keen  to
expand its operations into Thailand. When the acquisition was announced, the general
manager decided to take early retirement. The American chain kept all of the Regency
employees,  although  a  few  were  transferred  to  other  positions.  John  Becker,  an
American with 10 years of management experience with the chain, was appointed as
the  new  general  manager  of  the  Regency.  Becker  was  selected  because  of  his
previous successes in integrating newly acquired hotels in the United States. In most
of the previous acquisitions, Becker had taken over operations with poor profitability
and low morale.
Becker was a strong believer in empowerment. He expected employees to go beyond
guidelines  and  standards  to  consider  guest  needs  on  an  individual  basis.  He
demanded  that  staff  were  guest  oriented  at  all  times  in  order  to  provide  excellent
customer  service.  From  his  U.S.  experience,  Becker  found  that  empowerment
increases  employee  motivation,  performance  and  job  satisfaction—all  of  which
contribute to a hotel's profitability and customer-service ratings. Soon after becoming
general  manager  at  the  Regency  Grand,  Becker  introduced  the  practice  of
empowerment so as to replicate the successes that he had achieved back home.
The Regency Grand Hotel had been very profitable since it opened. Employees had
always  worked  according  to  management's  instructions;  their  responsibility  was  to
ensure  that  these  instructions  were  carried  out  diligently  and  conscientiously.
Innovation  and  creativity  were  discouraged;  indeed,  employees  were  punished  for
mistakes  and  discouraged  from  trying  out  ideas  that  had  not  been  approved  by
management. As a result, they were afraid to be innovative and to take risks.
