# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapter 14)

## Question 2 - Reading a culture through its artefacts

An organisation's deeper values and shared assumptions sit mostly below the surface. So the practical way to read its culture is through its artefacts, the observable signs and symbols. There are four broad categories, and a good answer works through all of them with concrete examples.

* **Organisational stories and legends.** The stories people repeat about the founders, about past crises or about "the way we do things here" carry the values the organisation actually rewards. Think of the idolisation of Steve Jobs at Apple, framed as the visionary who fused art and technology. That story signals a culture that prizes design and bold innovation. Stories work as artefacts because they are concrete, told and retold, and they prescribe how members are expected to behave.
* **Rituals and ceremonies.** Rituals are the less formal, programmed routines: how visitors are greeted, how people communicate, how long lunch takes, even how fast staff move through the corridors. Ceremonies are the planned, more formal events staged for an audience, such as customer-service awards or employee-of-the-month celebrations. Both reveal what the organisation treats as important enough to formalise.
* **Language.** What people say, and how they say it, reflects shared assumptions. Whether front-line staff are called "customer service representatives" or "customer solutions specialists" signals how the organisation frames its relationship with customers and employees.
* **Physical structures and symbols.** The building and its layout speak to values. An open-plan office symbolises collaboration and a culture of open discussion. Integrating sustainable design principles, in the same way, signals a genuine commitment to sustainability in operations.

No single artefact is conclusive, and that is the key point. Artefacts are often subtle and ambiguous, so to read a culture accurately you have to examine many of them together and look for a consistent pattern. From that pattern you can then infer the underlying values and the deeper, taken-for-granted assumptions that really drive behaviour.

## Question 3 - "Organisations with strong cultures are more successful than those with weak cultures"

The claim is only partly true, and the qualifications matter more than the headline. Culture does three jobs. It is a form of social control, it is the social glue that bonds people together, and it is a sense-making system that helps employees understand the workplace. A strong culture amplifies all three, so on average firms with strong cultures do perform better than those with weak cultures. But three conditions decide whether that strength actually helps.

* **The cultural content must fit the environment.** A strong culture only improves performance when its values suit the organisation's situation. A strong problem-solving, customer-service culture works well in a customer-focused firm. A strong, fast-paced, competitive sales culture can backfire, though, if it makes customers feel their needs come second to sales targets.
* **It must not become cult-like.** A culture can be so strong that it drives out dissenting values and locks people into a single mental model. These "cult-like" cultures blind employees to new opportunities and emerging problems, which is dangerous in a changing environment.
* **It should be adaptive.** The healthiest strong cultures are adaptive ones. Here employees support ongoing change in the organisation and in their own roles, and subcultures and emerging values are tolerated, so the firm stays aligned with a shifting external environment.

So I partly agree. Strength helps, but only when the content is appropriate, the culture stays open rather than cult-like, and it is adaptive. A moderately strong, well-aligned, adaptive culture beats an intensely strong but rigid one.

## Question 6 - Merging organisational cultures

Culture clashes are one of the most common reasons mergers and acquisitions fail, because two sets of values and assumptions get forced together. The first step is to cut the risk before the deal by running a **bicultural audit**. This diagnoses the two organisations' cultures and assesses how compatible they are, so leaders know in advance where the friction points will be. Once the firms combine, there are four main strategies for merging the cultures, and the right one depends on the strength of each culture and the businesses involved.

* **Integration.** Both cultures are combined into a new, composite culture. This works best when both cultures are relatively weak, or when they already share overlapping values, so neither side has much to give up.
* **Assimilation.** The acquired company takes on the acquiring company's culture. This fits when the acquired firm has a relatively weak culture and the acquirer's culture is strong and successful, so employees are willing to adopt it.
* **Separation.** The merged firms stay as distinct entities with minimal exchange of culture or practice. This suits cases where the two operate successfully in different businesses that genuinely need different cultures.
* **Deculturation.** The acquirer imposes its culture on the acquired company. This rarely works and breeds resistance, but it can be necessary when the acquired firm's culture is dysfunctional and its people do not recognise the problem.

So leaders should audit compatibility first, then pick the strategy that matches how strong and how different the two cultures are. Integration or assimilation suit overlapping or one-sided strength, separation suits cases where the businesses must stay different, and deculturation should only ever be a last resort.

## Question 7 - Organisational culture and ethical conduct

Culture strongly shapes how ethically employees behave, because ethical behaviour is driven by ethical values and those values are embedded in the culture. The critical distinction here is between **espoused and enacted values**. Espoused values are the ones an organisation lists publicly. Enacted values are the ones actually revealed in executives' and employees' decisions, in how resources and attention are allocated, and in how stakeholders are treated, and it is these that define the real culture and shape conduct. The gap between the two is where ethical failures live. National Australia Bank espouses "doing the right thing", yet was singled out in the Banking Royal Commission for not doing so.

Culture can also protect ethics. Healthy subcultures, and especially countercultures that oppose the dominant view, can encourage constructive conflict and independent thinking. That stops employees blindly following the dominant logic, and it can raise ethical behaviour by surfacing concerns the mainstream culture would otherwise suppress. The flip side is that when organisations behave unethically, the failure usually traces back to the culture. Culture is hard to observe and hard to change, but leaders still shape ethical conduct by embedding ethical values into the artefacts, the reward and recognition systems, and the socialisation of new employees, so that the enacted values, not just the espoused ones, end up being ethical.

# Activity 2: Case Study - 'CommsOne Bank' (Chapter 14)

## Question 1 - The problems that occurred, in terms of mergers and organisational culture

What went wrong here was a culture clash between two strong but opposite cultures. CommsOne, a leader in business banking, is conservative and control-oriented. It values detailed analysis and cautious decision making, it concentrates decisions at the top for control and consistency, and it promotes almost entirely from within (every senior executive started there). TaurusBank, the firm it acquired for its strength in digital banking and investment funds, is the opposite. Taurus is aggressive and entrepreneurial, known for edgy marketing and innovative products, willing to hire outsiders for fresh ideas, and built on employee involvement and autonomy (its investment-fund division could launch new products without full executive sign-off). The relative ordering of values in the two firms is almost reversed.

The deeper problem is that CommsOne treated the deal as a purely financial transaction and ignored culture. Due diligence went into "poring over Taurus's financial status to ensure the purchase was truly accretive," but no bicultural audit tested whether the two cultures could actually work together. The chosen approach was effectively deculturation. The CEO announced that Taurus would be a "seamless extension of CommsOne" and that Taurus staff would "learn the value of detailed analysis and cautious decision making." That is imposing the acquirer's culture on a strong, opposite acquired culture, the strategy that rarely works. Communication made it worse. Employees of both firms were told only minutes before the public announcement, with no involvement, realistic preview or socialisation, so the psychological contract was breached and Taurus staff were left shocked.

The result was that CommsOne destroyed the very asset it had paid for. The whole rationale was Taurus's talent in digital banking and investment funds, yet the culture clash drove that talent out. 20 of Taurus's 60 fund managers left within the first year, the executive heading the investment-fund business left for a competitor and took six managers with him, and several other Taurus executives quit. CommsOne's slow, top-down decision making suppressed Taurus's main advantage too. Six months on, the executive team was still undecided on the digital-banking affinity alliances, and the new rule that every fund needed CommsOne executive approval removed the autonomy that had made Taurus fast and innovative. Two years later the CEO admitted the merger had barely added any business in funds or digital banking, much of the key talent had gone, and the combined firm had fallen behind more innovative competitors. The merger failed, but not financially. It failed culturally.

## Question 2 - Strategies to avoid these culture clashes in future mergers

The starting point is to run a bicultural audit before the deal, alongside the financial due diligence. Diagnose how compatible the two cultures are and where they will conflict, so the firm goes in with a plan rather than discovering the clash afterwards.

From there, the merger strategy has to fit the situation rather than default to domination. Taurus had a strong, valuable and very different culture, so deculturation was the wrong call. Separation would have been far better, keeping Taurus as a relatively distinct unit with its entrepreneurial culture intact, since its speed and innovation were exactly what CommsOne lacked and the two businesses genuinely need different cultures. Integration into a real composite is only an alternative where overlapping values can be found.

A few practical moves follow from that:

* **Protect and retain the acquired talent, which was the whole point of the deal.** Use retention incentives, and give the investment-fund and digital divisions the autonomy and quick decision rights to keep innovating, rather than forcing every decision through CommsOne's executive group.
* **Communicate early and honestly, and use socialisation rather than imposition.** Tell and involve employees well before the announcement, give realistic previews, and use mentors and two-way learning (CommsOne could learn entrepreneurialism from Taurus, not only impose caution). Align the artefacts and rewards with the desired blended culture.
* **Have leaders model respect for the acquired culture.** The CEO's "seamless extension" and "learn our cautious ways" framing signalled superiority and triggered the exodus. Leadership should instead visibly value what the acquired firm brings.

# Activity 3: Team Exercise - Organisational Culture Metaphors

For this one we described our institution, Murdoch University, by finishing each line and giving a reason. A metaphor like this is basically a quick way of reading the culture without listing it out formally.

* If our institution were an animal, it would be a **camel**, because it is built for this part of the world and just keeps moving steadily through the heat and the long trimesters. It adapts instead of panicking, and that steadiness is sort of how the place runs.
* If our institution were a food, it would be a **mezze platter**, because it is lots of small different dishes from everywhere put on one table. People come from so many countries that the mix is the first thing you notice, and it still works as one meal. That mix is really the subcultures sitting inside one shared culture.
* If our institution were a place, it would be an **airport**, because people are always arriving and leaving and everyone is headed somewhere different. It is busy and international, there is a shared space, but each person is on their own timeline.
* If our institution were a season, it would be **summer in Dubai**, because the pace is intense and never really cools down. Trimesters are short and packed, so it always feels like the hot part of the year with no break coming.
* If our institution were a TV show or movie, it would be a **fast-cut montage**, because a whole trimester goes by like it is squeezed into a few scenes. Loads of characters, a lot going on at once, and by the time you catch your breath the assessments are due.

**Discussion.** Put together, the culture comes across as diverse, fast and adaptive. The food and place ones point to strong subcultures held together by a shared international identity, and the animal and season ones point to a value of just getting on with things under pressure. It was also clear that people from the same place read its culture quite differently, which is pretty much what subcultures are about.
