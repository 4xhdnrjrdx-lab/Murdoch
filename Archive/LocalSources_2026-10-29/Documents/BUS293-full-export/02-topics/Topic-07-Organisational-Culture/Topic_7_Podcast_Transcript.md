Welcome back to BUS293, Organisational Theory and Behaviour. Organisational
culture defines what is important and unimportant in the organisation and
establishes the ‘right’ way of doing things through shared values and
assumptions. This topic looks at the layers of culture, the complexity of a
consistent organisational culture, and the link between organisational culture and
ethical behaviour.
Organisational culture is a situational factor and consists of the artefacts, values
and assumptions shared within an organisation.
Shared assumptions are non-conscious, taken-for-granted perceptions or beliefs
that have worked so well in the past that they are considered the correct way to
think and act towards problems and opportunities. These subconscious mental
models are difficult for employees themselves to identify.
As discussed in previous topics, values are stable, evaluative beliefs that guide
our preferences for outcomes or courses of action in a variety of situations. They
are discussed as shared values in the context of organisational culture, and these
are the values that people within the organisation have in common, and rate as
highly important. Organisations usually list their core values on their websites,
however these espoused values can differ from their enacted, or actual, values.
For example, National Australia Bank has a listed value of “doing the right thing”,
however they were singled out in the Banking Royal commission for NOT doing
the right thing. The enacted values, not the espoused values, are what defines an
organisations culture, and these can be seen in actions and decisions of executives
and employees, resource and attention allocation, and treatment of stakeholders.
Organisations differ in their cultural content; that is, the relative ordering of
values. Some may prioritise team work over individual achievement, or stability
over innovation. Westpac bank lists integrity first in their list of values, before
customer service, whereas National Australia Bank lists “doing the right thing”
last, and ‘passion for customers’ first. There are several classifications and
categorisations of organisational culture, but these oversimplify the wide variety
of cultures and group large numbers of distinct, specific values.
In addition, an organisations culture has some level of variability, as individual
employees’ values differ, and clusters of employees may have different value
hierarchies, for example investment specialists and customer service specialists.
These clusters are called subcultures and they can either enhance the dominant
culture, differ but not conflict with the dominant culture, or they can embrace
values or assumptions that directly oppose the dominant culture. Subcultures that
are in opposition with the dominant culture are referred to as countercultures, and
although they can create conflict and dissension among employees, they may
encourage constructive conflict and creative thinking, which could lead to
increased ethical behaviour by preventing employees from blindly following the
dominant thinking. Subcultures are also useful for the creation of emerging values
that keep the organisation aligned with the external environment, as a dynamic
environment also necessitates the updating of organisational values.
Organisational culture can be deciphered through various signs and symbols on
an organisation, these are known as cultural artefacts. Four broad categories of
artefacts include organisational stories and legends, rituals and ceremonies,
language, and physical structures and symbols.
Organisational stories and legends are seen in the idolisation of Steve jobs at
apple, as a visionary of combining art and technology. Ceremonies are planned
activities for the benefit of an audience, for example customer service awards,
or employee of the month celebrations. Rituals are less formal, but more
programmed routines, such as how visitors are greeted, how people
communicate, how much time is taken for lunch, even how fast people walk
through the corridors. Language looks at what people say, and how they say it,
and can be seen at a basic level when staff are referred to as either customer
service representatives, or customer solutions specialist. Finally, the physical
structures and symbols reflect the organisations culture. An open-plan office
symbolises collaboration and a culture of discussion. The integration of
sustainable design principles may reflect the organisations commitment to
sustainability in its operations. To accurately gain an understanding of an
organisation’s culture, many artefacts must be examined, because they are often
subtle and ambiguous.
Organisational culture has three main functions: as a form of social control, as
the ‘social glue’ that bonds people together, and as a way to help employees make
sense of the workplace. Companies with strong cultures generally perform better
than those with weak cultures, but only when the cultural content is appropriate
for the organisation’s environment. So, a strong culture of problem-solving and
customer service may work well for a customer-focussed organisation, however
a strong competitive, fast-paced culture may not work well if it makes customers
feel like their needs are secondary to sales targets. Also, the culture should not be
so strong that it drives out different values, which may form emerging values for
the future. These cult-like cultures lock people into singular mental models which
can blind them to new opportunities and problems. Organisations should have
adaptive cultures so that employees support ongoing change in the organisation
and their own roles.
However, modifying an organisation’s culture is incredibly difficult. So,
changing, maintaining, or strengthening organisational culture, begins with the
founders and leaders, and is often begun by replacing the leaders. Companies can
also remove or introduce new artefacts, align rewards and recognition practices
to be consistent with the desired cultural values, support workforce stability and
communication, and/or use the processes of attraction, selection, attrition, and
socialisation to sculpt the workforce into alignment with the revised culture.
Organisational socialisation is the process by which individuals learn the values,
expected behaviours and social knowledge necessary to assume their roles in the
organisation. It is a process of both learning and adjustment of social identity,
team norms, and new behaviours. During the socialisation process, new and
potential employees develop and test their psychological contract – their personal
beliefs about the terms and conditions of the reciprocal agreement between
themselves and their employer.
Employees typically pass through three socialisation stages: pre-employment,
where they learn about the organisation and their job and form employment
relationship expectations, encounter, where as a new employee they test their
expectations against perceived realities,
And finally role management, where they strengthen relationships with co-
workers and supervisors, practice new role behaviours, and resolve conflicts. To
manage the socialisation process, organisations should provide realistic job
previews (RJPs) and recognise the value of socialisation agents in the process.
Realistic job previews give job applicants a realistic balance of positive and
negative information about the job and work context. Socialisation agents such
as mentors or buddies provide information and social support during the
socialisation process. If socialisation occurs effectively, the new employees are
likely to have higher motivation, loyalty and satisfaction, and lower stress and
turnover.
Organisational culture clashes are common in mergers and acquisitions. This
problem can be minimised by performing a bicultural audit to diagnose the
compatibility of the organisational cultures. The four main strategies for merging
different corporate cultures are integration, assimilation, separation, and
deculturation. Integration is best when both organisations cultures are relatively
weak, or if there are overlapping values, and consists of merging both cultures
into a new composite culture. Assimilation works best when the acquired firm
has a relatively weak culture, and the acquiring firm’s culture is strong and
successful. This consists of the acquired company accepting the acquiring
company’s culture. Separation works best when the companies require different
cultures as they are operating successfully in different businesses and consists of
the merging companies staying as distinct entities with minimal exchange of
culture or organisational practice. Finally, deculturation rarely works, but may be
necessary if an acquired firms’ culture is dysfunctional but they are not aware of
the problems. This consists of the acquiring company imposing its culture on the
acquired company.
An organisations culture influences the ethical conduct of its employees, as
ethical behaviour is driven by ethical values, which are then embedded in the
organisations culture. Similarly, unethical practices of an organisation are more
often than not blamed on the culture, so despite the difficulty in identifying
cultural artefacts, and changing organisational culture, it is a significant
organisational aspect when it comes to organisational behaviour. Thank you.
