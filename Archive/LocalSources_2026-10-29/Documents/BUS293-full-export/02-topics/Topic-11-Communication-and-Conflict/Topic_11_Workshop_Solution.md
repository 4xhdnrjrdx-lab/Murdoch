# Activity 1: Critical Thinking Questions (Chapters 9 and 11)

## Chapter 9, Question 5 - When lean media can work as well as rich media

We pick a communication medium based on two things: social acceptance and media richness. Media richness is about a channel's data-carrying capacity. A rich channel sends several cues at once, gives fast feedback, lets you tailor the message, and handles complex or emotional content. That is why face to face sits at the top. The standard advice is that ambiguous, non-routine situations need rich media, while routine and clear messages are fine on lean channels like email or a quick text.

What makes the question interesting is that the "always use rich media for the hard stuff" rule has real exceptions. Lean media can do almost as well as rich media under a few conditions. When people are very comfortable and skilled with the technology, they read a lot into a short message that an outsider would miss. When users can multi-communicate, running several conversations at once, lean channels actually suit the way they work. And sometimes the social distractions of a rich channel (the small talk and the pressure of being watched) get in the way of processing the information, so a leaner channel is cleaner. So lean media are not automatically worse. They match rich media when the users are proficient, the task is mostly about transferring information rather than emotion, and the richer channel would only add noise.

## Chapter 9, Question 6 - Why some channels are more persuasive than others

Persuasion is about shifting someone's beliefs or behaviour, and the channel makes a real difference to whether that happens. Spoken communication, especially face to face, is usually the most persuasive, and the chapter gives three reasons. First, speech comes packaged with non-verbal communication, so tone and expression back up the words and add emotional weight. Second, it allows high quality, immediate feedback, so the sender can read doubt on the other person's face and adjust the argument on the spot. Third, it has high social presence. The receiver feels you are right there with them, which makes the message harder to brush off.

Written channels like email lose most of this. They strip out the non-verbal cues, the feedback is slow, and the social presence is low, so the same argument lands with less force. None of that makes writing useless. It is the better choice when you need a permanent record or when the receiver wants time to think it over. But if the real goal is to move someone's opinion, a conversation will normally beat a memo. A good approach is often to persuade in person and then confirm the details in writing.

## Chapter 11, Question 2 - Is conflict always harmful?

The old view was that conflict is purely destructive and managers should stamp it out. You can see why people thought that, because conflict can waste time and resources, cut down information sharing, push up stress and turnover, weaken team cohesion, and feed organisational politics. Those costs are real, and they mostly come from conflict that has turned personal.

A more accurate position is that some conflict is healthy and too little of it is its own problem. A team with no conflict tends to stop questioning itself, which is how weak decisions slip through. Constructive conflict gets people debating the issue and re-examining their assumptions, it keeps the organisation responsive to a changing environment, and when the conflict is with an outside rival it can actually pull a team closer together. So conflict is not something to remove. It is something to keep at the right level and the right type. Task conflict that stays on the issue while respecting the people involved is the useful kind. Relationship conflict, where people see each other rather than the problem as the enemy, is what does the damage.

## Chapter 11, Question 6 - The five conflict handling styles and choosing between them

In interpersonal conflict people tend to fall into one of five styles, set by how assertive they are about their own interests and how cooperative they are about the other side's. Problem solving is high on both: a genuine search for a win-win where resources are treated as expandable rather than fixed. Forcing is assertive but not cooperative, a win-lose push to get your way using hard tactics. Yielding is the reverse, simply giving in. Avoiding is low on both, sidestepping the issue or the person. Compromising sits in the middle, trading matched concessions to meet halfway.

There is no single best style. It depends on the situation, which is the usual answer in this subject. Problem solving gives the strongest long term outcome and protects the relationship, but it needs trust, time and a willingness to share information, so it is not always realistic. Forcing can be necessary when a fast decision matters or an important principle is at stake, though it strains relationships. Avoiding makes sense for a trivial issue or to let things cool down, but it settles nothing if the problem is real. Most of us lean on one or two preferred styles that fit our personality, values and past experience. Getting better at conflict really means being able to switch style to match the situation instead of always defaulting to the same one.

## Chapter 11, Question 9 - Negotiation: claiming and creating value

Negotiation happens when two parties with divergent goals try to settle by reworking the terms of how they depend on each other. The bargaining zone model is a handy way to picture it. Each side has an initial offer, a target, and a resistance point (the walk-away line), and the two sides move toward each other from opposite directions. If the resistance points overlap there is room for a deal. If they do not, there is not.

Every negotiation pulls in two directions at once. Claiming value is getting the best outcome for yourself. You do it by preparing and setting clear goals, knowing your BATNA (the best alternative if the talks fall through, which sets how hard you can push), managing time to your advantage, and being careful with first offers and concessions. Creating value is the cooperative side, finding ways to make the deal bigger so both sides come out ahead. That comes from sharing some information, using offers to work out what the other side actually cares about, and building a working relationship. Skilled negotiators do both. Pure claiming may win you a slice of a small fixed pie, but adding the creating side often uncovers trades that leave everyone better off.

# Activity 2: Case Study - "Difficult Connections" (Chapter 9)

## Question 1 - Symptoms that something has gone wrong

The obvious symptom is the breakdown in communication between Sophia Reddy in Chicago and Thomas Kerbakker, the Nashville project leader. Her two text messages got no reply at all, and when she finally reached him by email his response was a few cold, terse comments ("good," "yes it will," "noted") with no capitalisation. The relationship symptoms follow from that. Sophia moves from excited to irritated, worried, bewildered and dejected, reads Thomas as angry or uninterested, and starts regretting the assignment before the project has even begun. The practical task has stalled too, since she still has none of the team list, schedule or background information she asked for.

## Question 2 - Causes, using communication concepts

The root cause is the wrong channel and a social-acceptance mismatch. Sophia chose texting because it is her default medium, but Thomas, near retirement, does not use text messages, and the whole Nashville office knows it. The channel was never accepted by the receiver, so her message effectively never landed. Choosing a medium depends on both media richness and social acceptance, and she failed the social-acceptance test.

There is also a codebook problem. The two do not share a codebook. Her style is casual and abbreviated ("Gr8," "Pls," "b/g," "wk," an emoji); his is formal and status-conscious. They decode the same signals differently, which is why his lowercase, one-line replies read to her as rude and angry when to him they may just be terse and efficient. She even reads the lack of capitalisation as disrespect.

Layered on top is the absence of common mental models, plus negative perceptions acting as noise. They have never met, so Sophia fills the gaps with assumptions, deciding they have "divergent ways of thinking" and that the whole Nashville office must operate differently. The silence was caused by a channel problem, but she reads it through perceptual bias as a personal message of disinterest.

Finally, the lean media themselves make it worse, and so does the generational difference. Text and email carry no tone and reduce politeness, so an emotionally important first contact lands badly. The age-related difference in channel preference (her cohort uses the phone for everything except calls, while his prefers phone or in person) sharpens the mismatch.

## Question 3 - What Sophia should do now

Since she cannot leave the project, she needs to repair the connection and switch to a channel that actually works for Thomas. The first step is to pick up the phone and call him, or arrange a short video or in-person introduction. Building a new working relationship and sorting out ambiguous coordination both need rich media, and the phone is the channel he accepts. She should empathise with the receiver and meet him on his terms rather than imposing her own. Use the call to introduce herself warmly and ask for the information directly, then follow up in writing if he likes a record. She should also reframe her attributions. His terseness is far more likely to be his style than anger, and with a month before the project starts there is no real delay. When they speak she should listen actively, postpone judgement and check understanding.

## Question 4 - What Alicamber LLC should do in the long run

* Set firm-wide communication norms so cross-office teams know which channels to use for what, rather than each person defaulting to their own.
* Maintain a team directory that lists each member's preferred contact method, so a colleague knows up front that Thomas is best reached by phone or in person.
* Run a proper kickoff (a call or short visit) for cross-office projects to build shared mental models and codebooks before remote work begins.
* Provide awareness or training on cross-generational and cross-office communication differences, and encourage choosing the channel by richness and the receiver's acceptance.
* Build a more consistent firm culture and regular interaction between offices so the "Nashville does things differently from Chicago" friction is reduced over time.

# Activity 3: Team Exercise - Kumquat Conflict Role Play

This exercise drops two parties into what looks like a straight fight over a scarce resource, a limited supply of kumquats that both sides insist they need. Taken at face value it turns into a win-lose negotiation. Each side digs in, guards its information, and tries to grab as much of the fixed supply as possible. That usually pushes people toward forcing, or at best a compromise where the resource is split down the middle and nobody walks away fully satisfied.

The lesson lands once the parties stop arguing about how many kumquats they want and start talking about why they need them. When that information comes out, it often turns out the underlying interests are not in direct competition at all, and a solution exists that can satisfy both sides. That is exactly the difference between claiming value and creating value from the chapter. A problem solving style, high on both assertiveness and cooperativeness, treats the resource as expandable and goes looking for the win-win, while the instinct to compete assumes a fixed pie. For our team the takeaway was pretty simple. The conflict felt real, but the assumption that one side had to lose was not, and asking questions and sharing information beat hard bargaining every time.
