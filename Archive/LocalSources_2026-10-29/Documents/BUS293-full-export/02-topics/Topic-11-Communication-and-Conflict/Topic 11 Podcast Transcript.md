Welcome back to BUS293, Organisational Theory and Behaviour. This topic looks at the
process of communication, conflict situations, and the use of negotiation in organisations.
Communication refers to the process by which information is transmitted and understood
between two or more people. It is important for: coordinating work activities, organisational
learning, decision making, changing behaviour, and employee wellbeing. The communication
process begins with the sender forming, encoding, and transmitting the intended message to a
receiver, through a communication channel. The receiver then receives and decodes the
message, and can form, encode, and transmit feedback to the sender. The encoded feedback is
received and decoded by the original sender.
Communication is effective when the sender’s thoughts are transmitted to and understood by
the intended receiver. However, the transmission and understanding are heavily impacted by
the encoding and decoding process. To improve this process, and therefore communication
effectiveness, it is helpful for both the sender and receiver to be proficient with the
communication channel, for both to have similar codebooks (for example, interpreting silence
as either agreement or disagreement) and common mental models of the communication
context, and for the sender to be experienced at sending that message.
The two main types of communication channels are verbal, which includes spoken
and written communication, and non-verbal, which is any communication that does not use
words.
Non-verbal communication includes facial gestures, voice intonation, physical distance and
even silence. Unlike verbal communication, non-verbal communication is less rule-bound and
is mostly automatic and non-conscious. Non-verbal communication influences the meaning of
verbal symbols and is an important part of emotional labour.
There is increasing use of internet-based verbal communication in organisations with email the
preferred medium for coordinating work. Email tends to increase communication volume,
alters communication flow, and can reduce some selective attention biases. Although
convenient, email is relatively poor at communicating emotions; it tends to reduce politeness
and respect; is inefficient for communicating in ambiguous, complex and novel situations; and
it contributes to information overload. Social media is an emerging channel in organisational
communication, more interactive and dynamic than email, it can take many forms and serve
several functions.
The most appropriate communication medium depends on its social acceptance and media
richness. Social acceptance refers to how well the communication medium is approved and
supported by the organisation, teams and individuals. This includes organisation and team
norms, individual preferences for specific communication channels, and the symbolic meaning
of a channel. The second contingency, media richness, refers to the channels data-carrying
capacity. A channel has high richness when it conveys multiple cues, enables timely feedback,
permits customised messages, and allows complex symbols. Non-routine and ambiguous
situations require rich media. However, technology-based lean media might be almost as
effective as rich media for transferring information, particularly when users can multi-
communicate and have high proficiency with that technology, and when the social distractions
of high media richness channels reduce the efficient processing of information through those
channels. These contingencies also should be considered when selecting the best channels for
persuasion, and spoken communication is usually more persuasive because it is accompanied
by non-verbal communication, has high-quality immediate feedback, and has high social
presence.
Noise, or, the psychological, social, and structural barriers that distort and obscure the intended
message, can hinder communication. Perceptions, Filtering, Jargon or Ambiguous Language,
and Information Overload are some of the barriers that make up noise. These barriers are
amplified in cross-cultural settings due to language, accent, speech styles, power distance, and
temporal context. Non-verbal communication has similar barriers, with differences in the
interpretation of deliberate gestures, silence, and conversational overlaps. There are also some
communication differences between men and women, such as the tendency for men to exert
status and engage in report talk (giving advice and asserting power) in conversations, whereas
women use more rapport talk (relationship building) and are more sensitive than are men to
non-verbal cues.
To get a message across, the sender must learn to empathise with the receiver, repeat the
message, avoid noise, focus on the problem, not the person, choose an appropriate time for the
conversation, and be descriptive rather than evaluative. The receiver must also listen, which
includes sensing, evaluating, and responding. Active listeners support these processes by
postponing evaluation, avoiding interruptions, maintaining interest, empathising, organising
information, showing interest and clarifying the message.
Organisations do a lot of different things to try and encourage communication, such as
designing the workspace with open office arrangements, and/or clustering people in teams,
providing internet-based organisational communication with intranets, wikis, and collaborative
document creation, and allowing direct communication with top management through town
hall meetings and/or management by walking around. However, in any organisation, and
especially in times of uncertainty or change, employees rely on the grapevine, which is an
unstructured informal network based on social relationships, not organisational charts or job
descriptions. Originally, research found that the grapevine transmits information rapidly in all
directions, follows a cluster chain pattern, is more active in homogeneous groups, transmits
some degree of truth, and is more persuasive, these findings may be altered now due to the
internet playing an increasing role. Grapevine Benefits are that it fills in missing information
from formal sources, strengthens corporate culture, relieves anxiety, and signals that problems
exist; however, distortions might escalate anxiety, and it may create a perceived lack of concern
for employees when company information is slower than grapevine
Conflict is the process in which one party perceives that its interests are being opposed or
negatively affected by another party. It was commonly thought that conflict is undesirable and
counterproductive, and it can cause negative consequences such as wasting resources and
taking up otherwise productive time, less information sharing, higher stress, dissatisfaction,
and turnover, lower team cohesion, and increased organisational politics. However, too little
conflict can also not be a good thing, as conflict can lead to better decision making through
energising debate and re-examining assumptions, organisational responsiveness to a changing
environment and, if the conflict is with an external source, increased team cohesion.
There are two types of conflict. Task conflict refers to constructive conflict where people focus
the discussion around the issue, or, the ‘task’, while showing respect for people and other points
of view. In contrast, relationship conflict exists when people view each other, rather than the
issue, as the source of conflict. Emotional intelligence and stability, a cohesive team, and
supportive team norms help to focus on the task during conflict.
Conflict can be separated into three main groups, Intrapersonal – such as emotional dissonance,
role conflicts, or value conflicts, Interpersonal – if there are incompatible goals and values,
communication problems, or negative workplace behaviour, and Interorganisational – if there
is differentiation, interdependence, scarce resources, or ambiguous rules. If interpersonal
conflict is occurring, people will generally take one of five conflict handling styles, depending
on their assertiveness, and cooperativeness. With low cooperativeness and low assertiveness,
‘avoiding’ occurs, where the person tries to smooth over or evade conflict altogether by either
avoiding the person or the topic. With low cooperativeness and high assertiveness, ‘forcing’
occurs, where a person tries to ‘win’ the conflict at the others expense. People who use this
style tend to have a win-lose orientation and believe that for them to get what they want the
other person must lose something. Forcing relies on hard influence tactics like assertiveness.
With high cooperativeness and low assertiveness, a yielding style is adopted, where a person
simply gives in to the other side or cooperates with no attention to their own wishes. With high
cooperativeness and high assertiveness, a problem-solving style is adopted, where there is a
search for a solution that benefits both parties. This has a win-win orientation, where resources
are seen as expandable, rather than fixed. Finally, if there is moderate cooperativeness and
moderate assertiveness, this leads to a compromising style, where someone looks for losses
that are offset by other gains, and matching concessions to find a middle ground. There are
limitations and benefits for each style, and, as is usually the case in organisational behaviour,
the most optimal style will depend on the situation, however people gravitate towards one or
two preferred conflict handling styles that match their personality, personal and cultural values,
and past experience.
In addition to addressing how a conflict is approached, managers can also look at addressing
structural causes of conflict by: Emphasising superordinate goals by stressing common
objectives rather than conflicting sub-goals, and reducing goal incompatibility and
differentiation; Reducing differentiation by removing sources of different values and beliefs,
for example moving employees around to different jobs; Improving
communication/understanding by (after reducing differentiation) using communication and
contact hypothesis to help employees understand and appreciate each other’s views; Reducing
interdependence by creating buffers, using integrators, and combining jobs; Increasing
resources by duplicating resources, and Clarifying rules and procedures by clarifying resource
distribution and changing interdependence.
Negotiation is a process where two or more conflicting parties attempt to resolve their divergent
goals by redefining the terms of their interdependence. Parties need to consider desired
outcomes, tactics, deal design, and the scope of the negotiation. The bargaining zone model
identifies three strategic positions for each party (initial, target, resistance) and shows how each
party moves along a continuum in opposite directions with an area of potential overlap. All
negotiations consist of two divergent objectives: claiming value (getting the best personal
outcome) and creating value (discovering ways to achieve mutually satisfactory outcomes for
both parties). Skilled negotiators claim more value by using the strategies of preparing and
setting goals, knowing their alternatives to the negotiation (BATNA), managing time to their
advantage, and managing first offers and concessions. Skilled negotiators create more value by
using the strategies of gathering information, using offers and concessions to discover issue
priorities, and building relationships with the other party. The situation is also an important
consideration in negotiations, including location, physical setting and audience characteristics.
Effective communication and conflict are vital to various aspects of managing organisations
and people. Good communicators select the right type and channel and reduce barriers through
awareness and active listening. Conflict management is supported through structural
approaches and effective negotiators need to engage in both collaboration and competition for
success. Thank you.
