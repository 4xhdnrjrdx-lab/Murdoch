I'm really excited to be here today to introduce you to a skill that can change the way you see yourself, it can change the way you see the world around you and everyone around you, and it can absolutely change the way you go about reaching your goals in life or pursuing your goals.

Emotional intelligence is an absolutely critical skill because we have more than **400 emotional experiences every single day**. Whether you're aware of these experiences or not, they can really drive the bus if you let them do that. And **emotional intelligence is your ability to understand your emotions and to respond to them effectively to produce the behavior that you want.**

## The story of Phineas Gage

To help you understand how emotional intelligence operates in the brain, I need to introduce you to a guy named Phineas Gage. Phineas was a guy who was building the Burlington Railroad in Vermont in the 1840s. He was not just any railroad foreman — he was considered to be the most capable foreman in the business. Phineas was very intelligent; he knew how to cut through the rocky terrain to lay the tracks on time. But he also had that extra something that made people want to work for him: he was polite, he was calm and cool under pressure, and he was great with people.

On this one day in particular, Phineas was working with an item called a tamping iron, made out of really dense metal like a crowbar. The way they'd use the tamping iron is they'd cut a hole in the rock, pour blasting powder in there, then pour sand on top, and tamp down the sand to give a very precise blast. On this day, Phineas was waiting for his assistant to pour sand in the hole, and his men overloaded a train car behind him with boulders — you can imagine the noise — and it distracted Phineas. It also distracted his assistant, who didn't pour sand in the hole. So when Phineas rammed the rod into the hole, it ignited the gunpowder and launched the rod through his head like a rocket. It landed 100 feet behind him in the bushes, and it entered right below his left eye.

The area of the brain that it removed is your **left orbital frontal cortex**. This wouldn't be much of a story, except Phineas survived the accident. He was sitting up under his own power within five minutes; he logged his exit from the job site in the logbook and told the town doctor what had happened. It took about six months for his physical wounds to heal. Once they healed, Phineas was ready to go back to work. He was still every bit as intelligent as before; he still had interest in building the railroad; his personality was the same. But there was something very key missing — it was **how he responded to his emotions**. Every emotion that Phineas had exploded unfettered into action. He was angry, impulsive, unreliable; suddenly he was showing up late.

## How it works in the brain

Everything you experience must enter at the base of your brain. Then it travels across your brain, through the **limbic system** — this is where emotions are generated. In Phineas' case, this part of the brain was still intact; his brain was producing emotions like normal, but he lost his **rational brain** — the area where he was able to read and respond to these emotions. **Emotional intelligence combines the two:** it's your ability to understand the emotions you're having. We're hardwired to be emotional creatures, so these emotions happen in a split second, before we're able to think rationally about them. What we do in response to our emotions is really what emotional intelligence is about.

## Why this is news to us

We live in a world that doesn't necessarily teach us what's good for us. (Bradberry shows vintage ads — a satirical "start Cola earlier" baby ad, plus real ads claiming sugar curbs cravings, doctors endorsing smoking, and "DDT is good for me.") We're taught the three Rs in school, but we're not taught how to lead, or all the capacities we possess to make the most of life — and emotional intelligence is one of these.

## EI vs IQ vs personality

Emotional intelligence is absolutely **distinct from your IQ.** You can be high in EI and high in IQ; low in one and not the other; low in both. They don't occur together in any meaningful way, despite the stereotype that people with high IQs have low EQs.

People also confuse EI with **personality**. Personality is a stable set of preferences and tendencies through which you approach the world; it's fixed at an early age, just like your IQ. Neurologists call this part of the brain "crystallized" — fixed, not responsive to change.

**Emotional intelligence, on the other hand, is highly plastic.** The pathway between your emotional and rational brains is flexible and responsive to change. When you work on your EI, your neurons actually branch out to each other and increase the flow of information between the rational and emotional brains. This is the essence of emotional intelligence.

## The skills: personal and social competence

There are four emotional intelligence skills, but statistically it really comes down to **personal competence** and **social competence**. Personal competence is about you — awareness of your own emotions and how you manage them. Social competence is about you with other people — how you read and respond to others and what you do with that. (Statistically it's really just two, because once you become aware of your emotions, it's hard not to do something productive with them.)

## Emotions operate beneath awareness

A study at a university in the Netherlands took people with cortical lesions — their eyes and optic nerves worked perfectly, but the brain couldn't process what the eyes saw, so they were effectively blind. Researchers flashed images of people expressing strong emotions. With sighted people, **"mirror neurons"** mirror others' emotional states, so you can't help having a small emotional reaction (a big smile makes you smile; anger makes you furrow your brow). What freaked researchers out was that these blind people had the *same* reactions as sighted people. Analyzing the MRIs, they found an alternate pathway: signals traveled down the optic nerve and branched out to the limbic system. The people didn't know what they were seeing — "It was a hunch." How many of you have walked into a room full of people and just felt the mood even though you can't put your finger on it? These are the kind of emotional signals driving your brain, and people who are highly emotionally intelligent are very tuned into them.

## Why EI matters for success

Emotional intelligence is a **foundational skill** — emotions are the primary driver of behavior. When you master your emotions, it trickles into everything you do: how you manage stress, give presentations, work in a team, make decisions.

- EI explains about **60% of how you do** at work.
- About **90% of top performers** are high in EQ.
- You can have a high EQ and be a bottom performer (other factors are at play), but EI is a very direct route to producing the behavior you want.

Research published in the *Harvard Business Review* looked at EI scores by job title. Scores rise from individual contributor → supervisor → and **peak at middle management.** Above middle management, it's "a ski slope all the way down to CEOs," who have the lowest EI scores in the workplace. The trick: within each job category, EI predicts performance — but organizations promote people above management based increasingly on tenure, short-term financial gains, and industry knowledge. The well-rounded people — those who achieve that *and* have high EI — really flourish.

## Three "silver bullets" to increase your EQ

**1. Get your stress under control.** Intermittent, mild stress (kept under control) entices the brain to produce cells responsible for improved memory. But when stress becomes severe or prolonged (per UC Berkeley research), it causes degeneration in the areas of the brain responsible for self-control — a vicious cycle. A UC Davis study taught people to **cultivate an attitude of gratitude**: every time they felt stress, they stopped and thought about something they were grateful for. This lowered the stress hormone **cortisol by 23%.** Taking walks, reading, exercising — breaks are key.

**2. Clean up your sleep hygiene.** When you're awake, toxic proteins build up in your neurons (a byproduct of normal activity). During sufficient-quality sleep, neurons clean themselves up and remove these proteins; without it, they hinder your capacity to think, make you groggy, and diminish self-control. The number-one offender is taking things that "help you sleep" (Benadryl, three glasses of wine, Ambien, Nyquil, melatonin) — these impair the body's ability to go through the necessary sleep stages. The co-offender is **blue light** in the evening: blue wavelength light halts melatonin production and tells your body it's morning. After dinner, bathing yourself in blue light from monitors or tablets confuses your brain and harms sleep quality. So: no blue light, don't take sleep aids, wake up at the same time each morning.

**3. Get your caffeine intake under control.** Caffeine has a long half-life — about six hours. A triple espresso in the afternoon is still half metabolically active by 9–10 PM, making it hard to get quality sleep. Recommendation: don't drink caffeine after noon, and know how it affects your body.

These three silver bullets will help you get where you need to be on the road to improving your emotional intelligence.

## Quick reference

- **Definition:** EI = your ability to understand your emotions and respond to them effectively to produce the behavior you want.
- **Brain basis:** emotions generated in the limbic system; EI = the plastic pathway linking the emotional and rational brains (Phineas Gage lost the rational-response part).
- **Distinct from IQ and personality** (both "crystallized"/fixed); EI is flexible and can be developed.
- **Two competence areas:** personal (self-awareness + self-management) and social (awareness of + responding to others).
- **Stats:** ~400 emotional experiences/day; EI explains ~60% of work performance; 90% of top performers are high in EQ; EI peaks at middle management.
- **Three silver bullets:** control stress (gratitude lowers cortisol ~23%), clean up sleep hygiene (no sleep aids, no evening blue light), control caffeine (none after noon).
