## Video transcript — Online Testing: Step-by-Step

*(5-minute video.)*

Hello and welcome. In this video, we will take a closer look at the procedure of conducting online testing. Overall, the testing process can be divided into four steps: **define success, identify bottlenecks, construct hypothesis, and then design and run experiments and analyze the results.** Let's look at each step using the example of one popular brand, **REI** (Recreational Equipment Inc.) — a large online and offline retailer of outdoor recreation goods (sporting goods, camping gear, travel equipment, clothing) that also provides services like bike tune-ups and ski boot fitting.

### Step 1 — Define success

The first step is to have a clear idea of what we're trying to achieve from the change we're thinking of making. For most e-commerce companies, a key business objective is to increase sales or profit margins — but **very often this is not the goal you set for your experiment.** The reason is that visit-to-sale conversion is a complex process affected by numerous factors; to tease out the effect of a particular design element (e.g., the color of an Add-to-Cart button), you'd need a very large sample size that may not be feasible. Instead, it's more common to focus on an **intermediary step** in the consumer's interaction with the site that nonetheless has a direct impact on the eventual sale (e.g., test whether the color of the Add-to-Cart button affects the decision to place the product in the cart).

For REI: past research found that having more product reviews increased the chances of a product being viewed and considered for purchase, yet very few buyers leave feedback after purchase. So the company wants to increase the rate of buyers leaving a review. Here, **success is defined as leaving feedback given the prior purchase of the product.**

### Step 2 — Identify bottlenecks

Review the process of soliciting and collecting buyer feedback and identify the potential bottleneck. The flow: send the customer an email inviting them to visit the site and leave feedback → buyer clicks the email link → lands on the review part of the landing page → option to proceed to the feedback form. Benchmark data suggests the email click-through rate and the feedback-form submission (given the buyer reaches that step) are performing well. However, the number of buyers who move **from the landing page to the feedback form** is lower than expected — so this transition is identified as the area for improvement.

### Step 3 — Construct hypothesis

Analyze the transition from landing page to feedback form to understand the subpar performance. Discussing with site designers and observing customers, we get the idea that the issue is **poor separation** between the links on the landing page — one taking the buyer to the Q&A part of the product page, the other to the feedback form. Hypothesis: increasing separation by moving the **Questions & Answers link farther down the page** will make the reviews link more prominent and lead to more clicks.

### Step 4 — Design, run, and analyze

With the metric established, the bottleneck identified, and the hypothesis constructed, move to designing and running the experiment and analyzing results. Since this is a simple setup of two versions of the landing page (original vs proposed with increased separation), we employ a standard **A/B testing** procedure: split the traffic coming from the invitation email into the two versions, wait for sufficient statistics, calculate the observed performance metrics, and test for a **statistically significant difference** between the two versions. If REI finds the new version performs better, they discard the original and adopt the new one (generating more reviews). Otherwise, they go back to the drawing board, reassess the reasons for subpar performance, and propose a different hypothesis to test.
