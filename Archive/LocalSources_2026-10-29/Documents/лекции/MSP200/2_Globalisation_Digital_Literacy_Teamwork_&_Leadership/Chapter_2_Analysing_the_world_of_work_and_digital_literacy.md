## What the report is about (Executive summary, paraphrased)

Australia's economic future depends not just on _how much_ is produced but on _how well_ people, employers and the skills system interact in a changing labour market. **Productivity** and **participation** are distinct but interwoven forces; the report's central argument is that **better matching** of workers' skills to jobs is the key strategic lever to lift both — improving recruitment, reducing turnover, supporting inclusion, and building a future-ready workforce. JSA's role is to provide data, analysis and engagement to enable this alignment. The report is structured around five themes: inclusive participation; barriers in today's labour market; the future labour market; improved matching and the skills system; and activating informed dialogue.

### The 5 recommendations

1. Align tertiary education **attainment minimum targets** (VET and higher education) with medium- and long-term labour market demand.
2. Governments should **commit to digital and AI capability uplift** across the skills system as a priority, targeting the whole population.
3. Governments should **embed contemporary data, digital and AI skills** in the design and delivery of higher education and VET qualifications, with mechanisms to respond to fast-evolving needs.
4. System actors should work towards a **national credit transfer system**.
5. System actors, governments and stakeholders should work with JSA to **accelerate the National Skills Taxonomy (NST)**.

## Chapter 1 — Productivity, participation and skills

- **Productivity** = how efficiently we create goods/services; **participation** = share of people 15+ working or looking for work. The two interact in both directions and are complex/multidirectional.
- **Productivity has stalled:** Australia's productivity growth over the past 10 years was less than a quarter of its 60-year average (Productivity Commission, 2025). A COVID "labour productivity bubble" reversed; labour productivity returned to its Dec 2019 level by mid-2023. The slowdown is international across advanced economies.
- **OECD position:** Australia ranks **17 of 37** OECD countries on labour productivity (GDP per hour worked) — just above the OECD average (Figure 1).
- **Drivers of productivity:** resource-allocation flexibility, competition, and especially **innovation**. Skills/human capital are central — matching workers' skills to job requirements is key. "Working smarter, not harder."
- **Participation:** Australia ranks in the **top 10** of OECD countries and above the US, Germany, Japan, UK (Figure 2). Six decades of rising participation driven largely by **women and mature-aged workers**. Benefits include economic output plus social gains (lower mortality, better physical/mental health, wellbeing). The Government's _Working Future_ White Paper targets "sustained and inclusive full employment."
- **Matching:** Job-matching efficiency has improved but varies by region/sector; persistent mismatches signal structural issues. JSA notes occupations with **less diverse workforces are more likely to face skills shortages**.

## Chapter 2 — Inclusive participation

### First Nations people

- Longstanding education/employment gaps persist; tied to _Closing the Gap_ (mixed progress; 4 targets worsening). Outcome 8 (economic participation): employment rose 4.7 pp since 2016 to **55.7%** (target 62% by 2031, on track).
- ~160,000 First Nations people aged 25–64 provided unpaid care in 2021; employment among carers rose (61.1% of those caring for children; 52.7% caring for someone with disability/health/age).
- More concentrated in Community and Personal Service roles, less in Professional roles; more likely in gender-segregated and shortage occupations and VET Skill Level 3 roles.
- **Gender segregation (Table 3):** only 10% of First Nations workers in gender-balanced roles (vs 21% total workforce); 26% in almost-completely male/female dominated roles (vs 21%).
- **Pay gaps:** First Nations women face the **highest gender pay gap in Australia — 35.3%** point-in-time (vs 25.7% national); 10-year accumulated gap 38.1% (40.5% vs non-CALD males). First Nations men: 19.3% gap. Both gaps have narrowed over time (First Nations female gap fell from 44.8% in 2010–11 to 35.3% in 2022–23).
- Drivers: deficit-based assumptions, systemic racism in education, lack of culturally safe workplaces. Solutions must be co-designed.

### People with disability (ABS SDAC 2022)

- Participation rate (15–64): **60.5%** in 2022 (up from 53.4% in 2018) but well below **84.9%** for those without disability.
- Unemployment **7.5%** (vs 3.1%) — more than double; rises with severity (6.4% mild → 10.7% profound). Participation falls with severity (64.8% mild → 37.2% profound).
- Over 40% of working people with disability are part-time (vs 29.7%); underemployment 7.2% (vs 4.9%).
- Under-represented in higher-skill roles (Professionals, Managers), over-represented in Labourers/Sales Workers; over-represented in Health Care & Social Assistance and Public Administration & Safety industries.

### Women's workforce participation

- 2006–2021: female employment grew 40% (vs 27% male). Women aged 25–39 with Certificate III+ rose from 48% to 72%; bachelor's+ from 28% to 46%. Yet participation still below men's.
- Economic prize: gender equality in the workforce could be worth up to **$128 billion annually** (Deloitte, 2024).
- **Gender segregation in education:** of 5.1m VET students (2023), women are only **17% of STEM enrolments** (12% in Engineering & Related Technologies) but 56% of non-STEM.
- **Gender pay gaps:** males outearn females in **98% of 688 occupations** analysed (6-digit ANZSCO). Typical gap 16.8%; 100+ occupations >25%; ~30 occupations >35%. 10-year accumulated gap 30.7% (vs 25.7% point-in-time, 2022–23). "Motherhood penalty" worst for women aged 25–39. Both horizontal and vertical segregation drive gaps.
- JSA's **Gender Economic Equality Study** (3 papers) introduced the **Gender Segregation Intensity Scale (GSIS)**. Fair Work Commission reviewing 5 priority awards for gender undervaluation (e.g., Children's Services; Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services).

### Youth and mature-age employment

- **Youth (15–24):** unemployment >9% (more than double national); ~half of Accommodation & Food Services workers are 15–24; youth employment concentrated in Retail (20%) and Accommodation & Food (20%).
- **Mid-career (25–54):** dominate Health Care & Social Assistance (16.8%), Professional/Scientific/Technical (10.3%), Construction (9.4%); ~80% of Finance & Insurance workers are this age. ~1/3 are Professionals.
- **Older (55+):** concentrated in Health Care & Social Assistance (16.3%), Education (9.9%); **over 40% of the agricultural workforce is 55+**. Important for knowledge transfer.
- **Employer survey on young applicants (REOS Youth module, 2024, 2,500+ employers):** 82% who received young applications hired at least one. Top reasons for success: **right attitude, relevant experience, good communication**. Top reasons for failure: lack of experience, then lack of qualifications/skills, then poor availability. Only 18% accessed any subsidy (most common: Australian Apprenticeships Incentive System, 11%).

### Foundation skills

- _National Foundation Skills Strategy 2025–2035_ defines two categories: (1) language, literacy, numeracy and digital skills; (2) **employability skills** (initiative/innovation, planning/organising, problem solving, teamwork).
- Foundation/employability skill gaps contribute to occupation shortages ("suitability gap"). OECD PIAAC (2023) analysis: adult skill differences accounted for a **quarter** of the productivity gap between the OECD average and top performers. Australia's most recent adult foundation-skills data is from PIAAC 2011–12 (rejoining PIAAC).
- **Key cross-cutting finding:** occupations with **more inclusive workforces are less likely to be in shortage**, especially when paired with well-matched skills.

## Chapter 3 — Barriers to productivity and participation today

### Occupation shortages — 4 driver types (Table 4, based on Richardson 2007 typology)

- **Long training gap** — few qualified applicants + long pathway (Cert III+).
- **Short training gap** — few applicants, qualification below Cert III.
- **Suitability gap** — enough qualified applicants but not seen as suitable (lack of employability skills/experience; sometimes unrealistic employer expectations, e.g., requiring 5 years' experience).
- **Retention gap** — below-average retention (low pay, poor conditions/culture, lack of services).
- (Plus **Uncertain/unknown**.)

Top-10 largest-employing unit groups per driver (Table 5) span health, education, construction, transport. Examples: long training gap → Secondary/Primary School Teachers, Civil Engineering Professionals; short training gap → Bus/Coach Drivers, Truck Drivers, Child Carers; suitability gap → Electricians, Construction Managers, various Engineers; retention gap → Aged & Disabled Carers, Carpenters, Chefs, Hairdressers; uncertain → Registered Nurses, GPs, Solicitors, Surgeons.

- **Registered Nurses** illustrate a shift: supply pipeline is growing but retention/replacement need is rising, so the driver moved from "training gap" (2024) to "uncertain" (2025).

### Persistent shortages (Table 6)

- **139 occupations** in shortage 2021–2025. Of these: **Technicians & Trades Workers 51%** (71), **Professionals 37%** (52), Community & Personal Service Workers 5%, Machinery Operators & Drivers 5%, Managers 1%.
- Industries with above-average shortages (Construction, Mining, Health Care & Social Assistance) tend to have weak productivity growth — poor matching may compound pressures.

### In focus — Veterinarians

- Demand rising (~6.9m pet-owning households; ~28.7m pets). Vacancies surged: 190 online ads in Sept 2024 vs avg 18/month in the prior decade; permanent vet postings up >300% (2014–2023) on one job board; time-to-fill tripled; half of roles open to graduates. Employment grew 32.1% over 5 years to Nov 2024 (~3x national average); qualification holders up 61.3% (2011–2021 census). Shortage persists due to burnout, emotionally demanding work, financial euthanasia, relatively low pay, regional pressures.

### Spotlight — Total New Vacancies (TNV)

- New experimental "flow" series (late 2024) estimating _total_ new vacancies each quarter (incl. non-advertised roles), combining online ad data (IVI) with employer surveys — complements ABS Job Vacancy Survey "stock" measure.

### Employer retention (REOS, 2023–24, 3,300 responses)

- ~**24%** of employers replace at least one staff member every ~6 months (medium-to-high); 41% every 6 months or more.
- **Reasons:** employees leaving for other jobs (42%); nature of the job/physically demanding/repetitive (27%); dissatisfaction with conditions/salary (15%).
- **Business impact:** recruitment/training cost and lost time (51%), then negative effects on existing staff, then decreased productivity/revenue.
- **Retention strategies:** ~1/3 offer higher wages/incentives; flexible/secure work (15%); upskilling (13%); over 1/3 use no specific strategy. Health Care & Social Assistance employers most proactive.

### Regional Australia (Jobs and Skills Roadmap for Regional Australia, Phase 1)

- ~1/3 of Australians live regionally; regions hold ~2/3 of employment in agriculture, mining, tourism, renewables.
- **Five guiding principles:** addressing barriers; developing skills; quality jobs; meeting demand; efficient matching of supply and demand.
- Ageing: working-age share (15–64) projected to fall from 65% to 61% over 2023–2063; 65+ to rise from 17% to 23%. As at March 2025, 65+ share was 19% (major cities) vs 28% (regional).
- Barriers: financial, digital connectivity, childcare, transport, housing, low foundation skills, health/disability. ~1 in 6 regional young people not in employment/education/training. **Almost 1 in 10 essential-service jobs vacant** in regional Australia (teachers, carers, nurses, doctors, pharmacists, emergency services). School non-attendance higher than major cities.

## Chapter 4 — The future labour market

### Structural change (10 years to May 2025)

- Biggest growth: **Health Care & Social Assistance (+886,700**, to >2.3m), Professional/Scientific/Technical (+359,700), Education & Training (+337,800), Construction (+307,900).
- Declines: Manufacturing (−29,300), Information Media & Telecommunications (−25,200), Wholesale Trade (−24,400), Agriculture/Forestry/Fishing (−9,000).
- **Service industries** ≈ **80% of employment**, ~90% of growth over the decade (+27% vs +14% for producing industries). Health Care & Social Assistance now 16% of total employment (up from 13%); Manufacturing fell 8%→6%.
- **By occupation:** Professionals grew most (+1,253,700, to >3.9m); then Community & Personal Service Workers (+525,500), Managers (+344,400), Technicians & Trades (+271,400). Top unit groups: Aged & Disabled Carers (+225,900), Registered Nurses (+107,600).
- **By skill level:** Skill Level 1 (bachelor+) up 41.2% (now 35% of employment, from 31%); Skill Levels 2–4 (VET) up 21.5%; Skill Level 5 (lowest) up just 4.2% (now 14%, from 16%).

### Employment projections to 2035 (VU Employment Forecasting Model, CGE-based)

- **5 years (to May 2030):** +6.5% (~961,000) to 15.7m employed.
- **10 years (to May 2035):** +13.3% (~2.0m) to >16.6m employed.
- The 3 largest service industries contribute ~**49%** of projected growth. Professionals + Managers rise from 39.6% to 41.7% of employment.
- **By state (10 yr):** largest by persons — Victoria (+579,500 / 15.3%), NSW (+576,800 / 12.7%), Queensland (+401,700 / 13.3%), WA (+227,400 / 13.6%). Health Care & Social Assistance is the largest employer in 2035 in all states/territories except ACT.
- **By skill level:** >90% of growth in Skill Levels 1–4 (post-secondary); 54.3% in Skill Level 1 (bachelor+), 40.1% in VET levels 2–4. Skill Level 5 share falls 13.7%→12.7%.
- **Education/supply caveat:** model includes a skills supply constraint → risk of **over-supply of bachelor-qualified graduates** but **under-supply at Certificate III/IV** (already visible in trades shortages — Electricians, Carpenters, Painters, Plumbers).

### 4.3 Generative AI — Gen AI Capacity Study ("Our Gen AI Transition")

First **national, whole-of-labour-market** study of Gen AI's impact. Key findings:

- Australia at an **early stage** of AI adoption; earliest adopters in tech sector; **57% of ASX200 companies** mentioned AI investment in 2024 annual reports.
- Three mechanisms: **automation, augmentation, adaptation.** Exposure analysis: **31% of the workforce in highly _augmentable_ occupations** vs only **4% in highly _automatable_** ones → Gen AI is more likely to **augment than automate**.
- **Shadow use:** ~**27% of workers use Gen AI secretly at work** (Deloitte, 2024); highest among white-collar workers aged 27–42. Raises governance/data-security risks.
- **Productivity:** time savings + better output quality; saved time redeployed, shifted to higher-value tasks, or recouped as leisure. Adaptation patterns: **job hybridisation, job specialisation, emerging new occupations.**
- **Displacement:** large-scale displacement **not currently occurring** in Australia; three adoption scenarios modelled. Occupations projected to decline most by 2050: General Clerks, Receptionists, Accounting Clerks & Bookkeepers, Sales/Marketing/PR Professionals, Business & Systems Analysts and Programmers. Relative growth: Cleaners, Midwifery & Nursing Professionals, Construction & Mining Labourers, Hospitality Workers.
- **Uneven effects:** women face greater exposure (to both automation and augmentation); regional/remote areas less exposed. **Digital exclusion:** nearly **1 in 4 Australians** lack the connectivity/skills to use Gen AI, highest among First Nations people, people with disability, older adults, lower-education/income groups.
- **Rising demand** for digital literacy and higher-order human skills: **critical thinking, communication, adaptability.** Lifelong learning, VET/HE updates, and embedding AI skills in qualifications are essential.

## Chapter 5 — Improved matching and the skills system

- **VET outcomes (VNDA project):** 88% of VET graduates employed after training (vs 72% before); 48% of those previously on income support exited it within a year; 29% enrolled in further VET. Full-time employment rose ~15 pp for all graduates after training (biggest gains for **women and First Nations people, +16 pp**). Partial completers also gain, but less than full completers.
- **Apprenticeships/traineeships:** of the 100 largest occupations, **68 grew since 2019**; growth concentrated in priority trades and care. Decline since 2022 mostly in non-priority occupations. Female participation in trades still low (**11.5%**) but ~**4x more female Electrician apprentices in 2025 than 2015** (also growth for Carpenters, Motor Mechanics, Plumbers, Sheetmetal Workers).
- **Higher education:** new longitudinal administrative data asset being built (enrolment, completion, financial outcomes, workforce alignment).
- **VET↔HE transitions** increasingly common in both directions → need for a flexible, harmonised system.
- **Tertiary harmonisation** advancing (clearer pathways, credit recognition); **Australian Tertiary Education Commission (ATEC)** established.
- **National Skills Taxonomy (NST)** under development — shared, evidence-based language for skills to support qualification design, skills-first hiring, and labour market insight.
- **Skilled migration** complements domestic supply; JSA advises on the **Core Skills Occupation List (CSOL)**.

## Chapter 6 — Activating an informed dialogue

- JSA website: **7.7m views (+77%)**, 1.68m unique users (+88%), 200,000+ downloads (Jul 2024–Jun 2025).
- **Referrals from ChatGPT increased >518-fold** — Gen AI amplifying reach. LinkedIn impressions +23%, 1,000+ news mentions.
- National roadshow (1,000+ attendees) showcased tools: **Jobs and Skills Atlas, Occupation Shortage List (OSL), Nowcast of Employment by Region and Occupation (NERO).**
- International partners: OECD, World Bank, ILO, World Economic Forum.
- JSA work informed: Migration Strategy/CSOL, clean energy workforce planning, early childhood education reform, National Skills Agreement Outcomes Framework, tertiary harmonisation/ATEC, VET Workforce Blueprint.
