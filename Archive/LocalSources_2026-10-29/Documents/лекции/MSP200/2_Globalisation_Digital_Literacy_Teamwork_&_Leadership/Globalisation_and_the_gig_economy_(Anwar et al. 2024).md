## Core argument (abstract + intro, paraphrased)

The world of work is changing in many directions at once. Digitization lets workers in different parts of the world compete in a single global market over the internet without relocating, and COVID-19 pushed many employers toward hybrid/remote arrangements. Some commentators read this as "the end of globalization," others hope a fairer society will emerge. The paper rejects both rigid readings and instead analyses the **global gig economy** as a lens on these transformations.

Central thesis: the gig economy is the **latest manifestation of capitalism's drive to accumulate**, and that drive produces **uneven geographical development (UGD)**. The paper ends with strategies for building fairer work futures.

Two key contributions stated by the authors:

1. In today's globalization, although some work moves freely across borders, platforms' attempt to create a **planetary labour market is not about ignoring geography but exploiting it** — so the gig economy unfolds unevenly (building on Graham & Anwar 2019).
2. **Labour has the power to reshape** these geographies, despite capital's attempt to "fix" the labour question via platforms. Worker struggles are placed at the centre; the outcome of labour movements will determine whether fairer work futures emerge.

**Defining the digital gig economy:** a system of economic exchange facilitated by digital labour platforms (e.g., Upwork, Uber) (Lehdonvirta et al. 2019). Split into two categories:

- **Remote work** — digitally intensive, can be done anywhere with digital infrastructure (e.g., image tagging, transcription, SEO, software development). _This is the paper's main focus._
- **Geographically tethered work** — digitally mediated but must be done in a specific place (e.g., ride-hailing, food delivery, care work).
- CEO quote framing the shift: Upwork's Hayden Brown said remote work "has gone mainstream" and won't reverse (CNBC 2020).

## Conceptual framework: Platform capitalism & Uneven Geographical Development (UGD)

**Uneven Geographical Development (UGD)** — drawn from Harvey (2006b) and Smith (1984), traceable to Marx. Key logic:

- Capitalism is inherently crisis-prone; to survive, capital continuously **produces space** (geographical expansion, infrastructure) to overcome over-accumulation.
- Samir Amin (2010): geographical expansion of capitalism creates polarizing tendencies _between and within_ regions — wealth concentrated in one place, poverty in another; urbanization and environmental degradation together.
- UGD = the process by which capitalist social relations take spatial forms so that **some places benefit at the expense of others** (Smith 1984). It operates at local, regional, national and global scales, and results _from capital accumulation_ — not (as modernization/globalization doctrines claim) from failure to adopt the "right" development model.
- "**Spatial fix**" (Harvey 2001) = capital resolving its crises across space and time.

**Platform capitalism** (Srnicek 2016): platforms as dominant players reorganizing production. After each crisis (1970s/80s/90s; especially 2008), capitalism restructures with new technologies, organizational forms, modes of exploitation, job types and markets. The 2008 crisis is widely seen as the watershed for platforms as the newest accumulation tool.

**Types of platforms:**

- Advertising platforms (Google, Facebook).
- **Labour platforms** (Upwork, Uber, Freelancer, Fiverr) — digital tools matching labour supply and demand via apps, infrastructure and algorithms (Woodcock & Graham 2019). Business model built on **data extraction as raw material** → "data colonialism" (Couldry & Mejias 2019).
- Work mediated by labour platforms + independent contracting = the **gig economy**.

**Related terms/typologies the paper notes:** sharing economy (Sundarajan 2016); microwork (e.g., image tagging — Amazon Mechanical Turk) vs macrowork (e.g., online freelancing/virtual assistant — Upwork); crowdwork / cloudwork; ILO's online web-based vs location-based distinction (ILO 2021). The authors critique these for blurring the (non)digital nature and the _geographical rootedness_ of platform work — all platforms are web-based, but the work is still done in a real place (Harvey 1989).

**Why geography still matters:** Thomas Friedman (2005) claimed a "flat" world where geography becomes irrelevant. The authors argue the opposite — platforms **take advantage of geography**, not erase it. Only a handful of countries (e.g., India, Philippines, Bangladesh) supply most of the labour, so platform networks are **asymmetrical and spatially uneven**. Platforms are "new digital spaces of production" enabling planetary networks of on-demand work (Anwar & Graham 2022), echoing Barlow's (1996) "a world that is everywhere and nowhere."

**Historical grounding:** platforms sit within the longer globalization of work — decades of offshoring/outsourcing to tap cheaper materials and workers (Peck 2017). The 1960s+ relocation of industrial manufacturing from high-income (US, EU) to low-income regions (Latin America, East Asia) (Fröbel et al. 1980), captured in Massey's (1995) "spatial division of labour" thesis (space is socially constructed). Special Economic Zones (SEZs) as enclaves of cheap production — China and Mexico (manufacturing), India (services) — show accumulation embedded in distinct socio-political/ecological settings.

## Empirical section: Uneven developments in the gig economy

The empirical material comes from secondary sources (Online Labour Index/OLI, Online Labour Observatory/OLO, ILO surveys, think-tank and government reports) plus some primary data scraped by the authors from Upwork (registered workers and job listings).

### 1. Geographies of work (the spatial distribution of demand and supply)

**Core pattern:** a clear division between high- vs low-income countries — most **employers/demand** sit in high-income countries; most **workers/supply** reside in low- or middle-income regions (Online Labour Index; Kässi & Lehdonvirta 2018).

- Demand dominated by four countries: **US, UK, India, Canada** (US is the dominant slice).
- Full country set shown in the pie chart (largest → smallest): United States, United Kingdom, India, Canada, Australia, Germany, Pakistan, Singapore, France, Philippines, Nigeria, Ukraine, South Africa, Egypt, Brazil, Kenya, Mexico, Argentina, Serbia.
- India is a special case: evidence of **re-intermediated** work transacted from India that originates mainly from the US (Anwar & Graham 2022).

**FIGURE 2 — Global market share of employer country and work types** (Source: Kässi & Lehdonvirta 2018)

- Tracks the five largest English-language platforms (≥70% of market by traffic).
- Biggest demand: **US, UK, Canada — together >half of global demand**; the **US alone ≈ 38%** of global demand for remote work.
- These three high-digital-penetration countries are also key drivers of demand for outsourced IT services (Beerepoot et al. 2017) → the remote gig economy _replicates the uneven geographies of the business-outsourcing industry_.
- Employer countries listed in the chart (high → low): United States, United Kingdom, Canada, India, Australia, Germany, United Arab Emirates, Netherlands, Singapore, Pakistan, France, Israel, Saudi Arabia, Spain, Switzerland, Ukraine, Hong Kong, Italy, Ireland, Philippines.
- Six work-type categories (legend): Software development and technology; Creative and multimedia; Sales and marketing support; Writing and translation; Clerical and data entry; Professional services.

**Supply side:**

- ~**165 million** workers estimated registered across remote gig platforms (Kässi et al. 2021) — but read with caution: workers register on multiple platforms, enter/leave often, and **reintermediation** (registered workers sub-contracting to unregistered others) means likely several million more "hidden" workers.
- Just four countries — **India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Philippines** — supply **more than half** of the world's remote workers. India and the Philippines are also global services market leaders (large outsourced-services sectors), having honed customer-service and cognitive skills.

#### Types of tasks (FIGURE 3 — Global market share of supplier country and work types; Source: Kässi & Lehdonvirta 2018)

Geographic variation in _which_ tasks are done _where_ (a distinct division of labour):

- **Asia** — software development and technology dominant (India, Philippines prominent in IT services).
- **South America** — mostly creative and multimedia.
- **South-Eastern Europe** — creative/multimedia plus software dev; major suppliers Ukraine, Serbia, Russia.
- **Africa** — creative/multimedia common but big intra-regional variation: e.g., Kenya → writing and translation; neighbouring Tanzania → sales and marketing. Linked to less-developed IT/engineering education in many African countries.
- Within-country variation too: US workers spread across professions; Russian/Ukrainian workers concentrate in software dev; Indonesian workers mainly creative/multimedia.
- Supplier countries shown in chart (high → low): India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, United Kingdom, United States, Philippines, Russia, Ukraine, Egypt, Serbia, Indonesia, Kenya, Sri Lanka, Australia, Turkey.

- Takeaway: **less than 10%** of registered workers earn any income at any time. India, Pakistan, Ukraine, Philippines have higher success rates than Egypt, Brazil, Ghana. Scholars link low first-job success in some countries to **racial discrimination by clients** (Anwar & Graham 2022). Government programmes (e.g., Kenya's **Ajira**) push more workers onto platforms, raising labour supply and _further dampening_ each worker's chance of winning a contract.

#### Rural vs urban divide

- Gig work depends on internet, laptops, smartphones; urban areas have better connectivity (ITU 2019), so more remote workers cluster in cities, especially in low/middle-income countries (ILO 2021).
- Counter-case: in the US, rural areas supply _proportionally more_ remote workers than urban (Braesemann et al. 2022) — platforms may bridge the rural-urban divide in some places.
- Serbia example: all four major cities (Belgrade, Novi Sad, Niš, Kragujevac) ≈ 35% of population but host ~70% of freelancers; **Belgrade alone ≈ 44%** of the country's gig workers (Anđelković et al. 2020). Similar urban concentration expected in Cairo, Manila, Nairobi.

#### Gender dimensions

- Hoped to empower women, but research shows platforms are **highly gender-segregated** and reproduce gendered hierarchies.
- Globally **only 1 in 3 remote workers is a woman** (ILO 2021); men participate more than women in the US, Australia, EU, UK, and in low/middle-income countries.
- Causes: men's better access to digital tools (ITU 2019); women's double burden of productive + reproductive labour; gender prejudice about "what work women can/can't do."
- Occupational sorting: women overrepresented in lower-income tasks (writing, translation, tutoring, admin); men in higher-income IT and creative sectors. African study: clients favour women for article writing, men for web-design/software (Anwar & Graham 2022); platform features "trigger cognitive shortcuts" about gendered skills (Galperin 2021); algorithmic bias adds to it (Vyas 2021).
- **Gender pay gap:** women earn only **82%** of what men earn despite similar hours, education and experience (Adams-Prassl & Berg 2017).

### 2. Labour geographies (workers as active agents, not just a factor of production)

Core idea (Herod 1997, 2001): like capital, **workers actively produce economic space** and exert agency — they create their own "fixes," rework space, and reshape the landscape of capitalism through movements and organizing.

**Why organizing is hard in the gig economy:** sub-contracted, extended production networks fragment work across hundreds of locations; union membership has declined (US, UK, South Africa, India); platforms **atomize** workers across borders.

**But worker movements are growing:** **1,268 protests across 57 countries between 2017 and mid-2020** (~one per day) (Bessa et al. 2022). Most protests: **US, then China, India, UK, Argentina, Spain** — driven by precarious, vulnerable conditions. Trend toward **self-organization outside traditional trade unions** (critiquing "trade union fetishism," Atzeni 2021).

**Concrete examples of worker agency:**

- **Serbia (2021):** government announced a tax on remote workers' online income → thousands protested in Belgrade; a self-organized **Association of Internet Workers** (born from a Facebook group) won concessions (scrapped interest on past debts; no income tax for low earners).
- **North Macedonia (2014):** freelancers/self-employed protested heavy taxes → government scrapped social-security contributions and set a low 10% tax rate (precedent for Serbia).
- **Turker Nation:** discussion board by Amazon Mechanical Turk workers for socializing and collective organization.
- **FairTube / YouTube creators:** teamed with German metalworkers' union **IG Metall** to challenge platform governance; FairTube aims to unionize geographically dispersed platform workers.
- **Alphabet Workers Union (AWU):** union spanning direct employees, temps and contractors in Alphabet's global production networks.
- **Africa (Anwar & Graham 2020a):** everyday **resilience, reworking, resistance** — sharing computers/accounts (resilience); two monitors, multiple accounts, re-outsourcing (reworking); filtering jobs, withholding outputs, exposing bad clients (resistance).
- **Kenya:** Association of Online Professionals (Nairobi) guides new entrants.
- Workers build "webs of care" (Katz 2004) and solidarity networks via Skype, Facebook, WhatsApp across cities (LA, New Delhi, Karachi, Manila, Cairo, Nairobi, São Paulo).

## Conclusions & strategies for better work futures

**Big picture:** work used to be tethered to a place (farm, factory floor). Now it is increasingly digitally intensive and digitally mediated, so work — not the worker — moves across borders. This produces a globalization of work never seen before: the emergence of **planetary labour markets**, potentially capitalism's ultimate "fix" (labour power sold globally as a commodity; workers atomized and pitted against each other) (Graham & Anwar 2019).

**But geography is not irrelevant:** labour supply/demand is **socially produced and regulated** (Peck 1996), not mechanically set by markets. Platforms leverage uneven geographies for **labour arbitrage** and cross-border competition; in some cases they may level up differences (e.g., rural vs urban). Crucially, **labour is not passive** — it actively shapes platform capitalism's geographies.

**Strategy 1 — Transnational solidarity.** Workers need solidarity across sectors and borders (Munck 2019). Even if a globally distributed workforce can't mobilize locally, they can build networks beyond their locality based on shared precarity (Turker Nation, FairTube, Kenyan Association of Online Professionals as examples). Use digital connectivity to "stretch" social relations (Massey 1995) and shift from union-based, place-leveraging movements toward **self-organized** digital mobilization.

**Strategy 2 — Stronger regulation.** Regulation is hard because remote work is largely invisible — done from homes, hidden from public view ("**perceptible invisibility**," Gruszka & Böhm 2020). Platforms also **disembed from local jurisdictions** and use technological/legal manipulations (e.g., classifying workers as **independent contractors**) to avoid responsibility for welfare and rights (De Stefano & Aloisi 2018). The authors argue governments — especially in rich countries where platforms are based and demand originates — have a duty to stop malpractice (enforce living wages, labour standards, social insurance) and that regulation needs **multi-actor dialogue**. Note: in December 2022, the **EU's Committee on Employment and Social Affairs adopted draft measures** to improve gig workers' conditions (European Parliament 2022).

**Normative close:** work doesn't have to be fragmented with workers invisible and treated as commodities. The task for lawmakers, workers, civil-society and activists is to make these workers visible _and_ expose and hold accountable the actors who profit from opaque, exploitative work networks.

**Future research agenda:** Who participates and who doesn't? Who wins/loses? How are labour markets being transformed? More intersectional work on gender, race, class; comparative case studies across _different_ contexts (e.g., India vs US; South Africa vs UK; China vs Australia); empirical study of worker resistance strategies; and far more research from the **Global South**, which supplies most platform labour (existing research skews Global North).

## Key concepts glossary (for your assignment)

- **Gig economy** — work mediated by digital labour platforms, characterized by independent contracting.
- **Remote work vs geographically-tethered work** — location-independent digital work vs work that must be done in a place.
- **Planetary labour market** — platforms enabling worldwide buying/selling of labour power (Graham & Anwar 2019).
- **Uneven geographical development (UGD)** — capitalism producing spatial inequality; some places gain at others' expense (Harvey, Smith).
- **Spatial fix** — capital resolving crises across space/time (Harvey 2001).
- **Data colonialism** — platform business model built on extracting data as raw material (Couldry & Mejias 2019).
- **Reintermediation** — registered workers sub-contracting jobs to (often unregistered) others.
- **Perceptible invisibility** — remote workers and their work being unseen by clients/public/regulators (Gruszka & Böhm 2020).
- **Labour geographies / spatial fix of labour** — workers actively producing space and exerting agency (Herod).
