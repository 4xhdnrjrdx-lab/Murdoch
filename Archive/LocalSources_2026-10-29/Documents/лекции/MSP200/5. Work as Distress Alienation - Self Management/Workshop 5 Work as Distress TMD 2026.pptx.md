# Karl Marx & the Dialectic Method
The philosopher studied contradictions that exist within society that are useful for understanding social change.

Example? The contradiction at the heart of capitalism re. workers and ‘the capitalists’ who own the means of production.
Capitalism Expands  Exploitation Increases  Resistance  Social Change

Marx focused heavily on Human Potential and believed there was a real contradiction between our human potential and the way we must work in capitalist society.

# The human as a producer (Marx)

The defining characteristic of human beings according to Marx = we are producers
Our connection to our labour (our work) distinguishes us from other animals
It gives us meaning in our lives
For example, we can imagine what we want to build before we build it
We transform materials when we build things or do our work
We also transform ourselves and our needs in the process (for example, when we write an essay, we learn more deeply about something; when we build a house, we gain shelter and relative safety)

<!-- Slide number: 7 -->
# Alienation (Marx)
According to Marx, in capitalist society, we no longer see a relationship between the work we do and a fulfilling of purpose and meaning in our lives. Instead, we work in accordance with the purpose of the capitalist who hires us. Rather than work being an expression of human capabilities, and of understanding and uncovering our full potential, work is simply a means to an end: and that end is often making money.
His take home message is simple: because we are separated from our work and the results of our work, it no longer transforms us. Instead, we experience alienation.

# Marx (1850)

The fact that labor is external to the worker, i.e., it does not belong to his essential being; that in his work, therefore, he does not affirm himself but denies himself, does not feel content but unhappy, does not develop freely his physical and mental energy but mortifies his body and ruins his mind. The worker therefore only feels himself outside his work, and in his work feels outside himself. He is at home when he is not working, and when he is working he is not at home. His labor therefore is not voluntary, but coerced; it is forced labor. It is therefore not the satisfaction of a need; it is merely a means to satisfy needs external to it.

# “Always on” work model

![Picture 3](Picture3.jpg)

Tuncalp (2025) observes that email, cloud storage, VPNs, Zoom, and other ways of bringing teams together has meant that work can now occur wherever someone has a device and an Internet connection. However, this has blurred the line between work and life, enabling an “always on” work model.

Q: What are some of the advantages of this model?
Q: What are some of the disadvantages?

<!-- Slide number: 13 -->

# ‘Always on’ work model(Tuncalp, 2025)
Some advantages:
Flexibility in hours (ability to tailor your work hours to accommodate your personal commitments)
Flexibility in work arrangements (ability to work remotely, giving more control over your work environment)
Enables seamless global connectivity between team members (more efficient teamwork across different timezones)
Some disadvantages:
 The potential for mental and physical exhaustion, leading to burnout
 ‘Continuous’ work can disrupt work-life balance, intruding into personal time, which can lead to strained relationships, and impact work
 A work culture of being ‘always on’ makes it difficult to disconnect from work
Long working hours can impact health: “Obesity, insulin resistance, and reduced lipid tolerance have been linked to poor-quality sleep and short sleep duration” (Tuncalp, 2025)

# Self-efficacy
Describes people’s judgements of their own abilities to do something successfully in a given situation. (Waddington, 2023)

If there is belief in one’s own ability to perform a task – then there will be an activation of sufficient effort, and persistence in the face of challenges.
