Increased casualisation of the labour market brings opportunities and challenges. This can lead to precarious individual contractual arrangements and underemployment.

The availability of casualised work can expand and contract to bring significant workplace challenges. In this topic, we explore these impacts through the lens of financial literacy and your skills for creating financial wellbeing.

## Core message (paraphrased)

Insecure work isn't just a financial or fairness issue — it is a genuine **health and safety hazard**, and Australian regulation is only beginning to treat it as one. **Western Australia** is the first jurisdiction to formally recognise insecure work as a psychosocial hazard in its workplace code; the article argues every state and territory should follow, but that codes alone won't deliver change without stronger laws or unions.

## Scale of the problem (data)

- About **3 million** Australian workers lack job security.
- An estimated **2.4 million (20–25% of the workforce)** are **casual** workers, with **no paid leave** entitlements.
- Affected workers span many fields: labour-hire labourers, checkout operators, ride-share drivers, casual university lecturers.

## The WA Code of Practice (the news hook)

- In an Australian first, **WA's new Code of Practice on Psychosocial Hazards in the Workplace** formally recognises insecure work as a hazard.
- A **psychosocial hazard** = any social/organisational factor in work design or management with potential to cause psychological or physical harm.
- Background: better recognition of such hazards was recommended by the **2018 Boland Review** of Australia's "model" WHS laws; federal/state/territory ministers agreed in **May 2021**. Most jurisdictions have since updated regulations/codes, but **WA is the only one so far to include insecure work.**

## What psychosocial hazards are

- The **World Health Organization lists ten** workplace psychosocial hazards — including high uncertainty in job content, lack of control, lack of support. Prolonged exposure raises the risk of serious mental or physical injury.
- The author's centre's submission to the WA government argued insecure work overlaps with most of the ten hazards. Examples:
  - Hazard 3 — **work schedule / unpredictable hours**: applies to most casual workers; **53% have hours (and therefore income) that vary week to week.**
  - Job insecurity **exacerbates** hazards that also hit permanent workers — e.g., workload (hazard 1), lack of control (hazard 4), lack of support.

## Worse health and safety outcomes (evidence)

- A growing body of research treats insecure work as a health hazard. Leading experts **Michael Quinlan (UNSW)** and **Elsa Underhill (Deakin)** told the current **Senate inquiry into job security** that insecure workers face:
  - higher incidence/frequency of injuries, including fatalities;
  - poorer physical and mental health (e.g., from bullying);
  - poorer knowledge of and access to employment rights, and less willingness to raise concerns.
- **Fear of losing work** discourages complaints or use of rights. A **2021 ACTU survey of 1,540 workers** found:
  - **40%** of insecure workers had **worked while unwell** because they lacked paid leave;
  - **67%** of those who worked through illness feared taking leave would affect their job (vs **55%** of permanent workers);
  - **50%** of those sexually harassed **took no action** for fear of consequences (vs **32%** of permanent workers).

## Are codes of practice legally enforceable?

- WHS regulation is a **three-tier framework**: legislation (broad responsibilities) → regulations → **codes of practice** (practical guides).
- Codes **don't have the same legal force as regulations**, but **courts can use them** to judge whether an organisation took the "reasonable steps" the law requires.
- The new **WA code applies to all workplaces** (unlike codes tied to specific hazards such as dangerous chemicals).

## From recognition to change

- In the short term, inclusion in WA's code is **unlikely to change much**.
- In theory, employers of casual/contract workers should run a **risk-assessment** and implement controls. WHS uses a **hierarchy of risk controls**:
  - **Eliminate the risk** (top) — e.g., convert as many workers as possible to **permanent** status.
  - **Reduce the risk** (lower) — e.g., give casual workers **as much notice of shifts as possible** and more **predictable scheduling**.
- The author is sceptical this will happen: **codes alone rarely drive change.** **Real change needs legislative reform or stronger unions.** Some European countries cap the number of temporary-agency and fixed-term workers — but such laws also need enforcement.
- Conclusion: WA's code is an important **first step**; insecure work is widespread, its harms are known, and it's time to do more.

## Key terms (for your assignment)

- **Psychosocial hazard** — work-design/management factor that can cause psychological or physical harm.
- **Insecure / precarious / contingent work** — casual, labour-hire, fixed-term, gig roles lacking security/leave.
- **Code of practice** — practical WHS guide; persuasive in court but not as binding as a regulation.
- **Hierarchy of risk controls** — eliminate > reduce (then lower-order controls).
- Bodies/sources cited: **WHO** (ten psychosocial hazards), **Boland Review (2018)**, **Senate inquiry into job security**, **ACTU 2021 survey**.
