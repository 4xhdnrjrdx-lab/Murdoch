## Core argument (abstract, paraphrased)

Industry 4.0 is reshaping manufacturing and has a strong knock-on effect on globalisation by changing the workforce and widening access to skills and knowledge. The paper maps the top skills industry needs to deliver Industry 4.0 and offers a "blueprint" for how people can acquire them. Its central claim: life-long learning must become a strategic goal for organisations, and both individuals and firms have to commit to reskilling and upskilling, making these opportunities accessible, available and affordable.

Two figures the author repeats as the headline statistics (sourced from the World Economic Forum):

- By 2025, ~50% of all employees will need reskilling because of new technology adoption.
- Over two-thirds of skills considered important today will change within five years.
- About one-third of the essential 2025 skills will be technology competencies not yet seen as crucial today.

## 1. Introduction (paraphrased)

- Industry 4.0 (I4.0) is described as a fusion system built on traditional manufacturing combined with cyber-physical systems, the Internet, IoT and IIoT, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, virtualization and hyper-converged infrastructure, producing an "intelligent production system."
- The three drivers behind every past industrial revolution: workforce, capital, technology.
- WEF _Future of Jobs Report 2020_ projected half of all employees would need reskilling by 2025 — and that figure excludes people not currently employed.
- A 2016 WEF report projected 65% of children entering primary school today will end up in job types that don't yet exist.
- Framing goal: build inclusive, sustainable economies via a life-long education system where "no one is left behind."

## 2. Background and Literature

### 2.1 What skills will be in high demand?

The article tracks WEF top-10 skill lists across 2015, 2020 and 2025 (Gray 2016; Whiting 2020).

- Between 2015 and 2020, lists overlap heavily (8 of 10 the same).
- From 2020 to 2025, ~60%+ of core skills shift; 7 of the 10 top-2025 skills are new versus 2020/2015.
- Analytical thinking and active learning take the top two 2025 spots ("cognitive self-management").
- Critical thinking and problem-solving slip to 3rd–4th in 2025 but have been consistently valued since 2016.

Note from the text: items 6–10 in 2025 are newly emerging skills focused on technology competencies, cognitive reasoning and leadership; 67% of today's important skills change within five years; one-third of 2025 essential skills are tech competencies not crucial today.

### 2.2 What skills are less focused on?

- Negotiation and people management ranked high in 2015 but drop off the 2025 list — as firms lean on data analytics, these retreat in decision-making.
- The article notes society expects AI and machine learning to provide decision-support to boards by 2026.
- Soft cognitive skills like quality control, active listening and emotional intelligence (core in 2015) vanish from the 2025 top-10; replaced by self-management skills: active learning, resilience, stress tolerance, flexibility.

### 2.3 Emerging disruptive technologies

Seven disruptive technology groups are presented as requiring major skill upgrades. The author notes 94% of business leaders now expect employees to learn new skills on the job (Whiting 2021).

**TABLE 2 — Seven disruptive technologies in Industry 4.0** (descriptions paraphrased)

1. **AI & ML** — ability of a computer/computer-controlled robot to perform tasks associated with intelligent beings; underpinned by NLP, machine learning, deep learning. Example uses: AT&T drones for base-station repair; SK Telecom anomaly detection. Many AI applications still at conceptual stage with limited commercial value so far.
2. **Quantum Computing** — processes information beyond binary 0/1 using quantum mechanics; promises rapid computation and "un-hackable" internet. Examples: Delft University 4-city quantum network (2020); China's ~2,000 km Beijing–Shanghai backbone; Google's quantum-supremacy proof.
3. **5G & 6G** — 5G boosts data-transmission efficiency (higher rates, lower latency, mass connectivity). 6G will connect "everything" with full-dimensional coverage, integrating sensing, communication, computing, control, positioning, radar, navigation, imaging.
4. **IoT & IIoT** — networks of physical objects; IIoT links fabrication/material-handling equipment, freight sensors, asset tracking; extends IT into Operational Technology (OT).
5. **Data Science & Business Intelligence** — requires coding, data mining, analytics, modeling, human–machine interaction; by 2025 nearly 30% of data will be "real-time."
6. **Cybersecurity** — protecting systems from unauthorized access/attack (e.g., 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware); differential privacy used by US Census, Apple, Facebook.
7. **Green Energy** — renewable/clean energy (solar, wind, water, nuclear, geothermal, bioenergy) as an integral part of I4.0; growing workforce demand.

US Bureau of Labor Statistics growth projections cited: network/computer systems administrators +15,000 jobs by 2029; computer & information research scientists +19% by 2026; information security analysts +20% by 2026.

### 2.4 High-tech manufacturing exports & patents (World Bank data)

- Tables 3–7 use World Bank indicators (regions coded: TEA East Asia & Pacific, CHN China, USA, EUU European Union, TLA Latin America & Caribbean, TSS Sub-Saharan Africa).
- 2017–2019: ~30% of manufacturing exports in East Asia & Pacific and China were high-tech; ~15–20% for US and EU; much lower for Sub-Saharan Africa.
- 2010–2019: resident patent applications in East Asia & Pacific and China grew >300%; EU and US stayed roughly flat.
- EU and US collect the most receipts for letting others use their intellectual property.
- Takeaway: technology innovation and workforce knowledge/skill level move together — hence the priority on skilling/reskilling/upskilling.

## 3. Global effects of reskilling/upskilling (country cases, paraphrased)

- **Africa (developing):** strong impact of I4.0 but weak human-capacity development; Nigeria — largest African economy yet ranked 152/157 on human capital (World Bank 2020); South Africa's manufacturing workforce skews low-skilled vs I4.0 needs (Maisiri & Van Dyk 2021).
- **Central & Eastern Europe (former Eastern Bloc):** Japanese automakers in the Visegrád Group (Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia) transferred on-the-job training, job rotation, quality circles and kaizen, creating a hybrid I4.0 production system.
- **Mexico (emerging):** large auto/parts exporter but many firms run on legacy systems; digitalization less mature than US/Europe/China; less-tech-intensive jobs at risk from AI; reskilling an urgent priority.
- **Norway & UK (developed):** both increased R&D funding for universities/research institutes; debates dominated by fears of job loss from robotics and AI (Lloyd & Payne 2019); Nordic "learning-rich" work organisation; UK manufacturing on a low automation level (some "haven't done Industry 3.0 yet").
- **China (manufacturing hub):** shifting from export-led to innovation/investment-led; prioritising self-reliant core tech amid US sanctions; Germany's dual VET system held up as a model; first-job tenure shrinking sharply across generations (1970s-born ~51 months → 1990s-born ~19 months); many employers still treat reskilling as a cost not an investment.
- Summary: ~one-third of global occupational transitions will occur this century; education and skill-development systems must transform across all industries.

## 4. A system-driven blueprint for reskilling/upskilling

**FIGURE 1 — "A Blueprint of Workforce Reskilling and Upskilling"** (structure described)
The figure shows three feeders converging on a **future-ready workforce**:

- **Top band — reskilling/upskilling through college education:** College education → Online program → Degree program → … → Non-degree program.
- **Middle band — the pipeline:** Early childhood training to tertiary education → Lifelong learning, reskilling, upskilling → Future-ready workforce.
- **Bottom band — reskilling/upskilling through non-traditional options:** Employer-sponsored on-the-job training → Conference/seminar/webinar etc. → Self-study → … → Certificate offered by professional organizations.
- Underlying system concept = technology + people + organization, built around a human–machine collaboration framework for a "win–win."

### 4.1 Scenario for skilling/upskilling

WEF (Schwab & Zahidi 2020): by 2025, 85 million jobs may be displaced by the human–machine division of labour, while 97 million new jobs may emerge. 84% of employers expect to digitalize work processes, including major expansion of remote work. Rising skills: analytical thinking, active learning, critical thinking, complex problem-solving, plus self-management (stress tolerance, flexibility).

### 4.2 Defining reskilling vs upskilling

- **Upskilling** = gaining new skills for your _current_ role (e.g., an accountant moving from abacus to digital spreadsheets).
- **Reskilling** = learning skills for a _different/new_ role (e.g., a switchboard operator retraining after the job disappeared).

### 4.3 Which sectors need the most?

- McKinsey (Ellingrud, Gupta, Salguero 2020): 39–58% of work activities in operationally labor-intensive sectors could be automated (predictable, repetitive tasks).
- Labor-intensive sectors (manufacturing, food service, retail, agriculture, mining) need most reskilling; skilled professionals need upskilling.
- Skill-biased technical change has driven job polarization; IT alone explains 60–90% of the increased relative demand for college-educated workers, 1970–2000 (Kim & Park 2020).

### 4.4 A collaborative ecosystem

Shift from automation-focus to intelligent human–machine collaboration. University + government + business form an "education alliance"/reskilling ecosystem (Li 2020). Sub-themes:

- **4.4.1 Higher education:** new programs (data science, cybersecurity); Stanford2025 project — "open-loop university" (six years of HE spread across an adult career) and "AXIS FLIP" (skills/competency over disciplines). **Experiential learning** — internships as a route to a future-ready employee, assessed via measurable rubrics for problem-solving, critical thinking, device-operation skills. Non-degree certificates growing.
- **Technical & vocational (VET):** Germany's dual VET system partners with ~430,000 companies, ~80% apprenticeship-to-job conversion; China's vocational/technical colleges (e.g., Wuxi Machinery Manufacturing School investing 6,000,000 yuan in hands-on centers).
- **4.4.2 Non-traditional training:** Professional certificates (e.g., ASCM/APICS, CPIM in planning & inventory management); re-certification (e.g., nurse practitioners recertify every 5 years via AANP); company-sponsored on-the-job training (Japanese Quality Circles; Microsoft Excel/Power BI upskilling); self-study open courses (MIT OpenCourseWare — 2,500+ courses; SAS self-paced coding courses).

### 4.5 Obstacles

- McKinsey survey of 116 executives (2017): ~1 in 4 managers lacked clear understanding of automation's skill impact; ~1 in 4 lacked tools to quantify the business case; ~1 in 3 felt their HR infrastructure couldn't execute a reskilling strategy.
- Reskilling hardest in operationally intensive sectors (manufacturing, transport, retail) where workers tend to have less formal education.
- Other barriers: employee unwillingness (especially older workers), accessibility, affordability, curriculum misalignment with I4.0 skills, and inadequate public funding/safety nets.

## 5. Conclusions

Restates the headline WEF figures (50% need reskilling by 2025; two-thirds of important skills change; one-third of 2025 skills are new tech competencies). Argues life-long learning is now a reality that must sit inside organisational strategy, and stresses alignment between industry skill needs and higher education.

**5.1 Contributions (three-fold):** (1) maps the 2015/2020/2025 top-10 skill trend as a basis for designing reskilling courses; (2) shows new skills/tech spreading faster than a decade ago across Africa, Asia, Europe, Latin America, so life-long learning must be strategic; (3) offers an Industry 4.0 education ecosystem guideline for individuals, universities and companies, stressing diversity and respect for different cultures/governance.

**5.2 Future research:** strengthen links between higher education, labor markets and industry; case studies on building life-long-learning cultures equitably; comparative cross-country studies; better skill-specific program design and accreditation.
