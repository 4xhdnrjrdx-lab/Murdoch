# What is a Project? (Five Phases) — Content Dump

Source: "What is a Project?" video text (Session 2 resource; the five-phases version that appears on Slides 17–18 of the BUS363 Session 2 deck). Provided as transcript text.
Note: this is the **phases** companion to the separate Knowledge Train video that covers the five project _characteristics_ (temporary / change / unique / cross-functional / risk). Same title, different angle — keep the two distinct.

## Intro

- Projects come in all shapes and sizes. Projects need project managers and teams to help plan, monitor, and achieve goals.
- It's important to distinguish projects from day-to-day activities we call **'business-as-usual' (BAU)**.
- Project definitions vary by **scope, budget, and size**, but all share certain features in common — set out as the five phases below.

## The five project phases

- **Initiation** — the pre-planning phase. To ensure a project succeeds, even plans need a little planning.
- **Planning** — after the project has been initiated, it's time to figure out exactly how to make the project succeed.
- **Execution** — the 'doing' phase. By now, everyone should know what needs to be done and what their role is.
- **Monitoring** — project managers form a bridge between stakeholders and project developers, running back and forth between the two, passing on important information and updates.
- **Closure** — the project manager's job doesn't end when the development stage is over. They sit down and review the details of the project, seeing what went wrong and what went right.

## Wrap-up

- Project managers must have a working understanding of each phase to carry out tasks successfully.
- By understanding what we mean by 'project', you can also deduce a fairly accurate idea of what's expected of project managers and project support teams in their respective roles.

## Key terms & references

- **Concept:** project vs **Business-as-Usual (BAU)**.
- **The five project phases:** Initiation, Planning, Execution, Monitoring, Closure.
- **Defining variables:** scope, budget, size.
- **Roles:** project managers; project support teams; the PM as a "bridge" between stakeholders and developers.

# What is a Project? — Content Dump

## Part 1 — Defining Business as Usual (BAU)

- **What a project is not:** projects are distinct from the everyday tasks done at work.
- **Business as Usual (BAU):** the everyday, ongoing, normal activities needed to keep an organisation running.
- **BAU examples:** answering the phone, making coffee, replying to emails, writing reports for a boss, regular weekly meetings with colleagues.
- **Organisational structure:** commercial organisations are usually structured into functional units; government entities into departments.
- **Narrow focus:** work within these units/departments typically has a narrow focus — e.g., the Sales department focuses on increasing sales; the IT department focuses on maintaining IT systems.

## Part 2 — Project Examples and Core Characteristics

- **Project examples:** developing a new smartphone; designing and running a marketing campaign; improving existing BAU processes and procedures; a wedding; moving into a new house.
- **Characteristic 1 — Temporary:** every project has a defined start and end date. Even if late, the work eventually stops; if it never stops, it's BAU, not a project.
- **Characteristic 2 — Brings change:** projects are intended to bring about changes to BAU.
- **Outputs:** projects achieve change by creating outputs that BAU then uses. E.g. (1) a new product brought to market may require new support and sales processes; (2) improving BAU efficiency, such as moving from a paper-based system to an automated one.

## Part 3 — Uniqueness, Scale, and Risk

- **Characteristic 3 — Unique:** projects are always unique. Even two similar projects produce different results each time they're run.
- **Characteristic 4 — Cross-functional / scale:** projects are often cross-functional and can be global in scale in large organisations. Example: rolling out a new email system across the entire globe is "infinitely more complex" than rolling it out within one small department in one country.
- **Characteristic 5 — Risk:** risk = uncertainty and the "things that might go wrong." Example: developing a new phone only to find nobody buys it.
- **Inherent risk:** projects are inherently riskier than BAU because they often involve doing things for the first time, whereas BAU repeats normal, everyday work.

## Key terms & references

- **Concepts:** Business as Usual (BAU); functional units / departments; the five project characteristics — **temporary** (start/end dates), **brings change**, **unique**, **cross-functional / global scale**, **risk (uncertainty)**; outputs (what a project hands to BAU).
- **Project examples:** smartphone development, marketing campaign, wedding, moving house, global email-system rollout, process automation (paper → automated).
- **BAU examples:** answering phones, making coffee, replying to emails, writing reports, weekly meetings.
- **Organisation:** Knowledge Train.
