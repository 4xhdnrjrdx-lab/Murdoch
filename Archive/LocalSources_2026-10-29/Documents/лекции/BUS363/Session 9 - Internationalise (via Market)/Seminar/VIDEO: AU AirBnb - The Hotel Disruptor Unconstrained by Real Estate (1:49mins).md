## Part 1 — Market impact and disruption

- Airbnb is framed as a "big deal" not just for its valuation but for its **fundamental disruption of the hotel industry**.
- (~2014, per PrivCo) Airbnb's **gross bookings ≈ $240 million** in the prior year.
- For comparison, gross bookings of major chains (same framing): **Marriott ≈ $1B**, **Starwood ≈ $2B**, **Hyatt ≈ $2B**, **Hilton ≈ $4B** — i.e. Airbnb was rapidly approaching established-chain scale.
- The growth was coming **at traditional hotels' expense**. A **Boston University study** found that for **every 1% increase** in Airbnb's business, the rest of the hotel industry saw a decline of about **five basis points** (0.05%).

## Part 2 — Scalability and inventory comparison

- Airbnb's core model advantage: growth is **unconstrained by real estate or capital investment** (it doesn't build or own the rooms).
- The Airbnb CEO (then **Brian Chesky** — verify) illustrated this on Twitter: where **Marriott** aimed to add **30,000 rooms in a year**, Airbnb was on track to add the **same amount of inventory in ~two weeks**.
- Year-end inventory (per PrivCo, ~2013): **Airbnb ≈ 90,000 rooms**, **Starwood ≈ 346,000**, **Marriott ≈ 373,000**, **Hilton ≈ 678,323**.
- Since that report, Airbnb's inventory had surged to **≈ 550,000 listings** — an increase of **over 500%**.

## Part 3 — Guest behaviour and listing composition

- **Longer stays:** Airbnb guests averaged **~5 nights** vs **~2.8 nights** in hotels.
- **Higher spend:** Airbnb users spent **~$978 per trip**, roughly **50% more** than typical hotel guests.
- **Listing mix:** **57%** entire apartments/houses, **41%** private (single) rooms, **2%** shared rooms.
- Wry aside: the share of Airbnb hosts who actually pay rental-income tax is unknown — "between the host and God and the IRS."
