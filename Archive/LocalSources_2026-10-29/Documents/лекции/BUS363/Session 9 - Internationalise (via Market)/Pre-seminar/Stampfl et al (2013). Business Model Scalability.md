## Abstract / purpose

There is broad agreement that a **scalable business model** is central to new-venture success, yet the factors that _drive_ business model scalability had received little attention. The paper builds an **explorative model of business model scalability** from two inputs: (a) a synthesis of a wide literature spanning entrepreneurship, strategy and business-model research, and (b) qualitative data from in-depth expert interviews with experienced entrepreneurs and investors. The model helps explain the role of a scalable business model in venture growth and identifies mechanisms behind successful web-based business model innovations. It is offered as a starting point for further research and as guidance for executives assessing the potential of new business models.

**Keywords:** scalability; business model; business model innovation; internet; new venture; start-up; venture capital; growth; e-business.

---

## 1. Introduction (context)

- Innovation pressure has risen for decades; one increasingly prominent response is innovative **business model design** — game-changing approaches to value creation and the architecture of a firm (citing Osterwalder et al., 2005; Markides, 2006; Teece, 2007; Johnson et al., 2008; Zott & Amit, 2008).
- Business-model research has been criticised for conceptual ambiguity (Porter 2001 called it "an invitation for faulty thinking and self-delusion"; Chesbrough & Rosenbloom 2002 noted it draws on many disciplines but gains prominence in none). Still, Zott et al. (2011) note the business model is now established as a **new unit of analysis** giving a holistic view of how firms do business.
- Many of the fastest-growing companies (Facebook, Groupon, Salesforce.com) were former internet start-ups. On the internet, startup costs are low but **attention** is the scarce resource. Cheap experimentation means start-ups often revise their models several times before becoming profitable. Prior research (Amit & Zott 2001; Rappa 2004; Bouwman & MacInnes 2006) identifies **scalability of internet-based business models** as a key factor in successful business model innovation — but the _drivers_ of that scalability are under-researched, which this study addresses.

## 2. Internet-based business models — why the web is fertile ground

Three reasons the internet enables business model innovation (Amit & Zott 2001; Mendelson 2000):

1. New technologies reduce the **cost of communication**.
2. New forms of interaction enable **innovative transaction and exchange mechanisms**.
3. The high **"clock speed"** (rapid environmental change) of IT fosters new organisational forms.

New ventures are disadvantaged by **newness and smallness** (Dean & Meyer 1996) but this also gives them **agility** to respond quickly with new models. A start-up typically revises its business model **four times or more** before becoming profitable (Johnson et al. 2008). Cheap experimentation lets internet start-ups switch (e.g. pay-per-use → subscription) with minor website changes, or even run multiple models at once. Most disruptive business model innovations come from **industry newcomers** (Markides 2008).

## 3. Scalability of business models — defining the concept

- A search of EBSCO Business Source Premier for "scalability" (in title/abstract) + "business model" (in text) returned **201 articles**: ~**83% focused on scalability in information technology**, only **9% on business**, and **8% on other areas**.
- In a **technological** context, scalability = a system's ability to accommodate increased workload (Hill 1990).
- In a **business** context there is **no generally accepted definition**, but it is strongly tied to a company's **growth potential**, often understood as the ability to exploit **economies of scale** (rising production capacity → falling unit cost) (Rappa 2004). The paper adopts this **economic** understanding.
- **Core definition (Hallowell 2001):** _Business model scalability = a business model's ability to increase revenues faster than the corresponding cost base._
- Gilbert et al. (2006): top predictors of new-venture growth are entrepreneur characteristics, resources, strategy, industry, and organisation structure/systems — and the business model (tied to strategy) is another key growth factor.
- Investors often seek **"infinite scalability"**: early investment at low profitability can yield high returns once fixed costs are amortised across a large customer base (Hallowell 2001).
- **Nicholas Carr** (former HBR executive editor, 2000): on the internet, if you can't get big fast you're nowhere; the _entire business model_ — not just the technology — must scale, allowing quick extension into new markets horizontally or vertically. "Will it scale?" is among the first questions VCs ask.

**Illustrative cases used in this section:**

- **Airbnb** (founded Aug 2008, San Francisco): online marketplace connecting tenants and landlords, handles booking + payments, takes a **10% commission**. Reported **800% growth in 2010**; listings in **170 countries**; marginal cost of additional transactions near zero (except extra server capacity); raised **>$100 million** funding.
- **Groupon** (launched Nov 2008): discount gift certificates redeemable at local businesses; spurned a **$5.3 billion** takeover offer from Google; grew virally (users recommend deals to hit the minimum sign-up threshold, else the deal is cancelled). **Key weakness:** relies on a large salesforce to acquire/manage deals → high personnel intensity → **inhibited scalability**. Could improve by switching to **self-service technology**.
- **Google** (advertising model needs high traffic volume) — reinforces that e-business models built for **mass markets** depend on **low marginal costs** and **low customer acquisition costs**.

## 4. Research method

- Approach: **grounded theory** (Strauss & Corbin 1990) — inductive, qualitative model-building — while accepting (per Vandenbosch et al. 2006) that researchers can't be fully free of prior theoretical expectations.
- **Two data sources:** (1) synthesis of literature from entrepreneurship, strategy and business-model research; (2) **12 in-depth, semi-structured expert interviews**.
- **Sample (Table 1):** 6 **entrepreneurs** (each founded ≥1 internet company; average **2.4** founded per interviewee) + 6 **investors** (each invested in ≥2 such start-ups). Mix gives an "inside view" (entrepreneurs = operational insight) and "outside view" (investors = many models seen). Foci spanned Internet, Mobile, Agency, Incubator, Venture Capital, TV, Gaming. Data collected **15 February – 22 March 2011**.
- Interviews asked about current activities, definition of "business model", preferences for model types, factors inhibiting/supporting scalability, the role of scalability in ventures and investment decisions, and examples of perfectly (or poorly) scalable web companies. Recorded, transcribed, reviewed.
- **Coding (Strauss & Corbin 1990), three procedures:**
  - **Open coding** — identify and categorise a multitude of potential scalability factors.
  - **Axial coding** — group similar factors, establish connections, enhance conceptual coherence/internal validity.
  - **Selective coding** — select the core category, relate it to others, validate relationships (antecedents ↔ consequences).
- Two researchers coded independently for reliability (Krippendorff 1980); the model was then shared with matching experts to refine and confirm (Charmaz 2000).

## 5. Findings — the explorative model

### Core distinction: Conceptualisation vs. Realisation

The model separates two phases (conceptually, not strictly chronologically — for internet ventures, design and implementation run in parallel; Sosna et al. 2010):

- **Business Model Conceptualisation** = the **design** of the business model → contains the **antecedents** of scalability.
- **Business Model Realisation** = the **implementation and operation** of the model → contains the **consequences** and **moderators**.

Key supporting idea (Osterwalder et al. 2005): a business model cannot be successful per se — a strong model can fail under bad management, and a weak model can succeed with strong execution. Interview Partner 2: a game-changing model won't scale if you can't operate it. Models are treated as **"recipes"** (Baden-Fuller & Morgan 2010) — like an architect's model used to advise on how to get the result right.

### Structure of Figure 1 (the model), described in text

**LEFT — Business Model Conceptualisation (5 antecedents)** feed into → **Scalability of Business Model** (centre):

1. **Technology** — Automation of Processes; Scalability of Technical Infrastructure
2. **Cost and Revenue Structure**
3. **Adaptability to Different Legal Regimes**
4. **Network Effects** — Going Viral; Critical Mass
5. **User Orientation** — Problem Solving; Simplicity; Previous User Knowledge

**RIGHT — Business Model Realisation:**

- **Consequences** of scalability: **Company Growth** and **Investor Attractiveness** (with a **bi-directional** link between the two).
- **Moderators** (sitting between scalability and its consequences): **Market** (Market Potential, Market Dynamics, Market Education) and **Management** (Team, Location, Partnerships).

---

### 5.1 Antecedents (the 5 design-phase factors)

These five "ingredients" were categorised as **mutually exclusive** (Chrisman et al. 1988).

**(a) Technology** — two sub-factors:

- **Automation of processes:** reduces dependence on human resources (e.g. automating user registration, customer service) → keeps **personnel intensity low** → lowers **fixed costs**. Example: **Salesforce.com** (cloud SaaS, subscription-based) handles registration via self-service and distributes entirely digitally — no physical shipping. _Caution (Hallowell 2001 "paradox of human resources in e-commerce"):_ the amount of physical service needed to deliver value shapes the variable-to-fixed-cost ratio and thus scalability. Less automation can mean lower scalability (a negative for investors), but **more human/personal service can improve customer experience** → potential competitive advantage. (Trade-off.)
- **Scalability of technical infrastructure:** techniques to support more users without performance decline (Bochmann et al. 2003) — software and server architecture. Critical once a start-up grows fast; firms must be **prepared for exponential user growth** or risk being "surpassed by its own scaling efforts" (Interview Partner 1). Example: German start-up **Phonedeck** (remote smartphone control via web app) went viral via media coverage days after launch, was unprepared for the surge, and suffered system overload (publicly apologised on Facebook, April 2012).

**(b) Cost and revenue structure:**

- Growth must be financed; new ventures often can't fund fast growth from cash flow alone (Storey 1994). **Bootstrapping** (meeting resource needs without long-term external finance; Winborg & Landström 2000) has **limited scalability** and hampers growth (Patel et al. 2011).
- Example: **Groupon** + copycats (**LivingSocial**, **Daily Deal**, defunct **CityDeal**) — high personnel intensity from large salesforces drives costs up with each new deal, casting doubt on viability.
- Experts preferred models that **generate revenue early while keeping fixed costs low**. Interview Partner 3: web models usually imply low initial costs — "start fast and scale even faster."

**(c) Adaptability to different legal regimes:**

- Mentioned by **7 of 12** interviewees (>50%) — different legal restrictions can severely inhibit scalability.
- Example: **Spotify** (Swedish music streaming) was long unavailable in Germany because its digital-distribution model didn't comply with German regulations — the rights society **GEMA** refused permission. Competitor **Simfy** negotiated licensing successfully and could stream legally in Germany. (IP-rights systems differ by country.)
- Interview Partner 7: **internationalisation** is laborious — translation, knowing each country's legal restrictions, even which colours are culturally inappropriate — all can threaten/constrain scalability and add cost. Interview Partner 3: a model easily transferable across countries is a big asset.
- Internationalisation is easier for purely-online (no physical transaction) models, e.g. **Dropbox** (online storage/sync). Still, every new market requires local legal compliance.
- Literature support: Beck et al. (2005) — **smaller firms are hurt more by legal barriers**, so new ventures should deliberately design models flexible to different legal regimes.

**(d) Network effects** (a.k.a. network externalities; Economides 1996; Shapiro & Varian 1999):

- **Positive network effect** = consumers value a product more as more others use it (Conner 1995). First observed in physical networks (telecoms; Oren & Smith 1987). Textbook case: **Facebook** — value rises with each new active user (friends invite friends).
- **Effects can turn negative:** user loss can accelerate exponentially. Example: **StudiVZ** (German Facebook clone) — once the leading German student social network via rapid network-effect growth, it collapsed as Facebook spread in Europe and users migrated en masse.
- **Platforms / lock-in:** charging higher prices possible once an offer becomes the standard (Brynjolfsson & Kemerer 1996; Gallaugher & Wang 2002). Distributing software without copy protection can be advantageous by growing the user base faster (Conner & Rumelt 1991). Historical example: **IBM vs Apple** PC platform competition (late 1970s) — IBM's **open architecture** led to "IBM-compatible" dominance and Apple losing share (Shim & Lee 2012). Network-effect models create a **"lock-in"** (Amit & Zott 2001) raising switching costs.
- Community size & revenue: larger communities → more transactions → higher margins (Rothaermel & Sugiyama 2001), who name **five revenue sources**: (a) subscription fees, (b) usage fees, (c) member fees, (d) advertising commissions, (e) transaction commissions — but argue only **advertising and transaction commissions** create _sustainable_ value.
- **Caveat (negative externalities at scale):** Asvanund et al. (2004), studying P2P music-sharing, found that as network size grows, users add less marginal value and contribute less; bigger ≠ always more valuable.
- Two sub-aspects experts stressed:
  - **Critical mass** (Markus 1987): once a critical number/proportion of users is reached, adoption spreads rapidly. A **double-edged sword** — failing to reach it kills the venture early; passing it boosts growth. Especially vital for community/advertising/transaction-commission models (Rothaermel & Sugiyama 2001). Example: **Zynga's FarmVille** reached critical mass easily by building on the fast-growing **Facebook** network (tools like **Facebook Connect** accelerate sign-ups). Interview Partner 10: ad/link-based models need extremely high critical mass to gain market power.
  - **Going viral** (viral marketing): reach critical mass and foster positive externalities fast (Ferguson 2008 — leverage "brand evangelists"). Design question: how many users will follow each one who signs up. **Groupon** is the example — users invite friends to hit the deal minimum ("a kind of a pull effect," Interview Partner 2).

**(e) User orientation:**

- Innovation should be **user-driven ("need pull")** rather than mere **"technology push"** (Rothwell 1994); "Customer experience must be at the heart of any innovation and design process" (Kaplan 2012). Three sub-factors:
  - **Problem solving** — does the model address a _pressing_ problem actually faced by a large enough segment? Tied to market size and future potential (small-looking ideas can become big).
  - **Simplicity** — easy-to-understand offers scale faster; people dislike complex products. Interview Partner 9: if people don't understand your offer, they switch instantly and tell no one.
  - **Previous user knowledge** — models that build on existing skills in the target group (no new learning required) scale more easily (linked to lead-user/product-design research: Schreier & Prügl 2008; Prügl & Schreier 2006).

### 5.2 Realisation — consequences & moderators

**Consequences of scalability:**

- **Company growth:** scalability positively drives venture growth (Miller 2001; Berry et al. 2006; Li 2009). Interview Partner 4: if a model isn't scalable, nothing else matters for investment.
- **Investor attractiveness:** all **six investors** named scalability as one of the most important investment criteria — though the **management team** ranks even higher (Interview Partner 3). No-scalability is a **knock-out criterion** (Interview Partner 12: they don't invest in models that can't scale quickly). Consistent with VC-evaluation research (MacMillan et al. 1987; Franke et al. 2006, 2008).
- **Bi-directional link:** growth raises investor attractiveness, and attracting funding accelerates growth (Cooper et al. 1994; Lee et al. 2001).

**Moderators** (illustrated by **Rocket Internet**, which replicates proven, scalable US models in Europe/Asia and often _outperforms_ the originals through execution excellence — showing the same model yields different outcomes in different environments):

- **Market** — moderates scalability → investor attractiveness and scalability → growth:
  - **Market potential** — current and maximum achievable size of the targeted market/segment; large markets enable high growth; the internet still has "white spaces" (social networks, virtual goods, SaaS).
  - **Market dynamics** — speed of transition from current volume to realised potential (Zahra & Bogner 1999); dynamic markets let models reach full potential fast. Investors rated this most important (Interview Partner 4: "market dynamics, followed by ticket size and sales cycle").
  - **Market education** — can inhibit _or_ support. If customers don't yet understand the offer, costly education slows scaling; once educated, expansion is easier. The first mover educates the market and can **benefit fast followers**. Examples: **Groupon** (group buying + viral marketing) succeeded where the near-identical **letsbuyit.com** (2001) failed — difference was market education; **MyHammer** (German craftsmen-services auction) vs the earlier **Yellout** (2001) — same concept, market wasn't ready then (Interview Partner 10).

- **Management** — Team, Location, Partnerships:
  - **Team** — composition, experience, communication matter for sales growth (Eisenhardt & Schoonhoven 1990; Ensley et al. 2002). Interview Partner 11: even a great-on-paper model fails with the wrong team.
  - **Location** — most successful internet firms founded in **Silicon Valley, London or Berlin**; location affects survival (Lechner & Dowling 2003) because ventures depend on the local environment for people and resources.
  - **Partnerships** — external relationships are a source of competitiveness and growth (Lechner & Dowling 2003); including partners in the model expands innovation output and opens otherwise-unreachable markets (Chesbrough & Schwartz 2007; Keinz & Prügl 2010). Needed partners include link-building firms, online marketing agencies, consultancies; choice of investors/business angels/partners is essential (Interview Partner 3).

## 6. Discussion / conclusions

- The model identifies **5 design-phase factors** (technology, cost & revenue structure, adaptability to legal regimes, network effects, user orientation) leading to scalability, which in turn raises **investor attractiveness** and **company growth**, **moderated** by **market** and **management**.
- Findings are relevant to **incumbents** too — scalability deserves attention already at the **conceptualisation** stage.
- **SaaS** ("digital licence sales") was named by **>50%** of interviewees as the internet model with **perfect scalability** (examples: Salesforce.com, 37signals, Dropbox) — no physical goods, online delivery/maintenance/updates, no salesforce or inventory needed (Interview Partner 6).
- **Limitations:** exploratory/qualitative → a starting point only; relationships need further qualitative + quantitative testing; model emphasises _design_ factors (market/management relate to _implementation_, not necessarily design); expert interviews are subjective and prone to false statements (cross-checked against literature); limited interview count → factors may not be exhaustive; new models will raise new scalability implications.
- **Future research:** whether investors vs entrepreneurs perceive scalability differently; other (non-scalability) drivers of successful business model innovation; environmental conditions for business model innovation (Stampfl & Prügl 2011); implementation of new models generally.

## Key terms & references

**The model in one line:** Five design antecedents → **Scalability of Business Model** → two consequences (Company Growth ↔ Investor Attractiveness), moderated by Market and Management.

**Core definitions:**

- _Business model scalability_ = ability to grow revenues **faster than the cost base** (Hallowell 2001).
- _Technical scalability_ = system's capacity to support more users without performance loss (Bochmann et al. 2003; Hill 1990).
- _Economies of scale_ = higher production capacity → lower unit cost (Rappa 2004).
- _"Infinite scalability"_ — the investor's ideal (Hallowell 2001).

**Frameworks / concepts:** business model as "recipe" (Baden-Fuller & Morgan 2010); design vs implementation split (Osterwalder et al. 2005); grounded theory + open/axial/selective coding (Strauss & Corbin 1990); network effects / externalities (Conner 1995; Shapiro & Varian 1999); critical mass (Markus 1987); going viral / viral marketing (Ferguson 2008); lock-in (Amit & Zott 2001); technology-push vs need-pull (Rothwell 1994); paradox of human resources in e-commerce (Hallowell 2001); bootstrapping (Winborg & Landström 2000).

**Company examples:** Airbnb (10% commission, 170 countries, 800% growth 2010, >$100M raised), Groupon (rejected $5.3B Google offer; viral pull; salesforce limits scaling), Salesforce.com / 37signals / Dropbox (SaaS — "perfect scalability"), Facebook (positive network effects), StudiVZ (negative network effects), Zynga FarmVille (critical mass via Facebook), Spotify vs Simfy (GEMA / legal regimes), Phonedeck (infrastructure overload), Rocket Internet (replication + execution → moderators), letsbuyit.com / MyHammer / Yellout (market education / timing), IBM vs Apple (open platform / lock-in), Google (ad model needs traffic).

**Key cited authors:** Hallowell (2001); Amit & Zott (2001); Rappa (2004); Osterwalder, Pigneur & Tucci (2005); Markus (1987); Conner (1995); Markides (2008); Johnson, Christensen & Kagermann (2008); Gilbert et al.
