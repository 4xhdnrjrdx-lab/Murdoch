## Purpose of the guide

A practical (not theoretical) framework to embed ethics into the **design, development and deployment** of any technology — from gene editing to driverless cars to social media algorithms. Core message: **you're responsible for what you design — make sure you build something good.** Responsibility "begins with you," whether you're Facebook or a brand-new start-up. The guide aims especially to help where the ethical issues **aren't obvious** (the riskiest zone, because nobody's looking). Author Beard: many tech scandals happen because people aren't equipped to take a **holistic view** of the ethical landscape.

---

## Foundational framework: Purpose → Values → Principles

Every ethical framework has three elements:

- **Purpose** — your reason for being; animates your choice of values and principles ("why do we do what we do?").
- **Values** — identify what is _good_; the things you strive for, desire, protect ("what do we care for and defend?").
- **Principles** — identify what is _right_; how you may or may not achieve what's good ("how do we achieve our purpose?").

The guide then adds **Functions** (how ethics is brought into the actual tool/object) below Principles. Flow: Purpose _determines/restrains_ Values _inform/shape_ Principles _operationalised by_ Functions.

## Ethical theories (the philosophical backdrop — "reflective equilibrium")

The framework draws on six traditions so it appeals across moral viewpoints (a "reflective equilibrium"):

1. **Consequentialism** — outcomes are what matter; maximise positive effects over negative (cost/benefit logic; value is measurable; intentions matter less than results; everyone counts equally).
2. **Deontology** (Kant) — focus on duties/obligations regardless of outcome; people have **dignity** and can't be used as mere tools or traded for a price; some acts (e.g. promise-breaking) are always wrong.
3. **Teleology** (Aristotle) — judge things by their **ultimate purpose/telos**; humans should act to support **flourishing**; align means with their reason for being.
4. **Virtue ethics** (Socrates/Plato/Aristotle) — choices shape **character** via virtues/vices; aim at the **golden mean** between extremes (e.g. honesty between dishonesty and tactlessness); practical wisdom (_phronesis_).
5. **Existentialism** (Sartre) — we are "radically free" and therefore "radically responsible"; "existence precedes essence"; denying our freedom/responsibility is **"bad faith."**
6. **Contractualism** (social contract) — power is legitimate only with the **consent** of those governed; increasingly, **commercial enterprises** also hold a "social contract" — their licence to operate depends on public trust and serving the public interest, or they should cease to exist.

## What is technology? Two senses

### (a) "Techno-things" — the three-part model of artefacts

Technological artefacts are **made**, **used**, and **serve a goal**. Their ethics breaks into three considerations:

1. **Unjust goals** — the intended purpose itself is wrong (e.g. slave collars designed to prevent escape).
2. **Unjust use/means** — a defensible goal pursued unacceptably (e.g. surveillance for safety that invades privacy; polluting fuel sources).
3. **Unintended effects** — failure to foresee alternate uses, and uncertainty about effects of new tech. Ethical design must be **responsible and agile** — pre-empting or being able to respond to what designers couldn't predict. (Illustrated by Facebook/Cambridge Analytica and the 2014 Facebook "emotional contagion" study, which fails on goal, means _and_ unintended effects.)

### (b) "Techno-logic" — technology as a way of thinking

A "logic of control" (Heidegger, Ellul) that sees the world as problems to solve, forces to measure, products to store and use. Hugely beneficial (STEM, raised living standards) but risks **"technologising"** things that shouldn't be reduced to use-value, and carries a **totalitarian potential**. The "Uber, but for X" meme captures techno-logic's assumption that the right invention can fix any problem.

## Four "techno-ethical myths" (all wrong)

1. **Technology is value-neutral** ("guns don't kill people…") — false; every artefact embeds value judgements and reshapes how we see the world (the gun reframes the world as targets).
2. **Blame the artefact if things go wrong** — false; technology can be _causally_ responsible but not _morally_ responsible (it lacks agency). The real "monsters" are the negligent/malicious people (Golem, Frankenstein, Jurassic Park). Siloing + incrementalism let people evade responsibility ("banality of evil," Arendt).
3. **We can't halt the tide of technology** (determinism) — false; humanity stays in control; "there is absolutely no inevitability, so long as there is a willingness to contemplate what is happening" (McLuhan). Belief in free will correlates with less cheating and better job performance.
4. **Ethical questions can wait — progress can't!** (Pinker: bioethicists should "get out of the way") — partly right about the cost of delay, but wrong to "bolt on" ethics afterwards: once tech exists, the strongest safeguard (it not existing) is lost. There's an ethical **double-bind** between guarding against bad tech and advancing good tech — which must be addressed **proactively**, not at the decision moment.

## Purpose as a verb: Proposing → Mediating → Purposing

A traditional view treats **purpose as a noun** (a bench is "for" sitting), which risks (a) overlooking unethical uses and (b) excluding legitimate unintended users ("hostile design" of park benches against homeless people). Instead, treat **purpose as a verb** — a fluid, three-stage process drawing on **postphenomenology** (purpose emerges through the user–artefact relationship; Gertz's fork example) and **affordance theory**:

1. **Proposal stage** — designers embed goals/functions/features (and affordances) into the artefact; these features, not the designer's mental intention, are what the user encounters.
2. **Mediation stage** — user evaluates features/affordances and starts deciding how to use it.
3. **Purposing stage** — user acts: accepting the proposed purpose, or repurposing it to a new end (within the affordances). Sometimes purposing seeds an entirely new artefact.

Key implications: purpose **can't be fully determined before use**, is **independent of designer intention**, and **emerges from the designer–user–artefact relationship**.

### Affordance theory (Davis & Chouinard) — six affordances

Affordances reveal what an artefact _can do_ (vs purpose = what it's _for_). Artefacts afford by:

- **Requesting** (recommend a line of action — a doorbell requests you ring it);
- **Demanding** (a behaviour necessary to use it — an ATM demands a PIN);
- **Encouraging** (promote one action — lumbar chairs encourage good posture);
- **Discouraging** (make an action harder — tiny hard-to-find unsubscribe text);
- **Refusing** (prevent an action — password protection);
- **Allowing** (neutral — a car allows any speed).

### Explicit vs implicit values

- **Explicit values** — openly endorsed (e.g. Facebook's "Connecting People," per the Bosworth memo; security in alarms; knowledge in ed-tech; excellence in F1).
- **Implicit values** — embedded/unrecognised (a luxury watch explicitly values accuracy but implicitly values exclusivity; techno-logic implicitly values control/efficiency/measurement — usually only _instrumental_ goods).

## The Eight Principles (the governing framework)

Each principle "goes all the way down" — applying to every feature of every artefact; one bad feature can make the whole artefact ethically unsatisfactory. Designers carry the **burden of proof** to show how each principle is met, and that demonstration must be **understandable, assurable (by a third party), accessible, and complete**. Default stance: **"if not, why not, and what else?"**

### PR00 — Ought Before Can _(governing principle)_

**"The fact that we can do something does not mean that we should."** Before asking if you _can_ build something, ask _why_ you'd build it at all. Ethical responsibility is a **precondition** of design, not an add-on. (Jurassic Park: "so preoccupied with whether they could, they didn't stop to think if they should.") No tech is groundbreaking enough to justify ignoring ethics.

### PR01 — Non-Instrumentalism

**"Never design technology in which people are merely a part of the machine."** Some things (people, ecosystems, some animals, political communities) have **intrinsic value/dignity** and must be **beneficiaries** of design, not cogs in it. Don't reduce people to "things," don't manipulate, promote dignity.

- _Rules of thumb:_ don't reduce people to mere things; people aren't parts; sacrificial tech (protect intrinsic value over the system itself); don't manipulate; promote dignity.
- _Design solutions:_ value hierarchies (block trade-offs "up the ladder" from instrumental to intrinsic goods); explicit, enthusiastic, reconfirmed **consent**; "protected by design" (e.g. an AR game making a landmark a "no-fly zone").
- _Case study — reCaptcha:_ users were unknowingly training Google's AI while verifying themselves → instrumentalisation. Fix: inform users / allow opt-out; "No Captcha reCaptcha" improved alignment.

### PR02 — Self-Determination

**"Maximise the freedom of those affected by your design."** Technology should extend human will and empower free choice; it raises red flags when it restricts choice, manipulates unconsciously, or reframes norms. **But maximising freedom ≠ maximising choice** (too much choice can paralyse).

- _Big topic — Nudging:_ nudges target non-rational decision-making. Permissible nudges: **toward rationality** (e.g. against discriminatory bias), **toward virtue** (e.g. organ donation), **toward a person's own "plan of life,"** and crucially **for the benefit of the nudged** (Rawls — inequality only justified if it helps the least well-off), not the designer/owner.
- _Other rules:_ **non-user immunity** (people can choose which tech shapes their lives); **user sovereignty** (users own what their use produces — data, IP).
- _Design solutions:_ opt-in processes; accessible EULAs; consent-based / best-interest nudging; user control over outputs.
- _Case study — Pokémon GO:_ drew crowds to landmarks and spawned Pokémon on private property/religious sites without consent (opt-_out_ only, and burdensome); the "walk to hatch eggs" health nudge required the app open, making walking unsafe but boosting engagement. Better: **opt-in** for locations; **best-interest nudging** (track steps with screen locked).

### PR03 — Responsibility

**"Anticipate and design for all possible uses."** Responsibility is **shared** across the proposing–mediating–purposing process (rejecting both the instrumentalist "blame the user" and the determinist "blame the designer"):

- **Designer responsibility:** intended purpose, nudges, affordances, openness to repurposing (the **"dual-use" problem**), embedded values.
- **User responsibility:** how they respond to affordances, knowingly accepting an illegitimate purpose, repurposing, personal implications.
- **Shared responsibility:** market **demand** (designers can and should say "no"); **impact on non-users**.
- _Limits:_ not blameable for genuinely **unforeseeable** uses; foreseeable-but-unpreventable harms can still be justified if **benefits outweigh harms, harms aren't intended, and all reasonable preventive steps are taken**.
- _Rules/solutions:_ **update and recall** (fix unanticipated harms fast; if you can't supervise, warn users to stop — like an unpatrolled-beach sign — or shut it down); **rights custodianship**; **transparent provenance** (a verifiable, indestructible record/**ledger** of an artefact's full history); **manage repurposing** (usage tracking, designing against abuse).
- _Case study — Killer robots (LAWS):_ could reduce casualties but create an **"accountability gap."** Solutions: a **human arbiter** for any lethal action; or apply **Ought Before Can** and don't build them at all.

### PR04 — Net Benefit

**"Maximise good, minimise bad."** Benefit measured against: human wellbeing, individual freedom, improving moral character, the common good, inspiring wonder/hope, access to basic needs, protection of rights/dignity, and the environment. Even net-positive tech must **actively minimise harm**, even at some cost to benefit.

- _Double-effect theory_ (when harm is unavoidable): only good outcomes intended; good outweighs harm; harm isn't _instrumental_ to the good; means aren't intrinsically wrong; harm minimised as far as possible.
- _Rules of thumb:_ acknowledge **opportunity costs** (Effective Altruism — do the most good); minimise harm; anticipate side-effects; **the ends don't justify the means**.
- _Design solutions:_ **harm metrics** (know when/how much harm you're causing) and **harm quarantines** ("circuit breakers" to stop harms snowballing).
- _Case study — mousepox/IL-4 (2001):_ a pest-control "contraceptive vaccine" using mousepox + IL-4 unexpectedly killed all the mice and resisted vaccination; because mousepox is related to **smallpox**, the method is a serious **dual-use biorisk**. Solutions: use only non-self-replicating viruses (**harm quarantine**); require a known **antidote** before release; **obstruct bad actors** (e.g. publish without the methodology).

### PR05 — Fairness _(definition + summary; full body truncated)_

**"Treat like cases in a like manner; different cases differently."** Designs can carry **biases**, reflect the status quo, or create **blind spots**, causing some groups to be treated worse on **irrelevant/arbitrary** grounds (race, age, gender, ethnicity, etc.). Fairness requires **justifying any differences** in how the design treats user groups; if some groups suffer greater harm or less benefit, you must ask why and whether the reasons are **defensible**.

### PR06 — Accessibility _(definition + summary; full body truncated)_

**"Design to include the most vulnerable user."** Defining intended users also **isolates non-users**, risking exclusion of people who'd benefit. Design can **reinforce social disadvantage or help overcome it** — but only if you keep **all** possible users in mind and don't dismiss groups as "edge cases."

### PR07 — Purpose _(definition + summary; full body truncated)_

**"Design with honesty, clarity and fitness of purpose."** Design is a **promise** to solve a user's problem — so be honest and clear about your design's **abilities and limitations**, tailor it to a **genuine** problem, and serve that purpose efficiently and effectively.

## A note on implementation / the bigger system

The guide deliberately **doesn't prescribe rules** (rules create a "culture of compliance" rather than genuine responsibility). Designers/developers — better placed than ethicists — choose _how_ to implement each principle, but must **demonstrate publicly** how they did (e.g. "what would an off-switch look like for a piece of synthetic biology?"). The framework is meant to be "bookended" by an **ethical design process** and a **market assurance process**: designers give evidence → an **independent third party** assures it → the technology is "marked" and released. Legislators must decide which principles are obligatory vs voluntary.
