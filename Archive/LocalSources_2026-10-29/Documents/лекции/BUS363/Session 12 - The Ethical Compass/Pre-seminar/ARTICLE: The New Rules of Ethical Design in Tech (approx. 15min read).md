## Core argument

- The author opens with the **"This is Fine" meme** (a dog drinking coffee in a burning room insisting everything's okay, then melting) as a metaphor for the tech sector's denial about ethics. People readily admit _others_ have ethics problems but rarely accept they have one themselves.
- Tech builds nudges that push users into privacy concessions and hardwires assumptions about right/wrong into products that shape millions of lives — yet many won't acknowledge any ethical exposure.
- **Central claim:** **tech design is a human activity, and there is no human behaviour without ethics.** If you believe your product generates no ethical issues, you've missed something — you can't be sure it's fine unless you've examined it (and its stakeholders) through an ethical lens. Ethics is framed as the same kind of craft discipline as eliminating bugs or bias: making people and products the best they can be.

### Worked example — Pokémon GO

Presented as a "business success story" (recorded a **profit of $3 billion at the end of 2018**) that _looks_ ethically innocuous but isn't. Its ethical problems:

- distracted drivers;
- drew users to **dangerous locations** chasing Pokémon;
- disrupted public infrastructure;
- **didn't seek consent** of the real-world sites it included;
- unintentionally **excluded rural neighbourhoods** (many populated by racial minorities);
- released Pokémon in **offensive locations** (e.g. a poison-gas Pokémon at the Holocaust Museum, Washington DC).

The point: these flaws meant it wasn't the best game it could be — and "great" requires "good," which is why designers must consider ethics.

## The Five Practices ("new rules")

### 1. Start with ethical premortems

A **premortem** is the inverse of a postmortem: instead of dissecting failure _after_ a scandal, the team sits down _before_ launch and asks what would make this product an ethical failure, then works backwards to root causes and mitigations. Postmortems can become a way of "washing over" negligence ("we'll look into it so it never happens again" → why not before launch?).

**Shannon Vallor's premortem questions** (Santa Clara "Ethical Toolkit"):

- How could this project fail for ethical reasons?
- What would be the most likely combined causes of our ethical failure/disaster?
- What blind spots would lead us into it?
- Why would we fail to act?
- Why/how would we choose the wrong action?
- What systems/processes/checks/failsafes can we put in place to reduce failure risk?

### 2. Ask the "Death Star question"

Drawn from the novel _Rogue One: Catalyst_ — the Empire built the Death Star by putting experts in **silos** on small projects so no team understood the whole. **Siloing is a major source of ethical negligence**: teams limited to their own contribution (and its incentives) rarely see downstream effects or what happens when their work is combined with others'.

**Questions to ask:**

- What's the big picture — what am I actually helping to build?
- What contribution is my work making, and are there ethical risks I should know about?
- Are there **dual-use risks** in this product I should be designing against?
- If there are risks, are they worth it given the potential benefits?

### 3. Get red teaming

Borrowed from security: the best way to know something is robust is to have someone try to break it.

> **Red team** = a security team whose goal is to find and report vulnerabilities before the "bad guys" do.

Applied to ethics: after building something you think is great, ask an **uninvolved** group to prove it isn't — creating **impartiality and objectivity** (it's hard to see flaws in something you're close to and proud of; new hires often spot problems on day one).

**Red teams should ask:**

- What are the ethical pressure points here?
- Have you made trade-offs between competing values/ideals — and made them the right way?
- What happens if we **widen the circle of users** to include people you didn't consider?
- Should we have taken this project on at all? (If you're knowingly building a Death Star / WMD, you can't make it ethical.)
- Is your solution the only one? Is it the best one?

### 4. Decide what your product is "saying"

Like a toddler with a new toy, users don't access the designer's intention — they test what the product _affords_ them. Technology use is a **two-way conversation**, not a set of instructions. Every product is full of **affordances** — subtle prompts that invite some uses over others — present whether intended or not. Example: hard-to-find privacy settings effectively _tell_ users not to change them, whatever the platform claims. Ask what your product would say if it spoke for itself.

**Design teams should ask:**

- What could a user infer from the design about how the product can/should be used?
- How do you want people to use this?
- How _don't_ you want people to use this?
- Do your design choices and affordances reflect those expectations?
- Are you unnecessarily preventing other legitimate uses?

### 5. Don't forget to show your work

Like getting more marks for showing your working in maths than for the answer alone. In ethical design, **showing your work = being willing to publicly defend the ethical decisions you've made.** This is a practical version of **The Sunlight Test** — would you still do this if the whole world were watching?

**Ask yourself and your team:**

- Are there any limitations to this product?
- What trade-offs have you made (e.g. privacy vs user-customisation)?
- Why did you build this — what problems are you solving?
- Does it risk being misused, and what have you done to mitigate that?
- Are there users who will struggle (e.g. people with disabilities)? If so, why can't you fix it, and why release it despite not being universally accessible?
- How probable are the good and bad effects?

## Closing argument — "Ethics is an investment"

- Organisations pour money into culture initiatives and wellbeing days but often spend **nothing** on ethics, assuming "good people build good things" — which the evidence disproves. Ethics must be **invested in**: learning, resources, systems, recruiting, and incentives.
- **Do it for the right reasons:** not because it makes money or attracts talent (you'd abandon it the moment a more effective route appeared).
- **Ethics ≠ self-regulation.** A lot of current tech-ethics talk is **anti-regulation** — hoping that "being ethical" lets companies self-regulate. The author rejects this: ethics guides good judgement and gives language/precision for what's right or excellent, but people also need **justice** — something to rely on if they're the **least powerful person in the room**. That requires **law and regulation** too, not just ethics.
- **Test of seriousness:** if an organisation claims to take ethics seriously, ask how open it is to **restraint and accountability**, how much it will invest in getting systems right, and whether it would **sack its best performer** if that person behaved wrongly.

## Key terms & references

**The five practices:** (1) ethical **premortem**; (2) the **Death Star question** (anti-siloing); (3) **red teaming**; (4) reading a product's **affordances** ("what is it saying?"); (5) **showing your work** / The Sunlight Test.

**Named tools / concepts:**

- **Premortem** (vs postmortem) + **Shannon Vallor's** ethical-toolkit premortem questions (Santa Clara University).
- **Siloing** and **dual-use risk**.
- **Red team** (security concept applied to ethics).
- **Affordances** — design prompts that shape user behaviour whether intended or not.
- **The Sunlight Test** — act as if the whole world is watching.
- **Ethics as investment**; ethics + **law/regulation** (not self-regulation) for **justice** for the least powerful.

**Illustrative references:** the "This is Fine" meme (KC Green); Pokémon GO ($3B profit 2018; its ethical harms); _Rogue One: Catalyst_ (Death Star siloing).
