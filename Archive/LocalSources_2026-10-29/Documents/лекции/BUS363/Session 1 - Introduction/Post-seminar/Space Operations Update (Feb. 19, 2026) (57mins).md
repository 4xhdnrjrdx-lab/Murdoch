## Part 1 — Opening Statement and the Starliner Investigative Report

- **Introduction:** the moderator opens the news conference on the findings of investigations into the 2024 Crewed Flight Test (CFT) of Boeing's Starliner to the International Space Station (ISS).
- **Administrator's letter:** Administrator **Jared Isaacman** reads a letter to the NASA workforce. He announces Congress will be briefed by the **Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel (ASAP)** and that the independent investigative report will be released publicly, redacted only to the least extent possible.
- **Admission of failure:** Isaacman says that although the crew returned safely, the path taken "did not reflect NASA at our best." Mistakes happen, but transparency and accountability are essential.
- **Core findings:** the investigation found design and engineering deficiencies in Starliner, but the "most troubling failure" was in **decision-making and leadership** — which, unchecked, could create a culture incompatible with human spaceflight.

## Part 2 — History of the Starliner Program and Early Anomalies (OFT-1 & OFT-2)

- **Program origins:** Starliner was conceived under the **Commercial Crew Program (CCP)** in 2010 to ensure dissimilar crew access to low Earth orbit (LEO) after the Space Shuttle's retirement.
- **Orbital Flight Test 1 (OFT-1):**
  - December 2019.
  - A **mission elapsed timing error** stopped the guidance software from calculating orbital insertion burn timing.
  - Caused excessive thruster firings, incorrect orbital insertion, major propellant use, and 10 thrusters declared "failed off."
  - Declared a "high-visibility close call."
- **Orbital Flight Test 2 (OFT-2):**
  - Attempted August 2021; scrubbed during countdown when 13 of 24 **manifold isolation valves** in the service-module propulsion system failed to open.
  - The service module was replaced, leading to a successful launch in May 2022.
  - In flight, three service-module **RCS thrusters** failed off.
- **Investigation deficiencies:** the OFT-1/OFT-2 investigations did not reach root cause. They often stopped at "proximate cause," accepted issues as "unexplained," or gave incorrect diagnoses for lack of technical rigor.

## Part 3 — The Crewed Flight Test (CFT) Failure and Recovery

- **CFT launch:** June 2024, after multiple delays and an unexpected helium leak. Crew: **Butch Wilmore** and **Suni Williams**.
- **Propulsion crisis:** during rendezvous and proximity operations, propulsion anomalies cascaded into multiple thruster failures and a temporary **loss of six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) control**.
- **Emergency management:** controllers and crew recovered control through "extraordinary professionalism" and by challenging flight rules to achieve docking. Isaacman notes that had docking failed or thrusters not recovered, the outcome could have been catastrophic.
- **Crew return:** the astronauts stayed on the ISS for testing and data review. In September 2024, Starliner undocked autonomously, experienced an unexpected **crew-module propulsion failure**, and landed successfully though "zero fault tolerant" during reentry.
- **Rescue:** Wilmore and Williams returned safely via **SpaceX's Crew-9** in March 2025.

## Part 4 — Root Cause Analysis: Organizational and Technical Failures

- **Three root causes:**
  1. **Insight vs. oversight:** NASA's "limited touch" acquisition/management posture left the agency without the systems knowledge needed to confidently certify a human-rated spacecraft.
  2. **Design approach:** Boeing's propulsion design and certification approach let hardware operate outside qualification limits — incompatible with safety margins.
  3. **Programmatic pressure:** the desire to maintain two dissimilar crew-transport systems influenced and clouded technical/operational risk discussions.
- **Decision fatigue:** pre-launch, more than 30 scheduled CFT launch attempts created cumulative schedule pressure.
- **Cultural mistrust:** witness statements reflected a belief the CCP could only succeed if Starliner launched immediately; on-orbit disagreements led to "unprofessional conduct," and advocacy for program viability persisted over safety.

## Part 5 — Redesignation as a Type A Mishap and Cultural Correction

- **Mishap reclassification:** despite the loss of 6-DOF control and costs exceeding the **Type A mishap** threshold (over $2M in damages) by roughly 100×, a mishap was not initially declared — out of concern for the program's reputation.
- **Correcting the record:** NASA is now formally designating the event a Type A mishap to capture lessons. Isaacman admits the CCP initially "investigated itself," inconsistent with safety culture.
- **Restoring trust:** NASA will not fly another crew on Starliner until technical causes are corrected, the propulsion system is fully qualified, and recommendations are implemented. Isaacman: "transparency is not a weakness; it is a strength."

## Part 6 — Q&A: Program Rationale and Station Retirement (Marcia Dunn, AP)

- **Question:** why continue with Starliner given the ISS is set for decommissioning in ~4 years and the investigation is ongoing?
- **Rationale:** NASA sees "near-endless demand" for crew/cargo access to LEO beyond the ISS; aims to "ignite the orbital economy" (per national space policy), which will need many commercial space stations; the nation benefits from "multiple pathways" (dissimilar systems) to orbit.
- **Boeing commitment:** NASA intends to work with Boeing in coming months to reach the technical root cause of thruster issues and return to crewed flight.

## Part 7 — Q&A: Schedule and "No Earlier Than" Dates (Jeff Foust, SpaceNews)

- **Question:** does the previously mentioned April 2026 cargo-only mission and a later crewed flight still hold?
- **Policy on dates:** a "No Earlier Than" (NET) date is just a date; NASA won't "perpetuate past problems" with target dates it can't meet. NASA will not fly again (crewed or uncrewed) until technical challenges are remediated, risk is fully understood, and report recommendations are implemented.

## Part 8 — Q&A: Defining Leadership Failure and Accountability (Eric Berger, Ars Technica)

- **Question:** how widespread were the failures, and what does "leadership accountability" actually mean?
- **Specific failures (Isaacman):**
  - **Cultural breakdown:** leadership failed to recognize a breakdown in culture/trust between the NASA workforce and contractors.
  - **Programmatic bias:** in deciding how to bring the crew home, "dissimilar crewed access" was repeatedly prioritized over mission and crew safety.
  - **The "single pathway" mistake:** leadership "pretended" alternatives (e.g., a rescue vehicle) didn't exist, focusing on one path that was clearly a mishap.
  - **Extent:** failures existed at "multiple levels," explicitly "right up to the administrator of NASA."

## Part 9 — Q&A: Starliner vs. SLS Development Methodologies (Will Robinson-Smith, Spaceflight Now)

- **Question:** is there leadership overlap/concern between Starliner and the **Space Launch System (SLS)**, both built by Boeing?
- **Technical distinction (Shastri):**
  - **Fixed price vs. classical development:** Commercial Crew runs in a "fixed-price environment" for services, typically with limited insight/oversight.
  - **SLS/core-stage approach:** SLS and Space Station development follow a more "classical" approach where test qualification, fault tolerance, and certification are more strictly aligned with how the hardware is built.
  - **Conclusion:** fewer concerns with SLS because they are "testing like we should," matching the hardware build.

## Part 10 — Q&A: Penalties, Ownership, and Culture (Joey Roulette, Reuters)

- **Question:** are there penalties for Boeing or NASA employees? Have you spoken with Boeing CEO **Kelly Ortberg**?
- **Conversations with Boeing:** Isaacman has spoken with Boeing/Ortberg multiple times; Boeing has taken ownership of its shortcomings and is making "concerted efforts" to adjust its internal culture.
- **NASA internal review:** Isaacman conducted numerous interviews with those involved to ensure witness statements and reports reflected current reality.

## Part 11 — Q&A: Insight vs. Oversight and HLS Application (Kenneth Chang, NYT)

- **Question:** was there an imbalance of oversight, and how does this apply to the **Human Landing System (HLS)**?
- **Risk-based approach (Shastri):** oversight must be tailored to each system's risk — propulsion and life support need more than "spot-level" insight. NASA cannot assume every company/process is symmetrical; each needs an "intensive application" of insight vs. oversight.

## Part 12 — Q&A: SpaceX Oversight and Artemis II Safety (Jackie Wattles, CNN)

- **Question:** is oversight also inadequate for SpaceX? Are Artemis II safety measures sufficient?
- **Distinction:** unlike Starliner (procured as a service), NASA "owns a lot of the design and assembly" of the SLS/Artemis vehicle (traditional procurement).
- **Artemis II confidence:** because it's the most important mission in half a century, Isaacman and Shastri dispatched "second, third, and fourth sets of eyes" down to the component level.
- **Shifting posture:** NASA gave Boeing "the benefit of the doubt" due to past performance — Isaacman admits that was a mistake. He notes almost every NASA partner has asked for _less_ oversight and fewer requirements; Starliner shows why that must sometimes be denied.

## Part 13 — Q&A: Mishap Classification Levels (Richard Tribou, Orlando Sentinel)

- **Question:** what is the gravity of putting Starliner on the level of Challenger and Columbia?
- **Defining "Type A":** human-health consequences or damages over $2M.
- **Comparison examples:** a WB-57 aircraft gear-up landing (no injuries) was a Type A mishap; a ground antenna over-rotation was a Type B mishap.
- **The Starliner case:** Type A because NASA lost 6-DOF control, the crew stayed on orbit well beyond mission design, and two scientific slots on the ISS were lost.
- **Correction (Shastri):** Challenger and Columbia vastly exceeded Type A thresholds (loss of life) and were investigated by **Presidential Commissions** — a higher level than the current independent investigation.

## Part 14 — Q&A: Initial Avoidance of Mishap Status (Mike Wall, Space.com)

- **Question:** how did teams "get away" with not declaring this Type A initially if the cost was so high?
- **Admission:** Isaacman says it was a clear, unambiguous mistake.
- **Hidden reason:** the desire to maintain the "overarching programmatic objective" of dissimilar crew access influenced a decision that should have been purely safety-based.

## Part 15 — Q&A: Danger to the Crew and Thruster Health (Anthony Leone, Spectrum News) [verify reporter name]

- **Question:** how much danger were Wilmore and Williams in?
- **Control crisis:** losing 6-DOF control is a "huge deal"; the crew was only brought to safety because controllers "challenged" flight rules to reach the ISS.
- **Return anomaly:** on autonomous undocking, Starliner suffered an unanticipated **crew-module (CM) thruster failure**, so it reentered "zero fault tolerant." Isaacman: "the spaceship was not healthy."

## Part 16 — Q&A: Private Sector vs. Technical Rigor (Brandon Bigelow, The Register) [verify reporter name]

- **Question:** does prioritizing contractor results suggest the CCP model is a failure or too risky?
- **Defense of the model:**
  - **CCP success:** it returned U.S. human-spaceflight capability after a "dark decade" of dependency on overseas (Russian) transport.
  - **Historical context:** private industry has always been essential — the U.S. didn't reach the Moon without companies like **Boeing and McDonnell Douglas**.
  - **The lesson:** the model is a strength, not a liability, but NASA must restore its "internal expertise" and "core competency" to manage contractors correctly.

## Part 17 — Closing Remarks: The "NASA Family" and Personal Accountability

- **Shastri's tenure:** reflects on 20 years at the agency and how hard it is to hear critiques of its culture.
- **The silence problem:** NASA talks about "speaking up," but people don't always do it; the agency must do better at fostering that environment.
- **Personal anecdotes about the crew:**
  - **Butch Wilmore:** Shastri was robotics lead on **STS-129**, working alongside Wilmore. On Shastri's first shift as a flight director, Wilmore called from the ISS (**Expedition 40**) to joke that "they'll let anybody run this place."
  - **Suni Williams:** Shastri worked with her on **STS-116** (installing trusses); says Williams is a "rock star" in India and often takes selfies to send to his immigrant mother.
- **Final admission:** "We failed them. The agency failed them." Though the crew is safe due to the real-time work of the team, the institutional system failed its responsibility to them.
- **Final message:** success comes through "extreme ownership, immense confidence, and decisive action."
