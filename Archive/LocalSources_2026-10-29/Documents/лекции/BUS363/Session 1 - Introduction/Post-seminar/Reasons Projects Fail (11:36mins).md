## [00:00–01:45] Symptoms vs. Underlying Causes

- **Stagnation of success:** project failure rates have been tracked for ~20 years and aren't improving — if anything, they're rising.
- **The blame game:** people blame specific variables — project managers not controlling scope, poor business cases, estimation approaches, lack of controls, lack of user input.
- **Symptoms vs. disease:** the speaker argues these are merely _symptoms_, not the underlying cause. Fixating on them is like trying to "eradicate smallpox by focusing on the rash it creates."

## [01:45–03:30] The Formula for Failure and Requirements

- **Core concepts:** success is framed as **"Mass"** (critical understanding of what the project is about) × **"Velocity"** (direction and speed toward a target) → **momentum**.
- **The 94% statistic:** of every 100 projects initiated, 94 will "start over again" at least once.
- **The requirement gap:** momentum is killed not by people who don't know what to do with requirements, but by the _inability to get the requirements_ in the first place.
- **The greatest threat:** 70% of failed projects fail because of "poor requirements" — labeled the "single greatest threat to IT productivity improvement."
- **Universal impact:** this happens regardless of methodology — package integration, iterative, waterfall, agile, visualization-centric, or hybrid.

## [03:30–05:15] The Economic Cost of Poor Requirements

- **Intuition vs. data:** everyone intuitively knows good requirements lead to good projects, but the financial impact is stark.
- **Budget doubling:** companies with "poor performance on requirements" spend roughly twice as much as peers. In a cohort that all budgeted ~$3M on average, the ~75% with poor requirements ended up paying ~$6M.
- **Strategic change:** improving requirements isn't "attacking symptoms" — it's "real strategic change."

## [05:15–07:00] Organizational Hurdles and "Stockholm Syndrome"

- **The steering-wheel metaphor:** requirements fail because too many people have "their own opinion" and are all "reaching for the steering wheel" at once.
- **Fear-based culture:** some processes are designed to "jump on failure," like a "tiger hiding in the weeds," so nobody wants to be "separated from the herd."
- **Executive interference:** senior executives may push processes that don't work, even when "no amount of pushing will make that water flow uphill."
- **Requirement "Stockholm Syndrome":** because so few projects are done well, people's only experience is with poorly constructed deliverables and missed objectives. The longer a project is "held hostage by bad requirements," the more the team believes the (wrong) current path is right — until it fails.

## [07:00–09:00] The Skill of the Elite Analyst

- **Complexity misconception:** people underestimate the requirements task, assuming that just getting Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) in a room to talk will solve it.
- **Facilitation as a unique skill:** running intense, efficient meetings of senior managers to reach agreement is a "fairly unique skill" — compared to the difficulty of getting a group of friends to agree on a restaurant.
- **Elite requirements analysts:** possess capabilities others lack — specifically handling massive information overload and facilitating agreement.

## [09:00–10:30] Information Overload and the "Parabolic Explosion"

- **Forest for the trees:** projects start with unorganized information; experienced analysts are like "experienced drivers" who know what to look for, while new drivers get overloaded.
- **The parabolic explosion of detail:** even a simple business function explodes into data:
  - 1 simple business function →
  - ~20 business processes →
  - 5–20 steps per process →
  - many variations per step →
  - one or more business rules per variation, governing "who can do what," "what they can do," and "what they cannot do."
- **Information overload:** most managers and analysts are rarely exposed to the "massive information overload" present at the inception of a large strategic project, which makes "seeing the forest for the trees" a rare, undervalued skill.

## [10:30–11:36] Final Statistics and Momentum

- **The CIO's real problem:** getting requirements right isn't merely technical — it's a business-improvement problem; it's the only way to launch projects and achieve results quickly ("business momentum").
- **IAG "Business Analysis Benchmark" findings:** organizations with poor requirements spend "twice as much time" and "twice as much effort" to reach the same results.
- **Competitive gap:** poor-performing organizations are highly inefficient — expending twice the effort to get half the results vs. competitors with great requirements.
- **Conclusion:** projects fail fundamentally from a "lack of momentum" and a "lack of great quality requirements."
