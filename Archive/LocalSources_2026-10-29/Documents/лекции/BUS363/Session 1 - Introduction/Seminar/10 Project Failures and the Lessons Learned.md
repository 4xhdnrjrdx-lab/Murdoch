## Core premise

Big, well-resourced brands fail at projects too — and because they're high-profile, those failures are well documented, which makes them useful case studies of what _not_ to do. Viewed through a project manager's lens, each failure points to a warning sign you can learn to spot early and avoid in your own projects.

---

## The 10 failed projects (counted down 10 → 1) with the lesson from each

**10. Sony Betamax (mid-1970s).**
Sony's Betamax cassette recorder lost the format war to JVC's VHS, which was cheaper and better; Sony kept producing Betamax tapes all the way until 2016. Betamax was innovative and first to market but still lost.
_Lesson:_ Project management doesn't end at launch. Keep analysing and evaluating a product after release so it can hold its momentum (velocity) and keep delivering value.

**9. New Coke (1985).**
Coca-Cola reformulated its flagship drink after taste-testing ~200,000 people who preferred the new recipe. But brand loyalty and habit meant customers rejected it — costing roughly $4M in development plus a ~$30M loss on unsellable back-stock.
_Lesson:_ Market research alone isn't enough; they misjudged customer _motivations_. You need a way to gather comprehensive, accurate, realistic customer insight, because customer input is essential to development.

**8. Polaroid Polavision / Instant Home Movies.**
A camera that recorded video and developed it in minutes for instant playback — groundbreaking, but limited to ~2.5-minute clips, had no sound, and couldn't play on a normal TV. It lasted only about two years.
_Lesson:_ It was revolutionary but Polaroid failed to keep up with evolving market needs. Keep your finger on the pulse of the market so you can innovate to meet actual demand.

**7. Crystal Pepsi (early 1990s).**
A clear cola that sold well initially on novelty, but interest faded fast and it never captured durable market share.
_Lesson:_ PepsiCo COO David Novak admitted he ignored bottlers who warned the flavour wasn't right. When people raise issues, they may well be correct — listen to internal feedback.

**6. McDonald's Arch Deluxe Burger (1996).**
McDonald's spent over $150M (its biggest-ever ad campaign at the time) on a more "grown-up"/sophisticated burger that customers simply didn't want.
_Lesson:_ Let customer data drive product strategy. A clearer picture of what customers actually wanted would have saved millions. Practical tip: pick a handful of key metrics, track them in as close to real time as possible, and base strategy on the numbers.

**5. Apple Lisa (1983).**
The first desktop with a mouse, but it cost $10,000 (≈$24,000 today) for just 1 MB of RAM. The advertising (featuring Kevin Costner) over-promised and the product under-delivered.
_Lesson:_ Transparency matters. Apple wasn't transparent enough about what the Lisa could really do. In a social-media world you can't overstate a product's capabilities — word of mouth exposes and destroys projects that lack transparency.

**4. Levi's Type 1 Jeans (early 2000s).**
Jeans with exaggerated buttons, stitching and rivets, pushed via a confusing Super Bowl ad. The style never caught on and Levi's abandoned it.
_Lesson:_ Improve internal communication so the final product carries a clear message that end-users understand. Avoid relying on email and spreadsheets; use an operational system of record to communicate, get status updates and track document versions.

**3. IBM PCjr (1983).**
Aimed at home users but offered fewer features than rivals while costing roughly twice as much as an Atari or Commodore. Customers complained about the poor keyboard; IBM's replacement keyboard had its own problems and couldn't revive interest.
_Lesson:_ IBM did the right thing by listening to users, but its response wasn't strong enough because the underlying product was low quality. When you respond to your market — especially in a crisis — the response has to genuinely fix the experience and ideally build loyalty, not just acknowledge the complaint.

**2. DeLorean DMC-12 (early 1980s).**
The famous gull-wing car had production problems from the start; founder John DeLorean was arrested in 1982 on drug-trafficking charges (incurred while trying to raise rescue funds) and, although later found not guilty, it was too late for the car.
_Lesson:_ Framed as a comeback/nostalgia story — a re-work can tap a niche market and bring a project back from the brink (the article notes a planned relaunch of a new DeLorean).

**1. Ford Edsel (1957).**
Ford did extensive market research (even studying the car's "personality"), but by the time the lengthy research was done and the car launched, the market had already shifted toward compact cars — and the Edsel wasn't one.
_Lesson:_ The clearest example of the importance of _speed to market_ and project velocity. Even a major brand fails if a project loses momentum to poor communication, inaccurate deadlines, or out-of-touch project managers. (Quoted PM expert Robert Kelly's point, paraphrased: keep your project schedule reflecting the project's actual, current reality, because things always happen even with good planning.)

## Five ways to spot project failure before it happens

**1. Low interest.**
Warning signs: people stop attending meetings; stakeholders stop participating or giving timely feedback; tasks stop being completed on time.
How to stop it: keep communication current, track all assignments, hold every assignee accountable, and if stakeholders disengage, meet to re-establish the project's perceived value to the organisation.

**2. Poor communication.**
Warning signs: the team doesn't know what's done, not done, or why; the lead isn't relaying changes; messages are late or inaccurate.
How to stop it: email/spreadsheets are fine for basics but are slow and cumbersome for fast-moving teams — use tools (e.g., work-management software) that automate communication as much as possible.

**3. Lack of velocity.**
Warning signs: assignments overdue, stalled waiting on an absent stakeholder's approval; team members increasingly pulled onto other work; the project effectively stops.
How to stop it: enforce accountability — make sure everyone knows their assignments and due dates and push to meet them; chase blocking stakeholders directly to surface issues.

**4. A "no bad news" environment.**
Warning signs: individual status reports are unrealistically rosy and don't match the visible chaos; staff dodge progress questions; leaders are in the dark about why work is late.
How to stop it: let numbers rule — guide the team by a few trackable key metrics (e.g., on-time delivery rate) measured in as close to real time as possible.

**5. Scope creep.**
Warning signs: the project drifts from its original requirements; timelines stretch past projections; "wouldn't it be cool if we also added \_\_\_" appears during review/approval.
How to stop it: run an airtight requirements-gathering process before starting; don't begin until team, stakeholders and requestor all agree; then treat the requirements document like a binding contract.

Overall: the best defence against failure is staying a step ahead of the project with safeguards like these so you can pivot quickly when obstacles arise.

## FAQ takeaways (paraphrased)

- **What is a failed project?** One that doesn't meet its objectives — which includes going over budget, missing deadlines, or losing the support of staff/stakeholders, not just missing the top-line goal. Thorough planning plus monitoring start-to-finish reduces the risk.
- **What can we learn?** The recurring root cause across these cases is poor communication. Automating communication and making sure everyone is aware of a few key metrics keeps morale and visibility high.
- **How to recover?** One failed project doesn't make the company or idea a failure — learn from the mistakes and restart with those changes built in.
