## Part 1 — The O-Ring Flaws and Early Warnings

- **The core failure:** Challenger was destroyed because of a faulty O-ring seal in one of its solid rocket boosters. The failed seal let burning gas escape during liftoff.
- **Acceptance of risk:** During testing, O-rings were found to be burned by escaping gases. Per the **Rogers Commission** reports, engineers at both **NASA** and **Morton Thiokol** (the rocket contractor) deemed the situation "undesirable but acceptable."
- **1977 design omen:** A 1977 test showed rocket ignition caused the steel casing to bend _outward_, reducing pressure on the O-rings — the opposite of the intended _inward_ bend that was supposed to help the O-rings seal.
- **Engineer alarms:**
  - October 1977 — an engineer called the design "unacceptable."
  - January 1978 — another said a redesign was necessary to "prevent hot gas leaks and resulting catastrophic failure."
- **The Columbia precedent:** On the second shuttle mission (November 1981), **Space Shuttle Columbia** was found with a badly eroded primary O-ring in its right booster. No action was taken.
- **Repeated damage:** Multiple missions through the 1980s showed O-ring damage, yet the design stayed unchanged.

## Part 2 — The Temperature Problem and Launch Delays

- **Cold-weather concern:** By 1985, Morton Thiokol engineers realized cold weather could reduce the elasticity of the O-rings, which was critical to sealing.
- **The "Help" memo:** In October 1985, engineer **Bob Ebeling** [corrected from "Ebling"] wrote a memo titled "Help" to flag the issue; it got no response.
- **The 18-degree question:** On January 27, 1986, NASA asked Morton Thiokol about launching in 18°F weather. The O-rings' lower safety threshold was 40°F.
- **The engineering recommendation:** Bob Ebeling and his team unanimously believed a launch at that temperature would kill the crew. In a teleconference they recommended NASA not launch below 53°F.
- **NASA's pressure:** Engineer **Roger Boisjoly** [corrected from "Beaujolais"] (later recounting to NPR) said a NASA official was "appalled" at the idea of waiting for warmer weather.
- **Ice on the pad:** Despite ice forming across the launch pad, NASA chose to proceed. The crew was briefed on the ice but never told about the O-ring temperature risk.
- **Chronology of delays:**
  1. **Jan 22 → Jan 23:** delayed by schedule ripples from mission **STS-61C**.
  2. **Jan 25:** moved by the **Program Requirements Change Board**.
  3. **Jan 26:** bumped again due to STS-61C delays.
  4. **The "Sunday" mistake:** pushed from Jan 26 on a forecast of wind and rain. Launch Director **Gene Thomas** later noted the forecast was wrong and Sunday was actually a "beautiful day" — a missed opportunity.
  5. **Jan 27:** a screw on the crew hatch wouldn't release; a drill brought in had a dead battery, and nine replacement batteries also died for unknown reasons. Liftoff was pushed to Jan 28.

## Part 3 — Launch and Breakup

- **Liftoff:** 11:38 a.m., January 28, 1986, from **Kennedy Space Center**.
- **Early signs:** footage showed dark smoke jetting from the right-side SRB O-ring less than a second after liftoff.
- **40 seconds:** the shuttle broke the sound barrier.
- **59 seconds:** a plume of flame began emitting from the right-hand SRB.
- **60 seconds:** the flame contacted the external fuel tank; the crew, heard saying "woohoo," was unaware of the danger.
- **73 seconds:** last words from pilot **Mike Smith** — "Uh-oh." The shuttle was torn apart as the external fuel tank erupted.
- **The parachute misconception:** TV coverage fixated on an object descending by parachute, but it was only the nose cap of an SRB. There was no crew escape system.

## Part 4 — The Missing Escape Systems

- **Historical context:** Space Shuttle Columbia originally had ejection seats (similar to the **SR-71 Blackbird**) for its first four test flights, but only for two-person crews.
- **1971 design decisions:** NASA considered three escape options:
  1. Conventional ejection seats — $10 million.
  2. Encapsulated seats — $7 million.
  3. Detachable crew compartment — $292 million.
- **Rejection:** all options were rejected for cost and implementation difficulty — both before and after the Challenger disaster.

## Part 5 — Recovery of the Crew

- **Discovery:** recovery began within an hour. In March 1986, two divers found the crew cabin about 100 feet below the surface off the Florida coast.
- **The scene:** the cabin was a wreck of twisted metal and wires; divers identified it after spotting a spacesuit bobbing in the water.
- **The recovery process** (handled by the **USS Preserver**):
  1. **Judith Resnik** [corrected from "Judy Resnick"] was recovered first.
  2. **Christa McAuliffe** was recovered second.
  3. **Gregory Jarvis:** as the cabin was being raised by crane, Jarvis's body came free, surfaced, then sank again.
- **The search for Jarvis:** astronaut **Robert Crippen** hired his own boat to help; Jarvis was found five weeks later, about 200 yards from where he was lost.

## Part 6 — Aftermath and the Question of Consciousness

- **Engineer guilt:** Roger Boisjoly and Bob Ebeling both felt responsible. Boisjoly suffered headaches, insomnia, and depression; he unsuccessfully sued NASA and Morton Thiokol and was shunned by some colleagues.
- **Boisjoly's legacy:** he eventually found peace lecturing at engineering schools about the disaster until his death in January 2012.
- **The Kerwin report:** NASA scientist **Joseph P. Kerwin** reported the breakup likely did not kill or seriously injure the crew immediately, because the cabin stayed intact.
- **Evidence of consciousness:**
  - The cabin fell for roughly two minutes before impact.
  - **PEAPs (Personal Egress Air Packs):** four were recovered; three had been manually activated.
  - **Expert opinion:** three-time shuttle commander **Robert Overmyer**, who helped with recovery, was certain the crew was conscious.
- **Final impact:** the cabin struck the ocean at about **207 miles per hour**, leaving no chance of survival.
