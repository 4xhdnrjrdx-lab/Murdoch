## Framing: Silicon Valley and its icons

The piece opens by arguing that Silicon Valley depends on iconic founder-figures — it names Steve Jobs, Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk as stars who attract investment, inspire careers, and sell the idea that every problem can be solved with smarter/faster technology. Elizabeth Holmes was positioned to become exactly that kind of icon.

## The rise of Theranos

- From roughly **2013 to 2015**, Elizabeth Holmes was a media favourite, appearing on the covers of _Fortune_, _Forbes_, _Inc._ and the _New York Times Style Magazine_.
- Her startup **Theranos** (a blend of "therapy" + "diagnosis") aimed to disrupt blood testing — an industry generating over **$73 billion** in annual revenue.
- The pitch: eliminate needles, replace them with near-painless finger-prick samples, and run tests using only **1/100th to 1/1000th** of the blood that conventional labs require.
- Holmes dropped out of **Stanford at 19** to found the company. The diagnostic-lab industry she targeted performs about **10 billion tests a year** and underpins roughly **70% of doctors' medical decisions**.
- Target customers included infants, the elderly, and patients on anticoagulants.

**Valuation and wealth:**

- In **2014**, Theranos was valued at **$9 billion** — the essay compares this to the entire GDP of the Bahamas (a nation of ~400,000 people), despite Theranos having no viable product.
- That same year Holmes topped the **Forbes list of America's Richest Self-Made Women** with an estimated net worth of **$4.5 billion**.
- By the time of writing, ~**91%** of the company's valuation had evaporated and Holmes's net worth was estimated at **zero**.

**Marketing, funding and partnerships:**

- The company raised about **$400 million** from venture capital and priced its tests at **less than half** the cost of traditional labs.
- Holmes branded the small blood-storage vial the **"nanotainer,"** making it the symbol of painless, efficient care.
- Theranos partnered with **Walgreens**, planning to install testing outposts in many of Walgreens' ~**8,200** US locations, with the stated aspiration of putting a Theranos centre **within five miles of every American**.

**The "all-star board" (cult of credibility):**

- The board included former Secretary of Defense **William Perry** and former Secretaries of State **Henry Kissinger** and **George Shultz**.
- George Shultz's grandson, **Tyler Shultz**, a Stanford alum, worked at Theranos — and later became a key whistleblower.

**The cult of personality:**

- Holmes deliberately echoed Steve Jobs (black turtlenecks, strong vision, tight control), to the point that the line between her and the company blurred — "Theranos became Holmes."
- She was the self-made female founder Silicon Valley wanted amid concerns about gender bias in tech.
- Key media profiles cited: **Roger Parloff's** June 2014 _Fortune_ cover story "This CEO is Out for Blood" (~5,000-word, highly admiring); Holmes's Stanford chemical-engineering professor **Channing Robertson** (who joined Theranos in 2009 and said he gave up two endowed chairs); and **Ken Auletta's** 2014 _New Yorker_ profile ("Blood, Simpler") which painted Holmes as a brilliant, unconventional loner who read _Moby-Dick_ at nine and designed time machines.

## The downfall

- In early **2015**, _Wall Street Journal_ reporter **John Carreyrou** received a tip alleging fraud and mismanagement — the source turned out to be **Tyler Shultz**.
- Carreyrou (a Pulitzer winner for investigative reporting into US healthcare) was skeptical: he doubted a college dropout could do advanced biomedical research, and noticed Holmes's public explanations of the technology were vague/oversimplified (she avoided peer review by claiming validation studies were "unnecessary and inappropriate" because the tests used the same fundamental chemistry as conventional labs).
- Investigation was hard: every employee had signed an **NDA**; sources were threatened and smeared as "disgruntled employees"; Tyler Shultz said he was followed by private investigators hired by Theranos.

**The exposé:**

- After ~**ten months**, Carreyrou published in **October 2015**: "Hot Startup Theranos Has Struggled With Its Blood-Test Technology."
- Core revelations:
  - Theranos's flagship device, the **Edison**, was used for **less than 10%** of the tests the company ran.
  - **Most tests were actually run on third-party analyzers** (e.g. bought from **Siemens AG**).
  - The Edison was **unreliable** — proficiency-testing samples split between the Edison and third-party machines gave different results.
  - President/COO **Sunny Balwani** instructed staff to report only the third-party results for proficiency testing — a violation of federal protocol.
  - A regulatory loophole let Theranos avoid FDA scrutiny: tests using outside vendors' equipment need FDA approval, but **lab-developed tests (LDTs)** run on a lab's own equipment do not — Theranos exploited this.
  - Despite the "pin prick" marketing, samples often had to be **diluted** to work in standard machines, and patients were frequently asked to give blood the traditional way (**venipuncture** — a syringe in the arm).
- In December 2015, **Parloff published a correction** in _Fortune_ ("How Theranos Misled Me"), admitting the venipuncture practices had been explained away to him as coping with volume for a "rapidly scaling" company, and that further questions were blocked as "trade secret" territory.

**The domino effect:**

- Theranos used **shell companies** to buy commercial lab equipment secretly.
- **Walgreens sued for $140 million** (later settled out of court for under $30 million).
- Theranos closed its clinical labs and Wellness Centers.
- To deter investor lawsuits, Holmes offered investors more shares in exchange for waiving legal claims.
- The _Washington Post_ reported Theranos hired corporate-research firm **Fusion GPS** (the same firm behind the 2016 "Trump dossier") to soften aggressive reporting; the firm allegedly contacted Carreyrou to urge a less aggressive approach.
- **Oct 15, 2015:** Theranos issued a press statement dismissing Carreyrou's sources and framing the criticism as the work of "entrenched interests" resisting change — the essay calls this classic Silicon Valley "savior syndrome."
- **July 2016:** Holmes was banned from owning or operating a medical laboratory for at least two years.

## How it happened: the "Fourth Estate" (the media's role)

This is the essay's central argument — the media both **enabled** and **eventually exposed** Theranos.

**Why the warning signs were ignored:**

- Even Parloff's 2014 piece noted oddities (opaque answers, extreme secrecy, skeptical doctors). He compared Theranos's end-to-end model to "FedEx trying to manufacture all its own airplanes and trucks" — yet confidence stayed high because **investors trusted Holmes**.
- Kissinger described Holmes as having iron will and determination "with no performance"; William Perry (via Auletta) called her "another Steve Jobs" with a bigger heart and social conscience.
- **Fear of missing out** on the next big thing made investors and journalists want to believe her.

**Structural media failures the essay identifies:**

1. **The cult of personality / demand for a female icon.** Silicon Valley craved a female founder-billionaire. _Inc._'s Oct 2015 cover ("Why the Next Steve Jobs Will Be a Woman," by Kimberly Weisul) featured Holmes in a Jobs-style turtleneck. The author argues celebrating progress is good, but the media celebrated her **unquestioningly**. (Note: Sandberg and Mayer were prominent women in tech but hadn't founded their own companies — Holmes had.)
2. **Media silos / wrong beat.** Theranos was covered by **tech reporters**, not **healthcare reporters**. Tech reporters were used to secrecy around billion-dollar "simple ideas" and accepted Holmes's opacity; a healthcare reporter (as Carreyrou was) would have caught the weak grasp of diagnostics she showed in interviews.
3. **Journalism as a game of access.** In the Valley, outlets that write negatively risk losing pre-release access and CEO interviews. **Nick Bilton** (Vanity Fair) is cited describing how the system is "molded to prevent reporters from asking tough questions." This deterred critical coverage.
4. **Private-company opacity.** Running on VC money, Theranos wasn't accountable to public shareholders. Its board was a "token" one where Holmes held near-majority voting control and members lacked tech/healthcare expertise. Carreyrou contrasts this with Enron, which his paper broke in 2000 and which went bankrupt within six weeks — whereas Theranos hadn't even launched an internal investigation.

- After the collapse, outlets that had praised Holmes were reluctant to discuss it (e.g. _Inc._ declined to comment citing litigation; TechCrunch didn't respond). The essay notes TechCrunch's Jon Shieber even had his blood drawn on stage as a PR stunt for Theranos.

**Lesson for journalists:** always second-guess the wisdom of the crowd; don't accept answers just because everyone else does; celebrate symbols of progress without abandoning scrutiny.

## The future (as of late 2017)

- Theranos "pivoted" to the **"MiniLab,"** essentially a repackaged Edison; its website carried disclaimers that the technology wasn't FDA-cleared and wasn't for sale in the US.
- Per the WSJ, the company held **less than $180 million** of the **$700 million** it had raised.
- **July 2017:** Theranos put its Palo Alto headquarters up for rent amid mounting legal costs and a collapsing valuation.

**Broader pattern — hubris in startup culture:**

- The essay generalises: hubris and unrealistic expectations increasingly plague startups. It cites **Outcome Health** (Chicago healthcare-advertising startup) which raised **$500 million** from **Goldman Sachs** and **Alphabet** in May 2017, then faced accusations in Nov 2017 of misleading investors.
- Carreyrou: "There is a hubris in Silicon Valley that has reached ridiculous levels" — founders who think making money makes them "godlike." He calls for a "reset."
- The hoped-for reset comes from **better media** — e.g. **The Information** (founded by ex-WSJ reporter Jessica Lessin), a subscriber-based outlet aiming to restore old-school journalistic integrity.
- Closing argument (via Bilton): the Valley functions as "one big confidence game" where founders, VCs and tech media pretend to vet each other while actually questioning nothing.
