## Part 1 — Background and entry into Theranos

- **Tyler Shultz** started at **Theranos** as an intern between his junior and senior years at **Stanford**.
- A **biology major** (switched from engineering), drawn in by the company's mission.
- Personal ties mattered: founder/CEO **Elizabeth Holmes** was a family friend, and Tyler's grandfather, **George Shultz** (former US Secretary of State), sat on the board. After graduating, Tyler joined full-time.

## Part 2 — Technical red flags and the "tiled world"

- Tyler describes a divide between the **"carpeted world"** (executives, product managers) and the **"tiled world"** (labs and the Theranos devices).
- First red flag: the device couldn't run the **hundreds of tests** claimed — it could barely run two at once, and during his time reached only **seven total**; running those seven simultaneously would have needed **seven separate machines**.
- In the lab he saw **high result variation** and a culture of **"picking and choosing"** which experiments to report — deleting parts of experiments and repeating them to swap out unfavourable data.
- **Quality-control (QC) failures** were frequent and statistically improbable; staff were pressured to run **patient samples** even when a test was malfunctioning and QC had failed.
- During a **CLIA inspection**, the room with the Theranos devices was **locked**, so inspectors only saw third-party **Siemens** equipment.
- In one case a split sample run on both the Theranos device and Siemens showed discrepancies of **over 300%** — yet the company reported the **Siemens** results while claiming they came from **Theranos** technology.

## Part 3 — Internal confrontations and hostility

- Tyler challenged Holmes on the website's claim of a **coefficient of variation (CV) < 10%**, which he never saw in practice. Holmes first denied the claim, then (seeing it on the homepage) said it applied only to **Vitamin D**, not the whole system.
- Told the "**faster, cheaper, more accurate**" claim was false, he was told the company never claimed "more accurate" — so he produced **multiple articles** where exactly that was claimed.
- A VP suggested Holmes just tends to **"exaggerate in interview settings,"** and asked him to put concerns in an email.
- President **Sunny Balwani** replied with hostility — calling Tyler **"arrogant, ignorant, patronizing, and reckless,"** and saying that with any other last name he'd already have been **held accountable to the strongest extent**.

## Part 4 — The whistleblowing process

- Tyler **resigned after eight months, age 23**. His parents urged him to say he was leaving to pursue a **PhD** to avoid "making waves."
- **John Carreyrou** (Wall Street Journal) reached him via **LinkedIn**; Tyler eventually replied using a **burner phone** and became a central **anonymous source**, gaining trust after researching Carreyrou's track record.
- Even after articles ran, Holmes kept attending **Shultz family functions**.
- Tyler eventually **outed himself** publicly — feeling vindicated by government reports and disrespected by continued intimidation.
- He notes colleague **Erika Cheung**, who also spoke to Carreyrou/regulators but moved to **Hong Kong** to avoid legal harassment (a path Tyler jokes he should have considered).

## Part 5 — Legal harassment and financial cost

- Theranos hired **private investigators** to follow Tyler for **two years**.
- The legal team, led by **David Boies**, used bullying tactics to push him to sign **affidavits** and name other whistleblowers.
- His family spent **~$400,000** on legal fees. Tyler saw money purely as **"runway"** to keep fighting.
- His analogy: the legal system is "one-on-one basketball" where the wealthy hire **"LeBron James"** (elite lawyers) to crush an opponent who can only afford a "high-school player."

## Part 6 — Family tensions and resolution

- **George Shultz** initially didn't believe his grandson.
- At a pivotal moment, Tyler went to his grandfather's house to talk privately and was instead met by **two lawyers** with a **temporary restraining order** and a letter from **David Boies**.
- George Shultz acknowledged the fraud only **after the SEC findings**, telling Tyler he was **proud** of him for standing up for his beliefs.
- Tyler credits his **moral compass** to his parents (a childhood memory of them helping him learn Japanese to befriend a classmate).

## Part 7 — Aftermath and insights

- **Theranos ceased operations in August 2018** and was dissolved. Holmes and Balwani were **charged with massive fraud by the SEC** and **indicted for wire fraud and conspiracy**.
- Contrary to warnings that whistleblowing would end his career, Tyler received **many job offers** from biotech and VC firms.
- He is **CEO/co-founder of Flux Biosciences** (home medical diagnostics) — _as of the talk_.
- **Leadership takeaways:** **trust the experts you hire**, and clearly separate a long-term **"vision"** from **current technical reality**. He notes the **"trade secrets"** excuse was Holmes's standard shield against hard technical questions.
