## The case in brief

- **Elizabeth Holmes** founded **Theranos** at age **19 in 2003**, promising to disrupt healthcare with a blood-testing device she intended to invent.
- She was **indicted for fraud in June 2018**, alongside **Sunny Balwani** (former Theranos COO and her former boyfriend); the federal investigation was ongoing at time of writing. **Theranos was dissolved in September 2018.** If convicted, Holmes faced **up to 20 years in prison**.
- The exposé came from **John Carreyrou** (Wall Street Journal), whose book **_Bad Blood: Secrets and Lies in a Silicon Valley Startup_** is the basis for the article (and a film with Jennifer Lawrence cast as Holmes).
- The columnist frames Holmes' core failure (paraphrasing MarketWatch's Francine McKenna) as **touting financial projections that never materialised, built on technology she never delivered.**

**Central governance question — "Where was the board?"** — is complicated in a privately held company where the **founder is simultaneously CEO, has close personal ties to board members, seizes control of voting rights, and becomes chairman.** The article's thesis: the board was **set up to fail** by a lack of independence and by the board's structure; the members functioned as **advisors who trusted but didn't verify**, with **no genuine independent fiduciary role** in place. The board fell for **the myth of the wunderkind.**

## The "wunderkind myth" and the halo effect

- **Channing Robertson** — Theranos' first board member, Emeritus Professor of Chemical Engineering at **Stanford**, taught Holmes before she dropped out. He likened her abilities to looking into the eyes of **Steve Jobs**.
- Former board chair **Donald Lucas, Sr.** (venture capitalist) and others saw the same Jobs parallels — a comparison **Holmes cultivated** in her dress and mimicked behaviour.
- Media hype escalated in **2011** when Holmes recruited **George P. Shultz** (economist, four-time US presidential cabinet member) to the board; he then recruited fellow elder statesmen and military/business leaders.
- **Fortune (2014)** called it possibly the most illustrious board in US corporate history (in terms of public service).
- **Between 2013 and 2015 the board raised more than $700 million** from investors (including themselves).
- Board members saw a brilliant, 24/7-focused young woman; they were **protective, loyal and trusting** — unclear whether captivated by her vision, the potential ROI, or both. **Shultz** was especially loyal, **siding with Holmes against his own grandson, Tyler Shultz, who was a whistle-blower**; he believed her healthcare vision aligned with his own sense of doing good.
- **Richard Kovacevich** (former Wells Fargo CEO, board member) said in a deposition he invested because a cheaper, faster, less painful blood test would win market share and make his investment very valuable.

**The danger:** believing a CEO is a wunderkind **gives credit before it's earned**, creating false confidence that overlooks inevitable human weaknesses that can derail a company.

### The Steve Jobs comparison was misguided

The article cites a Forbes piece (_"The Theranos Scandal: What Happens When You Misunderstand Steve Jobs"_) listing **five critical differences** between Jobs and Holmes:

1. Jobs had **other people run Apple**.
2. Jobs **teamed with a technical wizard** (i.e. Wozniak).
3. Jobs **didn't try to invent what hadn't yet been invented**.
4. Jobs **wanted to delight customers**.
5. **Secrecy wasn't what drove** Jobs' success.

## Board structure and the "velvet glove"

Holmes' board had an **"amoeba-like, shape-shifting" capacity** engineered to serve her goals:

- **2007 — forcing out a challenger:** Holmes asked former chair Lucas to obtain the resignation of board member **Avie Tevanian** (a former Apple executive) who was **challenging her pattern of rosy revenue projections** based on deals that never materialised. **Tevanian resigned.**
- **2008 — the near-removal:** the new head of sales & marketing, with the general counsel, raised the same concerns with Lucas. Lucas called an **emergency board meeting**; the board **decided to remove Holmes as CEO** (too young and inexperienced). Holmes responded with **contrition** — promising transparency and change. After about **two hours**, her **charm and contrition persuaded the board to let her stay CEO.** Weeks later, she **fired the head of sales & marketing and the general counsel.**
- **Late 2015 — prestige without power:** valuation reached nearly **$9 billion**, but board members were **advisors without fiduciary responsibility** — connections, not relevant expertise.
- **2011 — the board as "placeholder":** a job applicant recalled Holmes saying the board's role was a placeholder and **"I make all the decisions here."**
- **2013 — the voting takeover:** Holmes forced through a resolution giving **100 votes to every share she owned → 99.7% of voting rights**; thereafter the board **couldn't reach a quorum without her.**
- **October 2015 →:** the first WSJ articles challenged Theranos' technology. **By mid-2016 the advisory board was replaced by a fiduciary board; by 2017 scientific/medical/technical boards were created.** It emerged that **no audited financial statements had been prepared since 2009.**

## "Where was the board?" — the oversight failures

- The early years (a CEO in her twenties with little/no relevant leadership training) were exactly when **oversight mattered most** — and was absent.
- Holmes hired very talented people (from Apple and elsewhere) but **fired anyone who showed concern or resistance as "disloyal" or "not a team player."** **Turnover was very high** (Tevanian had flagged this too).
- Unanswered governance questions the article poses: Why didn't other members challenge her? When she got a **second chance** as CEO, why didn't the board **make leadership coaching / independent expert support a condition**? What follow-up ensured the **financial projections were accurate**? (The indictment indicates **misrepresentation continued.**) **Where was her accountability and verified proof of change?**
- **The patient-safety harm:** in her **2014 TEDMED talk** Holmes spoke of giving everyone access to actionable health information early enough to matter. But by allowing **unreliable analysers to run real blood tests commercially**, she **jeopardised patient health and safety** and undermined doctors' ability to diagnose accurately.
- **Diagnosis of the leadership style:** an **"end justifies the means"** approach that backfired. She charted her own course — as she told the 2011 applicant, **"I make all the decisions here."**

## Key terms & references

**Concepts / lessons:**

- **Wunderkind myth / halo effect** — granting credit (and trust) before it is earned, masking weaknesses.
- **"Velvet glove" governance** — a charismatic founder neutralising dissent through charm, contrition, and removal of challengers.
- **Advisory vs fiduciary board** — prestige and connections ≠ independent oversight or relevant expertise.
- **Founder control mechanisms** — dual-class / super-voting shares (100 votes/share → 99.7% control); founder as CEO + chairman; quorum dependence.
- **"End justifies the means" leadership** — and its backfire.
- **Trust but verify** failure — board "trusted but didn't verify."
- **Patient safety / ethics** — commercial use of unreliable technology.

**Key figures & dates:**

- Founded 2003 (Holmes aged 19); indicted June 2018; dissolved Sept 2018; up to 20 years prison.
- Board raised **>$700M (2013–2015)**; valuation **~$9B (late 2015)**; **99.7% voting control (2013)**; **no audited accounts since 2009**.

**People:** Elizabeth Holmes; Sunny Balwani (ex-COO); John Carreyrou (WSJ, _Bad Blood_); board members Channing Robertson (Stanford), Donald Lucas Sr. (VC, ex-chair), George P. Shultz, Richard Kovacevich (ex-Wells Fargo CEO), Avie Tevanian (ex-Apple, the challenger who resigned); Tyler Shultz (whistle-blower grandson); Francine McKenna (MarketWatch).

## Relevance to BUS363

This is the **applied case study to the Sadler-Smith et al. (2016) hubris theory** — pair them directly for **Session 11 (Agile Leadership in a VUCA world)** and for any leadership/ethics argument in the assessments.

- **Hubris in practice:** Holmes maps onto the hubris construct almost point-for-point — over-confidence/over-ambition, contempt for advice (firing challengers), identification of self _with_ the organisation, "I make all the decisions," and weak governance amplifying it (exactly Hayward & Hambrick's "weak board vigilance + combined CEO/chair roles"). Use it as the concrete example when explaining hubris/Hubris Syndrome.
- **Governance as the mitigation that failed:** Sadler-Smith argues strong, independent boards (outside directors, split CEO/chair, deferring to expertise) mitigate hubris. Theranos shows the **inverse** — an advisory board with no fiduciary teeth, no relevant expertise, and 99.7% founder voting control. Strong material for an "agile/responsible leadership vs hubristic leadership" contrast.
- **A3 (Team Presentation — scaling globally, 30%):** a cautionary anchor for the _risks_ of fast scaling — over-promising on undelivered technology, scaling commercialisation before the product works (patient safety), and culture of fear/high turnover. Good counterweight to the "growth at all costs" framing.
- **A2 (PM concepts, 35%):** illustrates the cost of **no verification / no accountability** — no audited financials since 2009, projections never tested. Use under risk management, governance, and stakeholder accountability.
- **Ties to Brett (2006) & culture sessions:** the "fire anyone who challenges me" culture is the opposite of teams that can challenge authority and self-correct (cf. HRO crews failing to challenge the captain in Sadler-Smith).
