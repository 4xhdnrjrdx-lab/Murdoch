## Abstract / core definition

**Hubristic leaders significantly over-estimate their own abilities** and believe their performance is superior to others', leading to **over-confident and over-ambitious judgements and decisions**. The problem is compounded because they are **resistant to criticism** and **contemptuous of, and invulnerable to, others' advice**. The review covers hubristic leadership from two standpoints:

1. **Psychological / behavioural** — hubris as over-confidence and its links to **core self-evaluation (CSE)** and **narcissism**.
2. **Psychiatric** — hubris as an **acquired disorder** ("Hubris Syndrome") whose onset is tied to **acquiring significant power**.

It draws distinctions between hubris and related constructs (over-confidence, narcissism, CSE, pride) and reviews how hubris can be recognised, diagnosed and researched.

**Working definition of hubris:** a dangerous cocktail of **over-confidence, over-ambition, arrogance and pride**, allied to **contempt for advice/criticism**, **catalysed by power** and **fuelled by past success** — a "malaise of the powerful and successful" that causes leaders to over-reach. Left unchecked it can destroy careers, wreck organisations, and damage whole industries/societies.

**Keywords:** core self-evaluation, hubris, Hubris Syndrome, leadership, narcissism, over-confidence, pride.

## Background — the classical frame

- **Hubris ↔ Nemesis:** in Greek myth, Nemesis was the goddess of retribution against mortals who assumed god-like powers. Hubris is associated with a **moment of failure** when leaders take over-ambitious decisions while disregarding contrary advice.
- **Allegory:** the **Icarus and Dædalus** myth (Ovid's _Metamorphoses_) — flying too high, the sun melts the wax, Icarus falls to his "self-inflicted doom."
- **Historical/political examples:** Napoleon's retreat from Moscow; Margaret Thatcher's post-Falklands hubris ending her premiership; **George W. Bush & Tony Blair's** "hubristic alliance" → 2003 Iraq invasion (Bush's "Mission Accomplished" speech on USS _Abraham Lincoln_). The Watson Institute (Brown University) estimated the Iraq mission cost **$1.7 trillion** and **134,000 Iraqi civilian deaths**.
- **Business examples:** the collapses of **Lehman Brothers** and **Royal Bank of Scotland (RBS)**; "corporate hubris" implicated in the **Columbia space shuttle disaster** (NASA, 7 crew died) and the **Deepwater Horizon** oil spill (BP, est. **$62bn**). The review focuses on **individual-level** hubris but flags corporate hubris as needing development.

## Perspective 1 — Hubris and over-confidence (psychological/behavioural)

### The "hubris hypothesis" (Roll, 1986)

- The first prominent management reference. **Roll (1986)** explained why some CEOs acquire firms despite evidence the deal won't pay off: **over-confidence** (a cognitive bias). CEO over-confidence → upwardly biased valuations of a takeover's value → **larger premiums paid** for acquisitions. A large body of behavioural-finance work broadly supports it.
- **Limitation:** the hypothesis struggles with cases where hubristic CEOs' M&As actually _gained_ (e.g. Lehman's Richard Fuld — his hubris may even have helped stabilise/grow the firm after the 1994 demerger from American Express, _before_ nemesis struck). Hubris is more complex than over-confidence alone.

### Hayward & Hambrick (1997) — broadening it

- Defined hubris as **"exaggerated pride or confidence often resulting in retribution."**
- Studied **three sources of CEO hubris** → acquisition premiums:
  1. Recent organisational **success**
  2. **Media praise** for the CEO
  3. CEO **self-importance**
- These effects were **amplified by weak governance** — more **insider-directors** on the board and **CEO + chairman roles combined**.
- **Mitigation:** more **outside directors**; **separate** the CEO and chairman roles.

### Over-confidence as more than a proxy

- Over-confidence = cognitive bias toward over-estimating positive future outcomes + untenable faith in one's ability. Often used as a **proxy** for hubris, but hubris is **distinct from / more than** over-confidence.
- **Picone et al. (2014)** — three manifestations of hubris: **over-estimation** of abilities/success probability; **over-precision** in one's beliefs; **over-placement** of one's performance relative to others (see "better-than-average effect").
- **Intuition risk:** hubristic CEOs over-rely on **intuition rooted in past success**. Useful in high-velocity environments, but combined with over-estimation it reduces attention to strategy and detail → **misguided diversification, over-ambitious internationalisation, over-reliance on acquisition-led growth, excessive debt**. Bush's Iraq "instinct/God gave me a mission" reasoning = Blaug's "pathology of power" (a vicious circle of cognitive simplification replacing effortful analysis).

### Hubris, CSE and narcissism

- **Better-than-average effect** (Alicke et al. 1995): most people rate themselves above average (e.g. driving). Drives **self-serving attribution** (success = me; failure = bad luck). CEOs compare themselves to the _average manager_, not the _average CEO_ → **base-rate neglect**, amplified in top management teams (TMTs) where few comparators exist.
- **Hyper Core Self-Evaluation (CSE):** said to correspond closely to hubris (Hiller & Hambrick 2005). CSE has four components: **self-efficacy**, **locus of control**, **emotional stability**, **self-esteem**. Up to a point CSE is beneficial (propels action, motivates others); **beyond a tipping point** it brings naïve/foolish behaviour. Hyper-CSE relates to _healthy/optimal_ narcissism, not _destructive/reactive_ narcissism.
- **Narcissism vs hubris:** narcissism is "at its most basic self-love" — a **relatively stable individual difference** (grandiosity, attention-seeking, lack of empathy). Hubris is distinguished by a **trajectory** of rise → retribution, and by being **acquired** (tied to power) rather than stable. They can be related (an unhealthy narcissistic personality may predispose toward Hubris Syndrome) but are **not equivalent**. Hubris ≠ simply "love-of-power" vs narcissism's "love-of-self."
- Hubris can be **acquired, succumbed to, or shed** at particular career stages (e.g. FDR became hubristic after the death of his restraining adviser Louis Howe; Owen sees it as an "intoxication of power" that may **abate once power is lost**).

### Table 1 — Distinctions between hubris and related constructs

| Construct                            | Description                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hyper core self-evaluation (CSE)** | Excessive self-efficacy, locus of control, emotional stability, self-esteem (Hiller & Hambrick 2005)                                                                                                            |
| **Hubris**                           | Over-estimation of one's abilities → over-confident, over-ambitious judgement/decisions; associated with the **acquisition of significant power**; invulnerable to and contemptuous of others' advice/criticism |

| **Pride**                            | _Authentic pride_ = positive emotion from recognising one's real contribution. _Hubristic pride_ = negative emotion of arrogance, conceit, self-aggrandisement (Tracy & Robins 2014)                            |

## Perspective 2 — Hubris as an acquired disorder ("Hubris Syndrome")

- **Owen & Davidson (2009)** studied British PMs and US Presidents over ~100 years and proposed **Hubris Syndrome**: an **acquired disorder** characterised by recklessness, contempt, and inattention to detail, tied to holding **power under largely unfettered discretion**, usually preceded by **overwhelming success** — at which point adaptive confidence/ambition tips into maladaptive over-confidence/over-ambition.
- **Diagnosis rule:** a person can be classed as having Hubris Syndrome if **≥3 of the 14 symptoms** are shown continuously, with **at least one being a _unique_ symptom**, AND no other psychiatric condition (depression, organic brain disease, bipolar, substance abuse) explains the behaviour.
- It overlaps with **DSM-V Cluster B** personality disorders (Narcissistic, Antisocial, Histrionic) but differs crucially: Cluster B disorders begin in childhood/adolescence; **Hubris Syndrome arises in adulthood only after holding power** — "an illness of position as much as of the person." So it is **not** just a subtype of Narcissistic Personality Disorder.
- **Trigger analogy:** like PTSD is triggered by trauma exposure, Hubris Syndrome is triggered by **exposure to power**. If power is **removed, the syndrome abates**.
- **Risk factors (Ghaemi et al. 2016):** lack of realism; lack of empathy; mania/bipolar/hyperthymic temperament; narcissistic/antisocial/histrionic personality; drugs (amphetamines, corticosteroids); **male gender**; PTSD; anxiety; psychotic conditions. Even mild **positive illusion** (normal but illusory sense of control/optimism) under power can predispose to hubris.

### Table 2 — The 14 symptoms of Hubris Syndrome (Owen & Davidson 2009)

_(symptoms marked ▲ are unique to the condition)_

1. A narcissistic propensity to see the world mainly as an arena to exercise power and seek glory.
2. A predisposition to take actions that cast oneself in a good light (image enhancement).
3. A disproportionate concern with image and presentation.
4. A messianic manner of talking about current activities; tendency to exaltation.
5. ▲ Identification with the nation/organisation to the point of treating one's own outlook and interests as identical with it.
6. ▲ A tendency to speak in the third person or use the royal "we."
7. Excessive confidence in one's own judgement and contempt for others' advice/criticism.
8. Exaggerated self-belief, bordering on omnipotence, in what one can personally achieve.
9. A belief that one is accountable not to colleagues/public opinion but to **history or God**.
10. ▲ An unshakable belief that one will be vindicated in that court.
11. Loss of contact with reality; often progressive isolation.
12. ▲ Restlessness, recklessness, impulsiveness.
13. ▲ Allowing a "broad vision" / moral rectitude of a course to override practicality, cost or outcomes.
14. Hubristic incompetence — things go wrong because over-confidence led the leader to ignore the "nuts and bolts" of policy.

_(Unique symptoms: 5, 6, 10, 12, 13.)_

## Hubris and context (the VUCA / HRO core)

- Over-individualising hubris neglects **social, situational and contextual** factors. Leadership as **sensemaking** (Pye 2005) means "staying in touch with context" (Weick 1995).
- Hubris is best seen as a **context-dependent, over-developed strength** (Kaplan & Kaiser 2006), embedded in an organisation's executive and **governance structures**. Boards are partly blamed for business failures, yet often fail to detect or act on CEO-hubris warning signs.
- **High-reliability organisations (HROs)** = organisations that sustain near-error-free performance in hazardous conditions where errors could be catastrophic (Lekka 2011). Lessons:
  - **Aviation (1980s):** fatal crashes from captains' erroneous decisions where **position power** over-rode others' knowledge; crew aware of errors but **failed to challenge** the captain in a "command-and-control" culture (Helmreich et al. 1999).
  - **Hospital crisis centres:** the most senior specialist automatically assumed command even when others were better qualified (Sundar et al. 2007).
  - **Fix:** airlines and hospitals shifted toward **team leadership and collective accountability** → significant reductions in accident/mortality rates. This mitigates positional-power hubris.
- **VUCA link — mindfulness (Weick & Sutcliffe 2001):** in dynamic, ill-structured, ambiguous, unpredictable contexts, leaders need **mindfulness**: accept situational complexity; resist the temptation to simplify; preoccupation with the possibility of error; sensitivity to operations; **defer to expertise**; commit to resilience.
  - These are the **opposite of hubris** (Picone et al. 2014), which produces "cognitive inertia"/"mindlessness": reduced attention to strategy, repeating past-success actions, ignoring unlikely scenarios/alternatives, and dogmatic unwillingness to change even when change is needed.
- **Hubris and risk:** hubristic CEOs take more risk because hubris (1) **warps recognition** of success/failure (over-estimating success) and (2) **amplifies anticipated gains** while downplaying the resources needed. Context-dependent: **Li & Tang (2010)** (>2,790 Chinese manufacturing firms) found the hubris→risk-taking link strongest when markets were complex but munificent, firms younger (less inertia) with more intangible resources, and where the CEO wasn't politically appointed and didn't chair the board. **Implication:** firms need **governance structures** to monitor and protect against CEO hubris (failures cited: Vivendi, Enron, Tyco, WorldCom, Long-Term Capital). Boards rarely intervene to disempower a hubrist (Kets de Vries 2016).
- **Media as stakeholder:** media praise → larger acquisition premiums (Hayward & Hambrick 1997). **Malmendier & Tate (2009):** CEOs who win "superstar" status (e.g. prestigious awards) subsequently **underperform**, extract **more compensation**, and spend more time on outside activities (other boards, writing books) — effects strongest in firms with the **weakest governance**.

## Paradox, retribution and process

### The paradox of hubris

- Hubris has **contradictory yet interrelated** elements (confidence/over-confidence; intended/unintended outcomes) — a **"bright and dark" side** (Judge et al. 2009; Picone et al. 2014). The very strengths that turn hubristic (self-confidence, ambition, decisiveness, identification with one's org) can first **enable** a leader's emergence/effectiveness, then **tip over** into weaknesses (over-confidence, over-ambition, over-identification, impulsiveness).
- Examples: Tony Blair's healthy energy → messianic self-exaltation (1997 election); **Steve Jobs** identifying so fully with Apple that "in his mind he was Apple" (his 1987 dismissal was personally devastating); **Tesco's** proper ambition to grow internationally → hubristic over-ambition in the failed US "Fresh & Easy" venture.
- In **virtue-ethics** terms, hubrists violate the Aristotelian **"golden mean"** between deficiency and excess; **humility** is posited as the antithetical virtue.

### Hubris and retribution (nemesis)

- Hubris exists in **duality with nemesis** — retribution as "the completion of a pattern already started" (Midgley 2004). Examples: Xerxes, Napoleon, ex-RBS CEO **Fred Goodwin**.
- **Ronfeldt's (1994) RAND report** (for the CIA) proposed a **"hubris-nemesis complex"**: leaders who think themselves God-like AND seek to "confront, defeat, humiliate and punish" rivals who also aspire to hubris (examples cited: Castro, Milošević, Saddam Hussein). Deterring/negotiating with such leaders using "normal-leader" approaches is likely ineffectual or counter-productive. Prescient irony: Bush and Blair, who sought to deliver nemesis on Saddam, were themselves hubristic and met their own retribution (Chilcot Inquiry on Blair).
- Hubrists can sometimes **escape retribution** by exiting before consequences land — e.g. **Sir Terry Leahy** left Tesco in 2011 (reputation largely intact) before the US "Fresh & Easy" chain's full collapse (**200 stores closed in 2013, ~£1.2bn cost**). Ironically his book _Management in 10 Words_ admitted "hubris is a common fault and one I hope to avoid."

### Hubris as a process

- A **processual view** sees leadership not as something a leader _possesses_ but as an **emergent process** of interactions among many actors evolving through time (Tourish 2014; Uhl-Bien et al. 2007; complexity theory). This focuses on **all those involved**, not just the formal leader, and sidesteps the problem of demarcating hubris from substance-based constructs (over-confidence, narcissism, dark-side charisma).

## Recognising and researching hubris (methods)

- People can intuitively name hubristic figures (Richard Fuld/Lehman, Sepp Blatter/FIFA, Donald Trump/politics), but a **systematic, reliable** approach is needed beyond gut instinct.
- Hubris is largely a **"rear-mirror" phenomenon** — analysed after the fact, once harmful effects are already entrenched and hard to dislodge. Studying the **trajectory** (not just the end-state) reveals when adaptive traits "tip over" into maladaptive ones.
- **Problem:** hubris-adjacent traits (confidence, ambition) overlap with _desirable_ leadership traits and are often **selected for and encouraged** (e.g. Steve Jobs/Apple, Jack Welch/GE; pre-2007 RBS CEO lauded as "world's best banker," knighted, then a pariah). Recruitment can actively seek the very traits that seed hubris.
- **Symptomology (Hubris Syndrome)** offers a potential "gold standard," but **Russell (2011)** suggests refinements: (1) the person must have held power for some time; (2) assess whether behaviour is **dysfunctional / produces unwise, risky decisions** harmful to self and others; (3) look for **deterioration** / excessive reaction to stressful events. **Loch (2016):** hubris may be the "tail end" of normal status-seeking/narcissism rather than a true medical syndrome (analogy: extreme height isn't a syndrome but can cause medical problems). Critiques note >half the 14 symptoms **overlap** with other disorders.
- **"At-a-distance" measures** (because elite hubrists won't submit to study — Hambrick 2007):
  - **Linguistic markers / "bio-markers":** personality shows up in natural language (Pennebaker et al. 2003). **Garrard et al. (2014)** compared transcribed speech of hubristic PMs (Thatcher, Blair) vs non-hubristic (John Major) and found differences — e.g. the **ratio of first-person plural to first-person singular pronouns** (aligns with symptom 6, the royal "we"). Concealing these markers is essentially impossible.
  - **CEO letters to shareholders** (Craig & Amernic 2011) and software like **DICTION** (used on Lord Browne/BP, Rupert Murdoch speeches — mixed results) and **LIWC** (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count; Tausczik & Pennebaker 2010).
  - **Caveats:** speeches/letters may be **ghost-written**; software needs further development; need for **in-depth qualitative** studies (ethnography, participant observation, story-telling, case studies, biography).
- **Proxies for hubris:** **hyper-CSE** (Hiller & Hambrick 2005); **hubristic vs authentic pride** (Tracy & Robins 2014) — pride is cross-culturally recognised and expressed verbally + non-verbally.
- **Processual methods:** longitudinal case studies; **temporal bracketing** (decomposing the process into phases); narrative/visual mapping of key events.

## Future directions & pedagogy

Building on Picone et al.'s (2014) agenda (antecedents; hubris↔decisions/risk; mitigating factors; hubris↔strategy/performance), the authors add three gaps:

1. **Relationality** — study hubrists' **dyadic relationships** with restraining figures ("foils," "alter egos," "sage fools," "toe-holders" — e.g. Whitelaw constraining Thatcher) and how that constraining role mitigates hubris.
2. **Corporate / collective hubris** — largely undeveloped (NASA, BP); study the corporate-culture conditions that breed it and its link to individual-leader hubris.
3. **Gender** — Hubris Syndrome is positioned as a largely **"male phenomenon"** (Thatcher, Carly Fiorina rare exceptions). Men are more over-confident; testosterone is linked to opportunity-seeking, cortisol to risk-avoidance (Ghaemi et al. 2016). Open question: is the lack of female-hubrist research because too few women hold the requisite power positions?
4. **Education:** **Collinson & Tourish (2015)** warn that business schools' romance with **charismatic/heroic leadership models** may unintentionally **incubate hubris** while ignoring its antithesis, **humility**. A more responsible pedagogy promotes **critical analysis and reflexivity** as safeguards.
