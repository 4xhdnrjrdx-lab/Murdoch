## Overview — What Project Scope Management is

Project Scope Management covers the processes needed to make sure the project includes **all the work required — and only the work required** — to finish the project successfully. Its core concern is **defining and controlling what is and is not included** in the project.

The Knowledge Area contains **six processes**:

- **5.1 Plan Scope Management** — Create a scope management plan documenting how project and product scope will be defined, validated, and controlled.
- **5.2 Collect Requirements** — Determine, document, and manage stakeholder needs and requirements to meet project objectives.
- **5.3 Define Scope** — Develop a detailed description of the project and product.
- **5.4 Create WBS** — Subdivide project deliverables and work into smaller, more manageable components.
- **5.5 Validate Scope** — Formalise acceptance of completed project deliverables.
- **5.6 Control Scope** — Monitor the status of project/product scope and manage changes to the scope baseline.

These are presented as discrete processes with defined interfaces, but in practice they overlap and interact in ways the Guide cannot fully detail.

## Key Concepts for Project Scope Management

**Two meanings of "scope":**

- **Product scope** — the features and functions that characterise a product, service, or result.
- **Project scope** — the work performed to deliver a product, service, or result with the specified features and functions. "Project scope" is sometimes viewed as including product scope.

**Life-cycle continuum (predictive ↔ adaptive/agile):**

- In a **predictive life cycle**, deliverables are defined at the project's start, and any scope changes are managed progressively.
- In an **adaptive/agile life cycle**, deliverables are developed over multiple iterations; a detailed scope is defined and approved for each iteration as it begins.

Adaptive projects are built to absorb high levels of change and need continuous stakeholder engagement. The overall scope is broken into a set of requirements and work to be done — often called a **product backlog**. At the start of each iteration the team decides how many of the highest-priority backlog items can be delivered next.

- **Three processes (Collect Requirements, Define Scope, Create WBS) are repeated each iteration** in adaptive projects. In predictive projects they're done near the start and updated as needed via integrated change control.
- In adaptive/agile life cycles, sponsor and customer representatives stay continuously engaged to give feedback on deliverables as they're created and to keep the backlog reflecting current needs.
- **Two processes (Validate Scope and Control Scope) are repeated each iteration** in adaptive projects. In predictive projects, Validate Scope happens at each deliverable/phase review and Control Scope is ongoing.

**Scope baseline (predictive):** the approved version of the project scope statement, the WBS, and its associated WBS dictionary. It can only be changed through formal change control, and serves as the comparison basis for Validate Scope, Control Scope, and other controlling processes. Adaptive projects instead use **backlogs** (product requirements and user stories) to reflect current needs.

**Measuring completion:** project scope completion is measured against the **project management plan**; product scope completion is measured against the **product requirements**.

**Requirement (definition):** a condition or capability that must be present in a product, service, or result to satisfy an agreement or other formally imposed specification.

**On Validate Scope:** it formalises acceptance of completed deliverables. The **verified deliverables** from the Control Quality process feed into Validate Scope. Its output, **accepted deliverables**, are formally signed off by the authorised stakeholder. This is why stakeholders should be engaged early (during planning, and sometimes initiating) and provide input on deliverable quality, so Control Quality can assess performance and recommend changes.

## Trends and Emerging Practices

Requirements have always mattered in project management and keep gaining attention. As the global environment grows more complex, organisations increasingly use **business analysis** as a competitive advantage by defining, managing, and controlling requirements activities. Business analysis work can begin **before** a project is initiated and a project manager is assigned. Per _Requirements Management: A Practice Guide_ [14], the requirements management process starts with a **needs assessment**, which may begin in portfolio planning, program planning, or within a discrete project.

Eliciting, documenting, and managing stakeholder requirements happens within the Project Scope Management processes. Emerging practices include collaborating with business analysis professionals to:

- Determine problems and identify business needs;
- Identify and recommend viable solutions to meet those needs;
- Elicit, document, and manage stakeholder requirements to meet business and project objectives;
- Facilitate successful implementation of the product, service, or end result.

The process ends with **requirements closure**, transitioning the product/service/result to the recipient so benefits can be measured, monitored, realised, and sustained over time.

Business analysis should be assigned to people with sufficient skills/expertise. If a **business analyst** is assigned, requirement-related activities are that role's responsibility. The **project manager** is responsible for ensuring requirements work is in the project management plan and that requirements activities happen on time, within budget, and deliver value. The PM–BA relationship should be a **collaborative partnership**; mutual understanding of each other's roles raises the project's likelihood of success.

## Tailoring Considerations

Each project is unique, so the PM tailors how these processes are applied. Considerations include:

- **Knowledge and requirements management** — Does the org have formal/informal knowledge & requirements management systems? What guidelines for reusing requirements in future?
- **Validation and control** — Are there existing formal/informal validation- and control-related policies, procedures, guidelines?
- **Development approach** — Does the org use agile? Iterative or incremental? Predictive? Would a hybrid work?
- **Stability of requirements** — Are there areas with unstable requirements? Do they call for lean/agile/other adaptive techniques until stable?
- **Governance** — Are there formal/informal audit and governance policies, procedures, guidelines?

---

## Considerations for Agile/Adaptive Environments

In projects with evolving requirements, high risk, or significant uncertainty, scope is often **not understood at the start** or it **evolves during** the project. Agile methods deliberately spend **less** time defining and agreeing scope up front and **more** time establishing a process for ongoing discovery and refinement. Environments with emerging requirements often find a gap between the real business requirements and those originally stated. So agile methods purposely build and review **prototypes and release versions** to refine requirements; scope is therefore defined and redefined throughout the project. In agile, the requirements constitute the **backlog**.

## 5.1 Plan Scope Management

The process of creating a scope management plan that documents how project and product scope will be defined, validated, and controlled. **Key benefit:** provides guidance and direction on how scope will be managed throughout the project. Performed **once or at predefined points** in the project.

The scope management plan is a component of the project/program management plan describing how scope will be defined, developed, monitored, controlled, and validated. Its development begins with analysing the project charter, the latest approved subsidiary plans, historical information in OPAs, and relevant EEFs.

### 5.1.1 Inputs

- **5.1.1.1 Project charter** — Documents project purpose, high-level description, assumptions, constraints, and high-level requirements.
- **5.1.1.2 Project management plan** — Components include:
  - _Quality management plan_ — How the org's quality policy/methods/standards are applied can influence how scope is managed.
  - _Project life cycle description_ — The series of phases from inception to project end.
  - _Development approach_ — Whether waterfall, iterative, adaptive, agile, or hybrid will be used.
- **5.1.1.3 Enterprise environmental factors** — e.g., organisation's culture, infrastructure, personnel administration, marketplace conditions.
- **5.1.1.4 Organizational process assets** — e.g., policies and procedures; historical information and lessons-learned repositories.

### 5.1.2 Tools & Techniques

- **5.1.2.1 Expert judgment** — From people/groups with knowledge or training in previous similar projects and in the relevant industry, discipline, and application area.
- **5.1.2.2 Data analysis** — Notably **alternatives analysis**: evaluating various ways to collect requirements, elaborate scope, create the product, validate scope, and control scope.
- **5.1.2.3 Meetings** — To develop the scope management plan. Attendees may include the PM, sponsor, selected team members/stakeholders, anyone responsible for scope processes, and others as needed.

### 5.1.3 Outputs

- **5.1.3.1 Scope management plan** — A component of the PM plan describing how scope will be defined, developed, monitored, controlled, and validated. Components:
  - Process for preparing a project scope statement;
  - Process enabling creation of the WBS from the detailed scope statement;
  - Process establishing how the scope baseline is approved and maintained;
  - Process specifying how formal acceptance of completed deliverables is obtained.
  - It can be formal or informal, broadly framed or highly detailed, based on project needs.
- **5.1.3.2 Requirements management plan** — A component of the PM plan describing how project and product requirements will be analysed, documented, and managed. Per _Business Analysis for Practitioners: A Practice Guide_ [7], some organisations call it a **business analysis plan**. Components can include:
  - How requirements activities will be planned, tracked, reported;
  - Configuration management activities — how changes are initiated, how impacts are analysed, how they're traced/tracked/reported, and the authorisation levels to approve changes;
  - Requirements prioritisation process;
  - Metrics used and the rationale;
  - Traceability structure reflecting the requirement attributes captured on the traceability matrix.

## 5.2 Collect Requirements

The process of determining, documenting, and managing stakeholder needs and requirements to meet objectives. **Key benefit:** provides the basis for defining product scope and project scope. Performed **once or at predefined points**.

The Guide does not address product requirements specifically (they're industry-specific); _Business Analysis for Practitioners: A Practice Guide_ [7] covers those in depth. Project success is directly influenced by active stakeholder involvement in discovering and decomposing needs into requirements, and by careful determination, documentation, and management of requirements. **Requirements become the foundation of the WBS**; cost, schedule, quality planning, and procurement are all based on them.

### 5.2.1 Inputs

- **5.2.1.1 Project charter** — High-level project description and high-level requirements used to develop detailed requirements.
- **5.2.1.2 Project management plan** — Components:
  - _Scope management plan_ — How project scope is defined and developed.
  - _Requirements management plan_ — How requirements are collected, analysed, documented.
  - _Stakeholder engagement plan_ — Understanding stakeholder communication requirements and engagement level, to adapt to their participation in requirements activities.
- **5.2.1.3 Project documents:**
  - _Assumption log_ — Assumptions about product, project, environment, stakeholders, etc. that can influence requirements.
  - _Lessons learned register_ — Info on effective requirements-collection techniques, especially for iterative/adaptive development.
  - _Stakeholder register_ — Identifies stakeholders who can provide requirements info; captures their requirements/expectations.
- **5.2.1.4 Business documents** — Notably the _business case_, which can describe required, desired, and optional criteria for meeting business needs.
- **5.2.1.5 Agreements** — Can contain project and product requirements.
- **5.2.1.6 Enterprise environmental factors** — Org culture, infrastructure, personnel administration, marketplace conditions.
- **5.2.1.7 Organizational process assets** — Policies and procedures; historical info and lessons-learned repository from previous projects.

### 5.2.2 Tools & Techniques

- **5.2.2.1 Expert judgment** — Specialised knowledge/training in: business analysis, requirements elicitation, requirements analysis, requirements documentation, requirements in previous similar projects, diagramming techniques, facilitation, conflict management.
- **5.2.2.2 Data gathering:**
  - _Brainstorming_ — Generate and collect many ideas about project/product requirements.
  - _Interviews_ — Formal/informal elicitation by talking directly to stakeholders, via prepared and spontaneous questions; one-on-one or with multiple interviewers/interviewees. Useful for identifying features/functions and for obtaining confidential information.
  - _Focus groups_ — Prequalified stakeholders + SMEs share expectations/attitudes about a proposed product; a trained moderator runs an interactive, conversational discussion.
  - _Questionnaires and surveys_ — Written question sets to quickly gather info from many respondents; best with varied/geographically-dispersed audiences, quick turnaround, and where statistical analysis fits.
  - _Benchmarking_ — Comparing actual/planned products, processes, and practices against comparable organisations (internal or external) to identify best practices, generate improvement ideas, and provide a performance-measurement basis.
- **5.2.2.3 Data analysis** — _Document analysis_: reviewing/assessing relevant documented information to elicit requirements. Documents that may be analysed include: agreements; business plans; business process or interface documentation; business rules repositories; current process flows; marketing literature; problem/issue logs; policies and procedures; regulatory documentation (laws, codes, ordinances); requests for proposal; use cases.
- **5.2.2.4 Decision making:**
  - _Voting_ — Collective decision technique with multiple alternatives:
    - **Unanimity** — everyone agrees on a single course of action.
    - **Majority** — support from more than 50% of the group (an odd-numbered group avoids ties).
    - **Plurality** — the largest block decides even without a majority (used when there are more than two options).
  - _Autocratic decision making_ — one individual decides for the group.
  - _Multicriteria decision analysis_ — a decision matrix giving a systematic, analytical approach to set criteria (risk levels, uncertainty, valuation) to evaluate and rank ideas.
- **5.2.2.5 Data representation:**
  - _Affinity diagrams_ — Classify large numbers of ideas into groups for review/analysis.
  - _Mind mapping_ — Consolidate ideas from individual brainstorming into a single map to show commonalities/differences and generate new ideas.
- **5.2.2.6 Interpersonal and team skills:**
  - _Nominal group technique_ — Enhances brainstorming with voting to rank the most useful ideas. Four steps: (1) a question/problem is posed and each person silently writes ideas; (2) the moderator records all ideas on a flip chart; (3) each idea is discussed until understood; (4) individuals privately vote to prioritise (commonly 1–5, 1 lowest, 5 highest); voting may run in multiple rounds, tallying after each round and keeping the highest-scoring ideas.
  - _Observation/conversation_ ("job shadowing") — Directly viewing people doing their work; useful for detailed processes or when users struggle to articulate requirements. Done externally by an observer, or by a "participant observer" who performs the process to uncover hidden requirements.
  - _Facilitation_ — Focused sessions bringing key stakeholders together to define product requirements; workshops quickly define cross-functional requirements and reconcile differences, build trust, foster relationships, improve communication, and surface/resolve issues earlier. Facilitation situations include:
    - **Joint Application Design/Development (JAD)** — software-industry sessions bringing business SMEs and the development team together to gather requirements and improve the development process.
    - **Quality Function Deployment (QFD)** — manufacturing technique to determine critical characteristics for new product development; starts by collecting customer needs (**voice of the customer / VOC**), then sorts, prioritises, and sets goals.
    - **User stories** — short textual descriptions of required functionality, often developed in a requirements workshop; describe the **role** (who benefits), the **goal** (what they need to accomplish), and the **motivation** (the benefit).
- **5.2.2.7 Context diagram** — An example of a **scope model**. Visually depicts product scope by showing a business system and how people/other systems (**actors**) interact with it: inputs to the system, actors providing inputs, outputs from the system, and actors receiving outputs.
- **5.2.2.8 Prototypes** — A method of getting early feedback on requirements by providing a model of the expected product before building it (small-scale products, 2D/3D computer models, mock-ups, simulations). Supports **progressive elaboration** through iterative cycles of mock-up creation, user experimentation, feedback, and revision. **Storyboarding** is a prototyping technique showing sequence/navigation through a series of images/illustrations (used in film, advertising, instructional design, and agile/software development — e.g., navigation paths through web pages/screens/UIs).

### 5.2.3 Outputs

- **5.2.3.1 Requirements documentation** — Describes how individual requirements meet the project's business need. Requirements may start high-level and become progressively detailed. Before being baselined, they must be **unambiguous (measurable and testable), traceable, complete, consistent, and acceptable** to key stakeholders. Format ranges from a simple list categorised by stakeholder and priority to elaborate forms with executive summary, detailed descriptions, and attachments.
  **Requirement classifications:**
  - _Business requirements_ — Higher-level org needs (business issues/opportunities, reasons the project was undertaken).
  - _Stakeholder requirements_ — Needs of a stakeholder or group.
  - _Solution requirements_ — Features, functions, characteristics that meet business/stakeholder requirements. Split into:
    - **Functional requirements** — the product's behaviours (actions, processes, data, interactions).
    - **Nonfunctional requirements** — environmental conditions/qualities needed for effectiveness (reliability, security, performance, safety, level of service, supportability, retention/purge, etc.).
  - _Transition and readiness requirements_ — Temporary capabilities (e.g., data conversion, training) to move from the current as-is state to the future state.
  - _Project requirements_ — Actions/processes/conditions the project must meet (milestone dates, contractual obligations, constraints, etc.).
  - _Quality requirements_ — Conditions/criteria to validate successful completion of a deliverable or other requirements (tests, certifications, validations, etc.).
- **5.2.3.2 Requirements traceability matrix (RTM)** — A grid linking product requirements from origin to the deliverables that satisfy them. Ensures each requirement adds business value by linking it to business and project objectives; tracks requirements across the life cycle; provides structure for managing changes to product scope.
  **Tracing covers:** business needs/opportunities/goals/objectives; project objectives; project scope and WBS deliverables; product design; product development; test strategy and test scenarios; high-level → detailed requirements.
  **Typical attributes:** unique identifier; textual description; rationale for inclusion; owner; source; priority; version; current status (active, cancelled, deferred, added, approved, assigned, completed); status date. Additional attributes for stakeholder satisfaction: stability, complexity, acceptance criteria.

## 5.3 Define Scope

The process of developing a detailed description of the project and product. **Key benefit:** describes the product/service/result boundaries and acceptance criteria.

Because not all requirements from Collect Requirements are included, Define Scope **selects the final project requirements** from the requirements documentation, then develops a detailed description of the project and product. The detailed scope statement builds on the high-level description from initiation; scope is described with greater specificity as more is known. Existing risks, assumptions, and constraints are analysed for completeness and updated. The process can be **highly iterative**: in iterative life cycles a high-level vision is set for the whole project, but detailed scope is determined one iteration at a time.

### 5.3.1 Inputs

- **5.3.1.1 Project charter** — High-level description, product characteristics, approval requirements.
- **5.3.1.2 Project management plan** — Notably the _scope management plan_ (how scope is defined, validated, controlled).
- **5.3.1.3 Project documents:**
  - _Assumption log_ — Assumptions/constraints that can influence project and product scope.
  - _Requirements documentation_ — Requirements to be incorporated into scope.
  - _Risk register_ — Response strategies that may affect scope (e.g., reducing/changing scope to avoid or mitigate a risk).
- **5.3.1.4 Enterprise environmental factors** — Org culture, infrastructure, personnel administration, marketplace conditions.
- **5.3.1.5 Organizational process assets** — Policies, procedures, templates for a scope statement; project files from previous projects; lessons learned.

### 5.3.2 Tools & Techniques

- **5.3.2.1 Expert judgment** — From those with knowledge/experience of similar projects.
- **5.3.2.2 Data analysis** — _Alternatives analysis_ to evaluate ways to meet requirements and the charter's objectives.
- **5.3.2.3 Decision making** — _Multicriteria decision analysis_ using a decision matrix to set criteria (requirements, schedule, budget, resources) to refine project/product scope.
- **5.3.2.4 Interpersonal and team skills** — _Facilitation_ in workshops/working sessions with key stakeholders of varied expectations/expertise, to reach a cross-functional, common understanding of deliverables and project/product boundaries.
- **5.3.2.5 Product analysis** — Defining products/services by asking questions about them and forming answers describing use, characteristics, and other aspects of what's delivered. Requirements are captured high-level and decomposed to the detail needed to design the final product. Techniques include: **product breakdown, requirements analysis, systems analysis, systems engineering, value analysis, value engineering**.

### 5.3.3 Outputs

- **5.3.3.1 Project scope statement** — Description of project scope, major deliverables, and exclusions; documents the entire scope (project + product), describes deliverables in detail, and gives a common understanding among stakeholders. May contain explicit scope exclusions to manage expectations. Enables detailed planning, guides execution, and provides the baseline for evaluating whether change/additional-work requests fall inside or outside project boundaries. Includes (directly or by reference):
  - _Product scope description_ — Progressively elaborates the characteristics of the product/service/result.
  - _Deliverables_ — Any unique, verifiable product/result/capability required to complete a process, phase, or project; includes ancillary results like PM reports and documentation; may be summary-level or detailed.
  - _Acceptance criteria_ — Conditions that must be met before deliverables are accepted.
  - _Project exclusions_ — What is out of scope; explicit statement manages expectations and reduces scope creep.
- **5.3.3.2 Project documents updates** — _Assumption log_, _Requirements documentation_, _Requirements traceability matrix_, _Stakeholder register_ (updated with new/changed assumptions, requirements, traceability, and stakeholder info).

**Table 5-1. Elements of the Project Charter vs. Project Scope Statement — described:**

- **Project Charter (high-level):** project purpose; measurable objectives and success criteria; high-level requirements; high-level project description, boundaries, key deliverables; overall project risk; summary milestone schedule; preapproved financial resources; key stakeholder list; project approval requirements (what constitutes success, who decides, who signs off); project exit criteria (conditions to close/cancel project or phase); assigned PM with responsibility/authority level; name and authority of the sponsor / person authorising the charter.
- **Project Scope Statement (detailed):** project scope description (progressively elaborated); project deliverables; acceptance criteria; project exclusions.
- **Note:** charter and scope statement may seem to overlap but differ in level of detail — charter = high-level; scope statement = detailed, progressively elaborated.

## 5.4 Create WBS

The process of subdividing project deliverables and project work into smaller, more manageable components. **Key benefit:** provides a framework of what has to be delivered. Performed **once or at predefined points**.

The **WBS** is a hierarchical decomposition of the total scope of work to be carried out by the team to achieve objectives and create deliverables; it organises and defines total project scope and represents the work in the current approved scope statement. The planned work sits in the lowest-level components called **work packages**; a work package groups activities where work is scheduled, estimated, monitored, and controlled. In WBS context, "work" means work products/deliverables (results of activity), not the activity itself.

### 5.4.1 Inputs

- **5.4.1.1 Project management plan** — _Scope management plan_ (how the WBS is created from the scope statement).
- **5.4.1.2 Project documents:**
  - _Project scope statement_ — Describes work performed and work excluded.
  - _Requirements documentation_ — How individual requirements meet the business need.
- **5.4.1.3 Enterprise environmental factors** — Industry-specific WBS standards relevant to the project (external reference sources).
- **5.4.1.4 Organizational process assets** — Policies/procedures/templates for the WBS; project files from previous projects; lessons learned.

### 5.4.2 Tools & Techniques

- **5.4.2.1 Expert judgment** — From those with knowledge/experience of similar projects.
- **5.4.2.2 Decomposition** — Dividing project scope and deliverables into smaller, manageable parts. The **work package** is the lowest-level work for which cost and duration can be estimated and managed; the level of decomposition is guided by the degree of control needed and varies with project size/complexity. Generally involves:
  - Identifying and analysing deliverables and related work;
  - Structuring and organising the WBS;
  - Decomposing upper levels into lower-level detailed components;
  - Developing and assigning identification codes to components;
  - Verifying the degree of decomposition is appropriate.
    Decomposition approaches include **top-down**, **organisation-specific guidelines**, **WBS templates**, and a **bottom-up** approach to group subcomponents. WBS structures can use: project life-cycle **phases** at the second level (product/project deliverables at the third); **major deliverables** at the second level; or **subcomponents developed outside the team** (e.g., contracted work — the seller develops a supporting **contract WBS**).
    Upper-level components are subdivided into fundamental components representing verifiable products/services/results. With agile, **epics can be decomposed into user stories**. Verify correctness by checking that lower-level components are **necessary and sufficient** for the higher-level deliverable. Different deliverables can have different decomposition levels. **Excessive decomposition** causes nonproductive management effort, inefficient resource use, decreased efficiency, and difficulty aggregating data.
    **Rolling wave planning:** when a deliverable far in the future can't yet be decomposed, the team waits until it's agreed before developing WBS detail.
    **100 percent rule:** the WBS represents all product and project work (including PM work); the total of the lowest-level work rolls up so nothing is left out and no extra work is performed.
    _(Refer to Practice Standard for Work Breakdown Structures – Second Edition [15] for WBS templates.)_

### 5.4.3 Outputs

- **5.4.3.1 Scope baseline** — The approved version of the scope statement, WBS, and WBS dictionary; changeable only via formal change control; a component of the PM plan. Components:
  - _Project scope statement_ — Description of scope, major deliverables, exclusions.
  - _WBS_ — Hierarchical decomposition of total scope; each descending level = more detailed definition.
    - **Work package** — Lowest WBS level, with a unique identifier; identifiers form a **code of accounts** for hierarchical summation of cost/schedule/resource info. Each work package belongs to a single **control account**.
    - **Control account** — A management control point where scope, budget, and schedule are integrated and compared to earned value for performance measurement; has two or more work packages.
    - **Planning package** — A WBS component **below the control account and above the work package**, with known work content but no detailed schedule activities.
  - _WBS dictionary_ — A document with detailed deliverable, activity, and scheduling info for each WBS component. May include: code of account identifier; description of work; assumptions and constraints; responsible organisation; schedule milestones; associated schedule activities; resources required; cost estimates; quality requirements; acceptance criteria; technical references; agreement information.
- **5.4.3.2 Project documents updates** — _Assumption log_ and _Requirements documentation_ (updated with new assumptions/constraints and approved changes).

## 5.5 Validate Scope

The process of formalising acceptance of completed project deliverables. **Key benefit:** brings objectivity to acceptance and increases the probability of final acceptance by validating each deliverable. Performed **periodically** as needed.

Verified deliverables from Control Quality are reviewed with the customer/sponsor to confirm satisfactory completion and obtain **formal acceptance**. Planning outputs (requirements documentation, scope baseline) plus work performance data from execution are the basis for validation and final acceptance.

**Validate Scope vs Control Quality:** Validate Scope is primarily about **acceptance** of deliverables; Control Quality is primarily about **correctness** of deliverables and meeting specified quality requirements. Control Quality is generally performed **before** Validate Scope, though they may run in parallel.

### 5.5.1 Inputs

- **5.5.1.1 Project management plan:** _Scope management plan_ (how formal acceptance is obtained); _Requirements management plan_ (how requirements are validated); _Scope baseline_ (compared to actual results to determine if change/corrective/preventive action is needed).
- **5.5.1.2 Project documents:** _Lessons learned register_; _Quality reports_ (QA issues managed/escalated, improvement recommendations, Control Quality findings — reviewed before acceptance); _Requirements documentation_; _Requirements traceability matrix_ (how requirements will be validated).
- **5.5.1.3 Verified deliverables** — Deliverables completed and checked for correctness via Control Quality.
- **5.5.1.4 Work performance data** — Degree of compliance with requirements, number/severity of nonconformities, number of validation cycles in a period.

### 5.5.2 Tools & Techniques

- **5.5.2.1 Inspection** — Measuring, examining, validating to determine whether work/deliverables meet requirements and acceptance criteria. Also called reviews, product reviews, walkthroughs.
- **5.5.2.2 Decision making** — _Voting_ to reach a conclusion when validation is done by the team and other stakeholders.

### 5.5.3 Outputs

- **5.5.3.1 Accepted deliverables** — Deliverables meeting acceptance criteria are formally signed off/approved by the customer/sponsor; the formal documentation is forwarded to **Close Project or Phase** (4.7).
- **5.5.3.2 Work performance information** — Project progress info (which deliverables are accepted/not and why); documented and communicated to stakeholders.
- **5.5.3.3 Change requests** — Completed deliverables **not** formally accepted are documented with reasons; they may need a **change request for defect repair**, processed via Perform Integrated Change Control (4.6).
- **5.5.3.4 Project documents updates** — _Lessons learned register_ (challenges and what worked); _Requirements documentation_ (actual validation results — notably when results exceed the requirement or a requirement was waived); _Requirements traceability matrix_ (validation results, method, outcome).

## 5.6 Control Scope

The process of monitoring project/product scope status and managing changes to the scope baseline. **Key benefit:** the scope baseline is maintained throughout the project. Performed **throughout** the project.

Control Scope ensures all requested changes and recommended corrective/preventive actions go through **Perform Integrated Change Control** (4.6); it also manages actual changes as they occur and integrates with other control processes. **Scope creep** = uncontrolled expansion to product/project scope without adjusting time, cost, and resources. Because change is inevitable, **some change-control process is mandatory for every project**.

### 5.6.1 Inputs

- **5.6.1.1 Project management plan:** _Scope management plan_ (how scope is controlled); _Requirements management plan_ (how requirements are managed); _Change management plan_ (process for managing change); _Configuration management plan_ (configurable items, items needing formal change control, and the change-control process); _Scope baseline_ (compared to actual results); _Performance measurement baseline_ (compared to actuals when using earned value analysis).
- **5.6.1.2 Project documents:** _Lessons learned register_ (improve scope control); _Requirements documentation_ (detect deviation from agreed scope); _Requirements traceability matrix_ (detect impact of change/deviation on objectives; provide requirement status).
- **5.6.1.3 Work performance data** — Number of change requests received/accepted; number of deliverables verified, validated, completed.
- **5.6.1.4 Organizational process assets** — Existing formal/informal scope and control-related policies, procedures, guidelines; monitoring and reporting methods and templates.

### 5.6.2 Tools & Techniques

- **5.6.2.1 Data analysis:**
  - _Variance analysis_ — Compare baseline to actual results; determine whether variance is within threshold or whether corrective/preventive action is appropriate.
  - _Trend analysis_ — Examine performance over time to see if it's improving or deteriorating.
  - Key aspects: determining the cause and degree of variance relative to the scope baseline and deciding whether corrective/preventive action is required.

### 5.6.3 Outputs

- **5.6.3.1 Work performance information** — Correlated, contextualised info on how project/product scope is performing vs the baseline: categories of changes received, scope variances and causes, schedule/cost impact, forecast of future scope performance.
- **5.6.3.2 Change requests** — Performance analysis may produce change requests to the scope/schedule baselines or other PM-plan components; processed via Perform Integrated Change Control (4.6).
- **5.6.3.3 Project management plan updates** (via change request): _Scope management plan_; _Scope baseline_ (per approved scope/scope-statement/WBS/WBS-dictionary changes — severe variances may need a revised baseline); _Schedule baseline_; _Cost baseline_; _Performance measurement baseline_.
- **5.6.3.4 Project documents updates:** _Lessons learned register_ (effective scope-control techniques, variance causes, corrective actions); _Requirements documentation_; _Requirements traceability matrix_.
