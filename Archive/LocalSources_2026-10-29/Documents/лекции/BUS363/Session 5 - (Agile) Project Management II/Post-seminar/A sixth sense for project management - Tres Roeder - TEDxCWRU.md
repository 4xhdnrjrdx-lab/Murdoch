## Part 1 — Introduction and Institutional History of Change

- Tres Roeder, ~30 years a student/professional of change, frames the talk around developing yourself to make a difference.
- He notes Case Western Reserve University's history of change: in the **1800s** it graduated the first African-American student in the US, and **six of the first eight female physicians** in the US. [Historical claims as stated in the talk — verify if cited.]
- Positive change isn't limited to large institutions — it happens in any local community.

## Part 2 — The Consulting Lesson: Why Facts Aren't Enough

- ~17 years earlier, as a consultant for a **Fortune 500** organisation: 30–40 consultants packed into a small windowless room, covering the glass with **paper and duct tape** to keep work secret — symbolic of producing a "fact-based" solution in private.
- They presented recommendations (facts, charts, PowerPoint) to senior executives; some were "diametrically opposed" to decades-old executive views. **Result:** executives pushed back and challenged them rather than accepting the facts.
- Lesson: facts matter (they ensure change is positive), but facts alone are **not enough to change people**.

## Part 3 — Discipline 1: Awareness

- A leader must be aware of **themselves, other people, the situation, and the environment**.
- Beyond plans/facts, focus on **people**. Clues to observe:
  - **Nonverbal:** facial expressions, movement of hands and arms.
  - **Remote/audio:** word choice, tone of voice, speed of voice.

## Part 4 — Discipline 2: Whole Body Decisions

Use **brain, heart, and gut** together.

- **Brain (frontal cortex):** US Military research cited that brain-only decisions yield a **~70% success rate** — insufficient in high-stakes settings. [Speaker's claim — verify.]
- **Heart:** described as the body's strongest electromagnetic force; sensitive equipment can reportedly detect heart signals **6–8 feet away**, so people constantly communicate electromagnetically. [Speaker's claim — verify.]
- **Gut:** contains "thinking cells" / biochemistry similar to the frontal cortex. [Speaker's claim — verify.]
- **Process:** visualise a fork in the road; clear the mind and imagine going left — watch for anxiety, raised heart rate, gut tightness; then imagine going right — if mind/heart/gut stay calm, that's the better path.

## Part 5 — Discipline 3: Clear Communication

- Defined as: the idea in the **sender's** mind being identical to the idea in the **receiver's** mind.
- **"Michelle's Party" example** — preferences for receiving directions reveal learning styles:
  1. **Printed directions (textual)** — ~one-third of people.
  2. **Map (visual)** — visual learners.
  3. **Talked through (auditory)** — usually the smallest group.
- **Application:** because people process differently, a leader must communicate **in writing, graphically, and verbally** to lead people through change.

## Part 6 — Discipline 4: Adaptability (the Chameleon)

- Change is never a straight line; things rarely go to plan.
- **Case study — Shaker Heights teen center** (Roeder on City Council):
  - **Stakeholders:** swapped a group fixated on why it wouldn't work for people who were **optimistic yet realistic** and believed in the goal.
  - **Location:** adapted through **four locations** before one worked.
  - **Leadership:** as a legislator he needed executive-branch support and recruited a director to be the project's "face."
- **Chameleon metaphor:** adapt to the environment, but the chameleon's centre is the **Core** — non-negotiable vision, ethics, morals. The art is knowing when to adapt vs stand firm (often resolved by a whole-body decision).

## Part 7 — Discipline 5: Diplomacy (managing conflict)

- Leaders shouldn't be judged on whether conflict exists, but on **how they handle it**.
- **Conflict is inevitable** when doing something different — e.g. **Martin Luther King Jr.** and the Civil Rights movement, where conflict was necessary to "push the envelope."
- The test of a leader: when conflict hits, do they freeze, run, or "figure it out"?
- **Three ways people process change:**
  1. **Rational** — interested in facts and data.
  2. **Emotional** — react based on how they feel.
  3. **Political** — focus on proximity to people they care about, influence, prestige.

## Part 8 — Discipline 6: Persistence

- Persistence is only a virtue if the other disciplines are followed — otherwise you're just "persistently wrong."
- **Marriage research:** videotaped newly married couples arguing; when Partner A used facts to prove Partner B "wrong," B didn't change — challenging opinions with facts makes people **"dig in their heels."**
- **Rhythm of persistence:** know when to **push**, when to **sprint**, and when to **back off** — pushing too hard at the wrong time makes things worse.

## Key terms & references

- **The six disciplines:** Awareness · Whole Body Decisions · Clear Communication · Adaptability · Diplomacy · Persistence.
- **Whole-body decision:** integrating brain (frontal cortex), heart, gut.
- **Three learning/communication styles:** textual, visual, auditory.
- **Chameleon metaphor:** adapt to environment while protecting a non-negotiable Core.
- **Three ways people process change:** rational, emotional, political.
- **Examples:** Fortune 500 consulting story; Shaker Heights teen center; Martin Luther King Jr.; marriage-conflict research; CWRU's history of social change.
- **Speaker:** Tres Roeder (author, _A Sixth Sense for Project Management_).
