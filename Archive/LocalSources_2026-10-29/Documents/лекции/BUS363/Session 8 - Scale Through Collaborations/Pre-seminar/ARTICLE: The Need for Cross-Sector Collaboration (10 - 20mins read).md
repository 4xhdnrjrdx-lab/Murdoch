## Core argument

- Today's biggest challenges — health care, environment, education, poverty — are **complex** at every scale (local to international). We too often attack them with **piecemeal, siloed solutions** that, however passionate or exhausting the effort, are **not sufficient to address problems at the scale at which they exist**. ("Solving problems at the scale at which they exist" is the supplement's recurring phrase.)
- **No single lever is enough:** no single policy, social program, or new technology, on its own, solves a genuinely complex problem.
- **Robert Kegan's framing** (developmental psychologist): facing an increasingly complex world, we have two choices —
  1. See the world as **simpler** than it is, or
  2. **Increase the complexity of our own perspective** to match the challenge.
     Applied to problem-solving: we can tackle just one piece, or **engage multiple stakeholders** to craft solutions complex enough — with the perspectives and resources — to actually address the challenge. The second path holds the **greatest potential for sustainable change at scale**.

## What cross-sector collaboration is

- The rise of **cross-sector collaboration**: alliances of individuals and organizations from the **nonprofit, government, philanthropic, and business** sectors that combine their **diverse perspectives and resources** to jointly solve a societal problem and reach a shared goal.
- Related structures named: **public-private partnerships**, **collective impact**, and **pay-for-success** models.

## Why now

- This century brings obstacles of unparalleled complexity: climate change, water scarcity, the rich–poor gap, declining educational outcomes, cultural conflicts.
- **Globalization and technology** are exponentially increasing the **scope, speed, and interrelatedness** of challenges. Faster, more **emergent** problems mean the capacity to **quickly iterate and adapt** becomes essential.
- The decisive ingredient, as ever, is **leadership** — specifically **transformative, collaborative leaders** who can lead cross-sector collaborations.

## Developing as a cross-sector leader

- One route: **purposefully shift sectors** across a career to become a **"tri-sector leader,"** gaining first-hand perspective from each sector. (References the HBR "tri-sector leader" idea, and earlier collective-impact writing on the **five traits of an effective "backbone" leader**.)
- But the supplement's thesis: effective cross-sector leadership is **not merely** the result of moving between sectors or a fixed set of hireable traits — it's a **set of mind-sets and skills that can be developed**.

### The key balancing acts / polarities

Meeting complex, interdependent, emergent challenges requires leaders to **balance their attention** between:

- the **people** involved (including multiple stakeholders), and
- the **results** needed for impact.

This means **managing key polarities** rather than falling into false either/or choices:

- a **set vision** vs. being **adaptive/receptive** to input (need both);
- being **charismatic** enough to lead through influence vs. **humble** enough to engage a diverse team (need both);
- choosing **collaboration** over other ways of handling difference — i.e. over **competing, accommodating, compromising, or avoiding** (an allusion to the **Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument / TKI**). Collaboration is hardest because it demands **both**:
  - the **highest degree of agency** (clarity and sustained action toward your own vision/strategy), **and**
  - the **highest degree of compassionate cooperation** (empathy, curiosity, and focus on others' needs).

### Differences as an asset (design _with_, not _for_)

- The "gift" of cross-sector collaboration: **using differences as an asset** — differences in resources, experience, demographics, industry, sector, and in perspective (e.g. assessments of risk, time, and scale).
- The most robust, sustainable solutions come from **designing _with_ — not just _for_** — the communities most affected.
- So effective cross-sector leaders understand **human-centered design**, engage stakeholders with **empathy**, and embrace an **iterative process**. They go further: they **map the system** and commit to putting those most affected **at the table as co-designers**, **addressing power dynamics** so all stakeholders buy into the co-designed solution.
- To do this they must **address power dynamics, build trust, and help the team repair breaches** of the shared culture they're building.

### Leadership habits & the "moments that matter"

- Treating diversity as an asset is one thing; the real test is **navigating differences in the moments that matter most** (e.g. a **high-stakes conversation**), where a leader must **not merely react** but **creatively choose** how to engage.
- This requires **awareness of one's own default behaviors / "leadership habits"** and the **capacity to override the default** response when needed.
- Encouraging note: research indicates these sophisticated capacities **can be developed**. Support cited: Becker's decade **teaching negotiation and collaboration at Stanford** and coaching purpose-driven leaders; and the **Presidio Institute's** four cycles of its **Cross Sector Leadership Fellowship**.
- Growing these capacities requires **a framework, practice, and awareness** of default habits, plus the ability to **discern and choose creatively in the moment**.

## Preview of the supplement's articles (examples of cross-sector leadership in action)

1. **"The Essential Skills of Cross Sector Leadership"** — presents examples plus the **Presidio Institute's framework**: building teams, solving problems, achieving sustainable results.
2. **"Cocreating a Change-Making Culture"** — by **Sara Brenner** (President, Community Wealth Partners). On building **trust** and a **change-making culture**. Invokes the **Peter Drucker** line _"Culture eats strategy for breakfast"_; argues funders should support not just strategic/programmatic work but the **culture-building** needed for lasting impact.
3. **"Creating a Cross Sector Leadership Network"** — by **Heather McLeod Grant**. With **James Irvine Foundation** funding, built the place-based **Irvine New Leadership Network** in **Fresno, CA**. Found that frontline nonprofit leaders often **lacked the power/resources** to shift systems alone, so the strategy shifted toward individual development, trust-building among diverse stakeholders, and engaging the wider community for systemic impact.
4. **Profile: Emily May** — cofounder/ED of **Hollaback**, a global nonprofit fighting **street harassment** via online platforms + offline organizing (engaging both people affected and underlying systems).
5. **"The Progressive Resurgence of Federalism"** — by **Lenny Mendonca & Laura D. Tyson**. State/local governments as **leverage points**; local leaders as **convener, visionary, weaver of stakeholders, and funder** able to take proven programs to scale.
6. **Profile: Siobhan Foley** — a **FUSE fellow** leading cross-sector climate-change collaboration in **New Orleans**; chose leverage points to build momentum, enable a quick win, and show how individual actions matter.
7. **Profile: Libby Schaaf** — Mayor of **Oakland, CA**. Her **Oakland Promise** (cradle-to-career initiative to raise college completion among Oakland youth) shows three leverage points a government leader brings: (a) **embracing the bigness/complexity** of systemic challenges so smaller initiatives coalesce into systemic change; (b) **convening grass-tops and grassroots** to co-design funded, sustainable solutions; (c) **catalyzing local leadership/creativity** via clear vision + support, mentorship, and freedom to innovate.

## Call to action (who is a cross-sector leader?)

The article invites four reader types to see a role for themselves:

- **Business leaders** — use fast-paced, **iterative, scalable** resources to make a difference beyond the bottom line.
- **Nonprofit leaders** — leverage programmatic expertise + community connections toward **systemic, scalable** change beyond their own programs.
- **Philanthropic leaders** — move from funding individual programs/orgs to **funding systems change**.
- **Government leaders** — combine programs into **scalable initiatives** that meet problems at the scale at which they exist.

Conclusion: complex, emergent, interdependent challenges require **transformative, collaborative leaders**; by developing personal/organizational leadership capacities and learning from others practicing (and studying) cross-sector work, they can find **innovative, sustainable, and scalable** solutions.
