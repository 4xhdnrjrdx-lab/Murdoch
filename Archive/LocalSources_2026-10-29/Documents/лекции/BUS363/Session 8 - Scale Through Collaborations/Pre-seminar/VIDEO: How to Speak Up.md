## Part 1 — The personal dilemma of speaking up

Galinsky opens by saying speaking up is hard, and tells two personal stories about the risk of being either too hesitant or too assertive:

- **Too hesitant:** As new parents, he and his wife worried their baby wasn't getting enough milk. They wanted to call the pediatrician but feared looking like "neurotic parents," so they waited until the next day — by which point the baby was dehydrated. ("I should've spoken up, but I didn't.")
- **Too assertive:** Ten years earlier, his twin brother (a documentary filmmaker) got an offer from a distributor. Galinsky, a negotiations researcher, pushed for a counter-offer and helped draft it. The counter was "perfect" but "absolutely offensive" — and the company withdrew the offer entirely.

He notes people everywhere face the same dilemmas: whether to correct a boss, confront a meddling colleague, object to a friend's joke, or share deep fears with loved ones.

## Part 2 — The range of acceptable behavior

- Everyone has a **range of acceptable behavior**.
- Go **above** it (too assertive) → you get punished (fired, discredited, socially excluded).
- Fall **below** it (too hesitant) → you get ignored.
- Stay **within** it → you get rewarded.
- Crucially, this range is **not fixed** — it expands and contracts with context.

## Part 3 — Power and the "low-power double bind"

- The main factor setting the width of the range is **power**.
- **Power takes many forms:** having alternatives in a negotiation, being an immigrant in a new country, starting a new job, being a boss vs. a subordinate, or being the partner who is more invested in a relationship.
- **High power widens** the range (more freedom of action); **low power narrows** it.
- **The low-power double bind:** a low-power person is _unnoticed if silent_ but _judged/punished if they speak up_.
- Galinsky argues that much of what looks like a **gender difference** in behavior is really a **power difference**. Over ~20 years his research suggests gender-based double binds are effectively **double binds of the weak** — driven by a lack of perceived power, not biology.

## Part 4 — Expansion tool: advocacy & the "Mama Bear" effect

To expand your range, you need to _feel_ powerful and make others _believe_ you are. Tools:

1. **Advocate for others:** Research by **Hannah Riley Bowles** and **Emily Amanatullah** shows women often achieve less when negotiating for _themselves_, but achieve the **same as men** when negotiating **for others**.
2. **The "Mama Bear" effect:** standing up for others helps people find their own voice, become more assertive, and expand their range.

## Part 5 — Expansion tool: perspective-taking

- **Perspective-taking** = seeing the situation from another person's point of view.
- **The E-test:** participants draw a capital "E" on their forehead (a classic perspective-taking test). Galinsky illustrates with two photos of his former student **Erika Hall**:
  - Drawn so it's legible **to others** = perspective-taking.
  - Drawn so it's legible **only to yourself** = self-focus — which is common, especially in a crisis.
- **Watsonville bank case:** A man entered a bank in Watsonville, California, and threatened to blow it up unless he got **$2,000**. The manager didn't refuse; she took his perspective and asked _why exactly $2,000_. He explained it was to stop a friend's eviction. She reframed the "robbery" as a **loan application** and defused the situation. (Lesson: perspective-taking lets you be ambitious and assertive while staying likable.)

## Part 6 — Flexibility, allies, and asking for advice

3. **Signal flexibility (offer choices):** giving options lowers resistance.
   - **Car-sales example:** offering Option A ($24,000 with a 5-year warranty) vs. Option B ($23,000 with a 3-year warranty) makes a sale more likely than a single take-it-or-leave-it price.
   - **Parenting example** (his sister-in-law's idea): asking a child "this shirt or that shirt?" makes getting dressed quick and resistance-free.
4. **Gain allies:** being a "Mama Bear" for others wins loyal allies.
5. **Ask for advice:** a powerful way to gain allies, especially among influential people.
   - It flatters the person asked and signals humility.
   - It solves the **self-promotion double bind** (bragging is disliked, but silence leaves you unnoticed): asking advice _about an achievement_ makes you look both competent and likable.
   - It works **even when the other person knows** you're using it — they still feel more invested in you.

## Part 7 — Expertise and passion

- Competence/expertise grants authority — but if you lack power, you must work to establish it.
- **Display passion** to come across as an expert. When people talk about their passions: pupils dilate, they smile, use large gestures, and speak faster and higher-pitched — and listeners tend to mimic these positive reactions.
- **Emotional resilience:** research by **Lizzie Wolf** (Elizabeth Baily Wolf) finds that crying at work is usually penalized — _unless_ the strong emotion is attributed to **passion for the work**, in which case it isn't punished.

## Part 8 — Conclusion: the "human comedy"

Galinsky closes with a story about his late father — a psychologist and film lover — who, at his brother's wedding, spoke about our roles in the "human comedy": the more flexible the performer, the better and fuller the performance. Core message: life assigns us roles and boundaries, but those boundaries are constantly expanding and evolving. By being a "Mama Bear," asking for advice, taking perspective, and having strong evidence and allies, anyone can expand their range of acceptable behavior — and always be able to speak up.

## Key terms & references

- **Adam Galinsky** — social psychologist; chair of the management division, Columbia Business School; researcher on negotiations, power, leadership, diversity, decision-making and ethics (co-author of _Friend & Foe_).
- **Range of acceptable behavior** — the spectrum of actions for which you're rewarded (inside) vs. punished/ignored (outside).
- **Power** — the primary driver of the range's width; comes in many forms (alternatives, status, role, investment).
- **Low-power double bind** — low-power people are ignored if silent, judged if they speak; underlies many apparent "gender" differences.
- **Mama Bear effect** — finding your voice by advocating for others.
- **Perspective-taking** — seeing a situation through another's eyes; expands your range and keeps you likable while assertive.
- **E-test** — drawing an "E" on your forehead as a perspective-taking measure (demo'd with **Erika Hall**).
- **Watsonville bank case** — perspective-taking ($2,000 question) defusing a bank "robbery" into a "loan."
- **Signal flexibility** — offering choices to lower resistance (car warranty options; "this shirt or that shirt").
- **Asking for advice** — builds allies and solves the **self-promotion double bind** (look competent _and_ likable).
- **Self-promotion double bind** — you're disliked for bragging but unnoticed if silent.
- **Passion / expertise** — displaying genuine passion signals expertise; passion-attributed emotion is not penalized.
- **Researchers cited:** Hannah Riley Bowles & Emily Amanatullah (advocacy/gender in negotiation); Lizzie / Elizabeth Baily Wolf (emotion & passion at work).
