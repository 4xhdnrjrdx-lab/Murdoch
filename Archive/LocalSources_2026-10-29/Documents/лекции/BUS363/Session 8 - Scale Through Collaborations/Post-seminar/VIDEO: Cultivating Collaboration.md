## Part 1 — The tale of two chickens: Green Zone vs. Red Zone

A research story from **Purdue University** comparing two groups of hens, used to show collaboration's effect on productivity:

- **Green Zone chickens:** collaborative groups that simply got along — no "star performers," but healthy, productive, mutually supportive hens.
- **Red Zone chickens:** groups of aggressive "star performers" who laid the most eggs — but achieved status by **suppressing others through pecking**.
- **The experiment:** after five generations of breeding for those traits, the **Green Zone** group's egg production **rose 260%**, while **half** the **Red Zone** hens were killed by their flockmates (pecked to death).
- **Lesson:** hostile, internally competitive "Red Zone" environments feed on themselves like a virus; supportive "Green Zone" environments produce far better results.

## Part 2 — From labor strikes to collaborative skills

Drawing on Tamm's **25 years** as a judge mediating collective-bargaining disputes and labor strikes:

- People locked in high-conflict strikes often feel exactly like the Red Zone chickens — trapped in a hostile existence.
- Most are good people who don't lack the _desire_ to collaborate — they **lack the skills**.
- Teaching collaborative skills to adversarial groups cut measurable conflict in nearly **100 organizations by almost 70%**, saving millions and improving working lives.
- Key point: a company **can't compete externally if it can't first collaborate internally**.

## Part 3 — How defensiveness damages intelligence

The single biggest factor in resolving conflict and building collaboration is **managing your own defensiveness**.

- **The "stupidity" effect:** when you get defensive, thinking becomes rigid and your **IQ drops about 20 points** — you effectively "become stupid" and lose the ability to solve problems.
- **Contagion:** defensiveness spreads — one defensive person invites the whole room into it, producing a Red Zone where nothing gets solved (Tamm jokingly calls that state "litigation").
- **Unconscious takeover:** defensiveness is an unconscious "operating system" that takes over fast — "we don't have defenses; they have us."

## Part 4 — The three core fears behind defensiveness

We don't really defend against other people — we defend against **fears inside us** we don't want to feel. The three big ones:

1. **Significance**
2. **Competence**
3. **Likability**

- **Self-deception:** defensiveness hides these fears from ourselves. Tamm's metaphor: **"putting whipped cream on dog poop"** — using defensive behavior (e.g. blaming others) to avoid feeling incompetent or unlikable.

## Part 5 — Build an early-warning system

Because we're often unaware of the internal fear until too late, watch for **outward physical signs** as an early-warning system:

- faster breathing, talking louder, feeling misunderstood or "put upon," flooding others with information to prove a point, or wanting the last word.
- **Denial trap:** defensiveness is universal — anyone who thinks they don't get defensive is probably in denial. Ask a spouse or kids to help spot your specific signs.

## Part 6 — Five practical steps to recover

1. **Acknowledge:** admit to yourself you're getting defensive.
2. **Slow down your physiology:** deep breaths, a walk, fresh air — calm the body.
3. **Monitor self-talk:** consciously turn toxic/negative self-talk into something more positive.
4. **Create an action step** tailored to your signs (e.g. if you over-talk when defensive, "zip it up" and stay quiet for 15 seconds).
5. **Recovery, not perfection:** this is a **recovery model** — when you inevitably get defensive, notice it, act, let it go, and start over.
