## Part 2 — Core framework and strategy

- Metaphor: raising money is like "sex, relationships & money" — complicated but needn't be; people fear asking, and VCs often protect its opacity.
- **Typical funding roadmap:** Angel/Seed/Accelerator → Equity (VC, Growth Equity, or PE by stage) → Public Company (IPO).
- **Alternative methods:** **Bootstrapping** (Skok calls it "the best one of all" — he bootstrapped his first business to **$20M+** and profitability before raising VC); **Crowdfunding** via **AngelList** (initial funding) and **Kickstarter** (products/services, validating demand).
- **When NOT to raise:** if you're risk-averse (taking others' money creates pressure and puts them at risk); **lifestyle businesses** — e.g. **SAS Institute**, a ~$2B business (Jim Goodnight) built without external capital.

## Part 3 — Business stages and the perfect pitch

- Stages: **Ideation → Confirmation → Creation → Validation → Repeatability** (consistent with the deck's fuller 8-stage value↑/risk↓ model).
- **Two great times to raise:** on **Potential** (brand-new venture, nothing yet disproved) or on **Proof** (data points / a clear "vector" toward a valuable company).
- Investors look for **decreasing risk + increasing value**. By Series B/C/D show **customer validation** and **references**; later stages need a clear **go-to-market** (defensible beachheads) and **business model**; a public company needs a **financial model** showing leverage (high value for low investment).

## Part 4 — Validating the problem (value proposition)

- #1 early-stage mistake: building a product without validating a real need.
- **Value proposition:** define a problem so well a customer says "I'd pay to have that solved."
- **Customer money is the best money** — if the pain is real, ask customers to invest in building the product with you.
- Quote (attributed to GE innovation): "A problem well-defined is half solved."
- **Series A crunch:** (referencing Supertramp's _Crisis? What Crisis?_) lots of "spray and pray" seed money exists, but not enough follow-on Series A capital — use seed money to prove enough value to justify the next round.

## Part 5 — Human capital and team building

- **Team/Management** is the most-often-missing slide. In tech, "IP walks out the door every night."
- Investors check whether a founder can build around their own strengths/weaknesses.
- **Human capital is the most important capital you raise.** Secure a significant **option pool** to hire top talent — agree it upfront so you don't renegotiate later with investors.

## Part 6 — Deep dive into funding sources

- **Angel (Maya, Common Angels):** typically $1,000s to a few $millions. **Angel networks** (e.g. Common Angels, Tech Coast Angels) bring domain expertise (product design, channels, distribution) and structure deals so Series A investors can follow on.
- **Seed (Jeffrey Beir):** "spray and pray" = too much capital to mentor; "seed-to-A" = investors who commit to Series A if milestones are hit. **Path selection:** a $50M business or a $1B business? Raising only $3–5M and exiting for $100–300M is great for the owner but may not interest VCs.
- **Accelerators (Reed Sturtevant, Techstars):** investors, not vendors/donors. Techstars takes ~**6% equity** for a small check (~**$18,000**) and a 3-month program [figures as stated — verify]. Value = mentorship + network. Over two years, Techstars Boston backed **52 companies**; **<2%** of applicants get in. For every $1 Techstars invested in the 2011 cohort, ~**100×** was invested afterward by follow-on investors. [Accelerator-stated metrics — verify.]
- **Non-profit / non-equity (Carmichael Roberts):** find one credible, passionate philanthropist as the "anchor" to help explain the mission; use creative finance (strategic partnering, government support). Examples: **480 Biomedical** (stents), solar ventures.
- **Strategic / corporate investing:** e.g. **VMware, Intel.** Warning — corporate agendas often want "free R&D" or to hedge against a startup's better idea; startup agendas want brand credibility + market access. Tip: **close the commercial deal first, then take the investment**, to keep leverage.

## Part 7 — The VC relationship ("the dating game")

- 2×2 matrix: **Potential vs Capital Intensity.** High potential + high capital = ideal for VC (e.g. medical trials, industry-changing plays).
- **Acquia** — started in North Bridge's offices with no capital and six months' work to find a business model.
- **Selecting a VC:** operating background vs investment professional (different founders need different advice — Rich D'Amore's "hardest question" approach forced Skok to prioritise). Check the firm has **free reserves** to follow on (some ran out of capital in 2008).
- **Pitch process:** research geography/sector/stage/partner; **the hook** = first connection is "Who, What, Why" (don't spew the whole plan); build **momentum** by sharing progress ("here's what I said I'd do, here's how it turned out").
- **Diligence & term sheets:** VCs make **blind reference** calls (people you didn't list). An early term sheet carries less certainty than a post-diligence one — keep the process **competitive**; beware firms forcing **exclusivity** too early.

## Part 8 — Financial strategy and valuation

- **How much to raise — ~18 months of runway:** ~3 months to raise + ~15 months of "doing," so you're not out of cash when the next raise starts.
- **Realistic optimist:** don't promise $0→$50M in 3 years (almost no one does — rare examples **Compaq**, **Salesforce**; **<0.0001%** of companies) [rhetorical stat — verify]. Show upside on realistic assumptions. Raising more than needed is viable if money is cheap and you want a cushion.
- **Valuation** — Skok calls it the "least important question" (1% of Google beats 100% of a failed company). **Valuation vectors:** think one round ahead — e.g. Series A at $10M post, to raise Series B at $30M post you likely need ~$6M revenue run-rate to justify the step-up.

## Part 9 — Case study: Steve Papa and Endeca

- Endeca began in a dorm room (**Hamilton Hall** [verify]).
- **1999 bubble:** angel financing took ~3 days; ~$4–5B/yr going into the industry, rising to ~$100B by 2000. Endeca was effectively a basic-research project; first customer (a financial-services firm) closed on a 6-week sales cycle in summer 2000.
- **2001 crash:** IT spending fell for the first time in 40 years; discretionary spend vanished; VC shrank to a third; existing investors reluctant to lead internal rounds; objection "e-commerce is dead." Endeca survived by re-casting itself as a **platform company** for business intelligence.
- **Near-death close:** by 1 Aug 2001 only ~10 weeks of cash; received a predatory "bottom-feeder" term sheet. **Luck:** an **Ampersand Ventures** partner lived next door to an **Arrow Electronics** exec who loved the demo, giving "proprietary diligence" and confidence.
- **9/11:** the deal was set to close the week of 9/11; despite chaos and "force majeure" clauses, **Ampersand stayed committed** (a partner reportedly drove from Minneapolis to Boston because planes were grounded) and closed the following week.
- **Exit:** in 2011 (after the US lost its triple-A rating and amid Europe's crisis), Papa exited to **Oracle** — a **60-day** process from first call to announcement, with no banker.

## Part 10 — Q&A and practical advice

- **Hiring on a budget:** sell the opportunity and yourself — people invest their scarcest resource (time), so they must believe in you.
- **"Sinatra store" analogy:** Endeca's breakthrough — instead of a deep manual taxonomy (users going ~30 levels deep, ~0% success), they inverted relational-database logic to surface a "Sinatra store" via relevant guided questions (faceted search).
- **Exit calculus:** managing an acquisition means protecting human capital as much as the company; Endeca chose Oracle as best for its people and survival.
