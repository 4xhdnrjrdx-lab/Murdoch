## The unifying framework: the lean management system (four integrated disciplines)

Every article in the compendium returns to the same core model. The lean management system is a people-centred, continuous-improvement system articulated through **four integrated, mutually reinforcing disciplines**:

1. **Connecting strategy, goals, and meaningful purpose** ("Connect") — every employee understands how their work relates to the organization's vision; everyone is fully engaged in meeting individual goals and targets.
2. **Delivering value efficiently to the customer** ("Deliver") — employees adapt day-to-day capacity to changes in demand; each day starts with a formal team discussion of yesterday's results and today's work.
3. **Discovering better ways of working** ("Discover") — best practices are systematically identified, shared, and improved; everyone is actively engaged in catching errors and defects before they reach customers.
4. **Enabling people to lead and contribute to their fullest potential** ("Enable") — the organization develops people so they can surpass performance expectations; managers understand their primary role is to lead and develop their teams.

The key claim: no single discipline stays strong for long unless the others strengthen too. To deliver more value, a company must discover new ways of working, which requires people to develop new capabilities consistent with strategy and purpose. Survey evidence (McKinsey _Quarterly_ transformational change survey, Nov 2014; n = 1,624 transformations) shows that implementing more of the disciplines' eight underlying elements dramatically raises the chance of _sustaining_ improvement over time (success rate rises steeply from 1 element implemented up to all 8).

## Article 1 — The continuous improvement leader: Engaging people for a digital age

_Author: Zachary Surak (opening framing piece)_

Core argument: lean management thinking, born in carmaking, now drives better work everywhere — from the largest companies to start-ups, across healthcare, IT, financial services, and nonprofits. But in an era of constant disruption (digitization, automation, analytics, design thinking all competing for leaders' attention), even successful organizations find it harder to keep building on lean's gains. A few organizations grasp what "continuous-improvement culture" really demands: the practices that _support_ continuous improvement must _themselves_ improve continuously.

Why engaging people matters: organizations are fighting commoditization. Competitive advantage from technical excellence alone is now fleeting — e.g. automobile defect rates have plummeted even as complexity rose, so quality barely differs across brands; US customer-satisfaction gaps among the largest retailers, food makers, banks, and appliance makers are small. What increasingly matters is human capability in how companies communicate and work with customers (a single bad service encounter, filmed on a phone, can reach hundreds of millions on social media). Lasting advantage comes from learning faster, responding faster, and building deeper customer ties.

On technology: the McKinsey Global Institute estimates at least 30% of activities in about 60% of occupations could be automated, potentially freeing people for higher-value work. But technology alone won't fix a competitive hole — when leaders fixate on the latest algorithm/big-data app/straight-through-processing platform, the tools take on a life of their own and dissipate impact. The organizations that win are those that best manage the humans who shape, use, and revise new capabilities daily.

New value from lean management: leaders double down on lean to build operating models that deploy human skill with agility and responsiveness _and_ stay focused on delivering value, so teams can respond to problems in real time rather than waiting for decisions to climb a bureaucratic hierarchy. Lean informs everything from **transforming customer journeys** (the cumulative steps across touchpoints and channels in providing a service/product) to accelerating value creation as part of a digital-era strategy.

Examples cited: **ING** (Dutch banking group) uses lean principles to create new customer product experiences and an unusually flexible, constantly evolving organizational model. **Commonwealth Bank of Australia** changed how it structures change so people adapt faster. Corporate functions traditionally seen as poor lean ground (HR, risk, finance) are just as ripe and can multiply benefits by encoding lean into governance and people processes. **Seyfarth Shaw** (Chicago international law firm) uses lean to help traditionally siloed experts align.

Leaders building leaders: the most striking difference is how leaders work — they become role models _and_ anchors that keep the organization from sliding back to old habits. As lean matures, they transfer the scientific mind-set to other leaders, codifying and role-modeling practices such as **"standard work."** The goal is a shared understanding of lean's purpose, with rigorous planning, positioning continuous improvement not as a thing to achieve but as a self-reinforcing way of thinking and working. The result is a virtuous cycle: more engaged workers, coached by more capable managers, in more agile organizations that make more effective decisions — competing through learning.

## Article 2 — Sustaining continuous improvement: Five leaders' views

_Authors: Erin Ghelber, Ted Iverson, Christian Johnson (interview compilation)_

Framing statistic: only a small minority of transformations both raise performance and sustain it over time. Five long-experience lean executives were interviewed:

- **Tom Hartman** — ~20 years adapting lean at **Autoliv** (global auto-safety-systems manufacturer); now an executive coach at **Catalysis** (healthcare training/coaching).
- **DJ Johnson** — VP for transformation at **Worthington Industries** (steelmaker); ex-McKinsey, 12 years a US Navy officer.
- **Gary Peterson** — exec advisory board of the **Shingo Institute**; EVP supply chain & production at **O.C. Tanner** (global employee-recognition company).
- **Scott Powell** — director of operational excellence at **Export Development Canada (EDC)**; leading EDC's lean transformation since 2013.
- **Rich Sheridan** — cofounder/CEO of **Menlo Innovations** (software firm that pioneered many agile/collaborative-development concepts).

Key themes drawn out:

- **Where they'd be without lean:** Peterson — likely reduced to a distribution business, losing the ability to make custom products. Powell — harder to innovate, still siloed/fragmented, less humble leaders, lower employee satisfaction. Sheridan — they were essentially doing lean without naming it, responding to accumulating problems in 1990s software (Moore's Law meant moving from individual "heroes" to teams, but teams were built on non-scalable "towers of knowledge").
- **After the intensive transformation ends:** the journey is a continuum, not an endpoint — the essence of **kaizen** (continuous improvement) is driving hundreds of thousands of small improvements at every level. "Let's run the experiment" (Menlo's motto). Never declare "mission accomplished"; never let the intensity fade.
- **Biggest challenge:** keeping people from reverting to pre-lean ways. DJ Johnson cautions against over-focusing on "projects" or kaizen "events" (useful for learning, but the big money comes from solving problems daily). Powell: they once asked the Shingo organization to assess their lean maturity and scored much lower than expected — hard to hear, but it revealed more opportunity.
- **Evolution over time:** start with tools and techniques, then move to a **principle-based approach** (Hartman) across product development, HR, finance, supply chain, operations. EDC built lean into performance measures for _every_ employee including the CEO (an "EDC Way" improvement target); the CEO's **hoshin kanri** (policy-deployment) initiatives include improving culture.
- **Engaging senior leaders:** for most C-suite people, the idea that their greatest contribution is teaching/mentoring is not natural. Worthington built a two-week **executive immersion program** culminating in a **kaizen event**. EDC changed performance reviews to incorporate lean behaviors. Hartman stresses C-suite must keep learning (humility): "How many times have you missed an opportunity to learn because you were blinded by your own knowledge?"
- **Develop from within vs. hire:** ~70% of EDC executives are promoted from within. O.C. Tanner withholds the actual job for three months — giving the candidate a no-authority cross-enterprise project and watching how well they follow lean behaviors; if they don't make the cut in three months they revert or leave. Menlo uses immersive, hands-on hiring (no traditional interviews — "two people sitting across a table lying to each other").
- **Reaching middle managers/front line:** EDC "zigged and zagged" — middle/front line first, then top-down, now re-engaging the middle. Autoliv invested top-down with messages getting more concrete near the factory floor; team leaders needed more than tools training — they needed to understand _why_ the tools exist, leading to a "Lean 201" following "Lean 101." O.C. Tanner rotates people (managers moved every ~3 years, usually to a different value stream) to support teams, not individuals.
- **Most powerful effect:** capability building; empowerment of people (from "quietly doing what they're told" to doing real strategy implementation); employee engagement; belief in / respect for people's capability. Hartman's mentor's question: "Is this a people job or a dog job?" — if people aren't changing, engage them daily so it becomes a people job.

# THEME: What leaders do — Integrate new technologies and approaches

Framing: in a lean system, leaders serve as role models for new ways of working, seeing improvement opportunities for the whole system. Note the parallels between agile (software) and lean (manufacturing/services): both center on creating customer value, steady flow, and continuous improvement. This theme's articles cover digital + operations, ING's agile model, two-speed customer journeys, the next-gen operating model, and RPA.

## Article 3 — Transforming operations management for a digital world

_Authors: Albert Bollard, Alex Singla, Rohit Sood, Jasper van Ouwerkerk_

Thesis: combining digital innovation with operations-management discipline lifts performance higher, faster, and at greater scale than either alone. Even as organizations shift investment from traditional operations toward digital, operations management won't disappear — it becomes more important, but a _new_ kind that offers both stability and the agility/responsiveness digital demands.

Two reasons operations management still matters: (1) for the next several years, exploiting digital will still depend on people, and early data suggest human skills become _more_ critical, not less, as automated tasks commoditize (a "cutting-edge" feature like smartphone claims submission quickly becomes ubiquitous; advantage shifts to human capacity — thoughtful retirement advice, calm post-accident guidance). (2) Building people's capabilities — as machines take on complex work, the human role changes (e.g., a claims adjuster moves from using software, to improving it, to making it more predictive), requiring constant experimentation/testing/learning.

Digital needs operations management _and_ vice versa: dashboards let leaders adjust workloads instantly; automated analysis frees managers to spend more time with teams. The biggest breakthroughs require embracing digital innovation and operations discipline simultaneously. Example: a large North American property-and-casualty insurer cut call-center demand by **30%** in under a year via a revamped digital channel, plus an additional **25%** workload reduction through better call-center team management.

**Four major shifts required:**

1. **Digital and analog reinforcing each other** — find where each contributes most; don't let digitization eliminate productive human intervention. Example: a B2B data-services provider (white-glove model) enlisted customers and frontline employees to decide which custom reports could become self-serve automated products; developers worked in agile **sprints**, the front line taught customers and gathered feedback, producing an ongoing dialogue that lets the firm develop/test/roll out almost any automated report fast.
2. **Driving digital enterprise-wide** — building a product isn't enough. A global bank launched an online portal but most customers stuck to branch habits; the whole bank had to reorient (modifying roles from tellers to investment advisers, new communications, new feedback loops). Result: portal use up **70%** in months; onboarding new customers **60%** faster; employee engagement improved.
3. **Realigning from the customer back** — redesign internal roles around how customers actually work with the firm. A major European asset manager's manual payment/payout processes were a set of tiny silos (document review, payment processing) unrelated to customer goals. Fix: assign a single owner per **"customer journey"** (e.g., "I've retired and want my annuity to start paying out"), responsible for digital elements _and_ working with operations. Result: handoffs cut by **>90%**, cycle times more than halved, total capacity effectively doubled.
4. **Making better leaders through digital** — digital's speed demands stronger day-to-day leader-team skills. Two years after a transformation, the same P&C insurer saw cracks (competitors catching up, performance slipping, manager stress/turnover): the daily huddles, problem-solving sessions, and check-ins had become ritualized. Digital let leaders recommit — an online portal gave a central view of leadership activities at all levels; master calendars prioritized on-the-ground work; redefined daily-measured targets per management tier raised engagement and frontline retention.

Critical leader questions: Do we really understand how customers interact with us now and want to in future? How do we give the experience they want across digital + human channels? How would a "faster metabolism" uncover new performance opportunities?

## Article 4 — ING's agile transformation (interview)

_Interviewer: Deepak Mahadevan; interviewees: Peter Jacobs (CIO, ING Netherlands) and Bart Schlatmann (then-COO, ING Netherlands)_

Context: In summer 2015 ING shifted from a traditional organization to an **"agile" model** inspired by **Google, Netflix, and Spotify**. Comprised ~**350 "squads" in 13 "tribes."** Outcomes: improved time to market, higher employee engagement, increased productivity.

**Why change:** no financial imperative (company was performing well) — but customer behavior was changing fast with new digital channels, and expectations were being set by digital leaders in _other_ industries. ING needed to stop thinking about product marketing and start understanding **customer journeys** in an omnichannel environment (seamless cross-channel service). Agile was the means to deliver that strategy.

**Definition of agility (Schlatmann):** flexibility and the ability to rapidly adapt/redirect; minimizing handovers and bureaucracy; empowering people; building stronger, more rounded professionals. It rests on the **"end-to-end principle"** and **multidisciplinary squads** (mix of marketing, product/commercial specialists, UX designers, data analysts, IT engineers) focused on client needs and united by a common definition of success.

**Four pillars of the transformation (Jacobs):**

1. The agile way of working itself — IT and commercial colleagues sit together in squads, constantly testing offerings.
2. The right organizational structure and clear new roles/governance — as long as old departments, steering committees, project managers/directors persist, silos remain and agility suffers.
3. **DevOps** and continuous delivery in IT — aspiration to release software every two weeks rather than 5–6 "big launches" a year; integrating product development with IT operations helped make ING the #1 mobile bank in the Netherlands.
4. A new **people model** — in the old model, a manager's status/salary tracked project size and headcount; in agile there are no projects as such, and what matters is how people deal with knowledge and the mix of knowledge/expertise layers.

**ING's agile org model (exhibit — no fixed structure, constantly evolves):**

- **Squad** — basis of the new organization; ≤9 people; self-steering and autonomous; cross-functional representatives in a single location; **end-to-end responsibility** for a client-related objective; can change functional composition as the mission evolves; dismantled once the mission is executed.
- **Tribe** — a collection of squads with interconnected missions; ~150 people on average; the **tribe lead** sets priorities, allocates budgets, and interfaces with other tribes so knowledge/insights are shared.
- **Chapter** — develops expertise/knowledge across squads; the **chapter lead** owns one chapter and represents the hierarchy for squad members (personal development, coaching, staffing, performance management).
- **Product owner** — a squad _member_ (not its leader); coordinates squad activities, manages the backlog/to-do lists/priorities.
- **Agile coach** — coaches individuals and squads to create high-performing teams.

**Scope & timeline:** Started with ~3,500 staff at group HQ (marketing, product, channel management, IT development). Support functions (HR, finance, risk), branches, call centers, operations, and IT infrastructure were initially left out of the tribe/squad shift — but they still adopt agility differently (self-steering teams in operations/call centers, inspired by **Zappos**; daily team stand-ups in sales/branch network; legal/finance/operational risk stay independent but squads can call on them). About **8–9 months** from written strategy/vision (late 2014) to the new HQ organization being implemented — preceded by two months and five board off-sites to develop the target "nervous system," plus 5–6 pilot squads.

**Other notable practices:**

- Three-week onboarding (Zappos-inspired): every employee spends a full week at the Customer Loyalty Team call center taking customer calls.
- **Peer-to-peer hiring** (Google-inspired): teams select hires; leaders have only a right of veto (never exercised across thousands of hires — a sign the system works); teams now better diversified by gender, character, skill set.
- Office redesign: tore down walls for open spaces and informal interaction; few formal meetings — "more a tech campus than an old-style bank."
- Re-establishing an **engineering culture** in IT (inspired by a Google I/O conference) — make it good to be an engineer and write code again; engineering craftsmanship drives careers. ING came to see itself as "a technology company operating in financial services," learning from real tech firms (Facebook, Google, Netflix, Spotify, Uber) rather than other banks — what they share is a way of working, small teams united by purpose, an agile "manifesto," close customer interaction, constant reshaping of work.
- **Spotify** especially inspired cross-silo collaboration (visits to Sweden became a two-way exchange).
- Glue without traditional reporting lines: squads write down their purpose and how they'll measure client impact; tribes add **scrums, portfolio-wall planning, daily stand-ups**, and the **QBR (quarterly business review)** (borrowed from Google and Netflix) — each tribe records quarterly achievements, biggest learning, celebrates successes _and_ failures, sets next-quarter aims and which tribe/squad to link with; QBR docs are open and shared transparently. Everything is managed daily and made transparent on office walls (which reassured regulators that agile ≠ chaos).
- **Selection:** In Jan 2015 all HQ employees were put on "mobility" (effectively jobless) and had to reapply; the intense process weighted **culture and mind-set** above knowledge/experience. ~40% of the 2,500 ended up in different positions; some good-knowledge people were lost for lacking the right mind-set ("knowledge can be easily regained"). Age was not a key differentiator — many "old guard" adapted faster than younger staff.
- **Impact (15 months):** quicker to market, higher engagement, fewer impediments/handovers, better client experience; software releases every 2–3 weeks; **Net Promoter Score** and employee-engagement scores up multiple points; working with **INSEAD** to measure metrics as a neutral outsider.
- **Risks (Jacobs):** (1) over-focus on shipping software risks making all innovation incremental — must organize for more disruptive innovation. (2) Product-owner autonomy risks squads drifting apart unless aligned (e.g., quarterly/semiannually) to strategic priorities.
- **Advice (Schlatmann):** agility is a means, not an end — start with "Why agile? What's the broader purpose?" with a clear, compelling, all-in reason backed by the whole leadership team. Then ask "What are you willing to give up?" — they gave up traditional hierarchy, formal meetings, overengineering, detailed planning, and "input steering" in exchange for empowered teams, informal networks, and "output steering."

## Article 5 — Speed and scale: Unlocking digital value in customer journeys

_Authors: Driek Desmet, Shahar Markovitch, Christopher Paquette_

Thesis: digitizing at scale needs a distinct structure that lets organizations digitize their most important customer experiences consistently — at scale _and_ speed — alongside pragmatic short-term moves. Consumers abandon apps after one or two failed tries, so companies get only one or two chances to win adoption. Customers don't want digital versions of yesterday's bureaucratic, multi-step processes; the answer is to reimagine and digitize entire **"customer journeys"** (beginning-to-end processes across whichever channels customers choose).

**How many journeys? (sidebar)** A small number of core journeys typically covers ~**80% of customer interaction** and ~**50% of the workforce**. Industry patterns: **banks** 10–20 core journeys (account opening/onboarding, payments, mortgages, service requests like lost PIN codes, credit-card issuance); **life & retirement** 10–20 (account opening/enrollment, onboarding, servicing, guidance); **telecom** 8–15 (mobile postpaid sales, customer-care, fixed-line provisioning, network repair, prepaid top-ups); **electrical utilities** fewer than 10 (sign-up, payment, meter reading, change of address).

Example results (Exhibit 1): telecom faster mobile sign-ups raised satisfaction **+20%** and cut costs **30%**; a European lender cut account opening/loan approval from days to minutes, raised customer-engagement touchpoints from once a month to 3–4×/week, and cut IT release time from a year to a month. Benchmarks: loan top-up approval ~5 days → 1 minute (≈7,000×); account opening 2 days → 5–10 minutes (≈300×); engagement touchpoints ~5–10/yr → ~150–250 (≈20×); time to release a major feature 13 months → 1 month (≈13×).

**Six critical, parallel shifts** for manageable, predictable digitization at scale:

1. **Start with your story** — a consistent "enterprise customer experience story" distilling strategy, brand, positioning into practical guidelines. Example: a North American bank's focus groups identified **accessibility** and **flexibility** as priorities → became the story's theme; possible journeys were run through filters (strategic/CX value, economies of scale, regulatory/tech hurdles, resource readiness) producing a prioritized road map; top priority became a new onboarding process letting customers open a "relationship" without naming a specific product.
2. **Sequence your tech transformation** — IT changes are the hardest. Designing a one-off mobile app is easy; the challenge is linking it to all channels and back-end systems (authentication, credit scoring, postsale servicing). Resist two temptations: digitizing each journey separately (recreates silos) and over-investing in channel-specific IT. Identify shared **horizontal components** (BPM layers, central admin platforms, externally facing channels) and standard reusable components (eSignature, authentication, document scanning/data extraction). One firm used onboarding as a test case — modernizing common BPM architecture, building reusable e-archiving/authentication, and an extra interface layer so later back-end services could plug in.
3. **Turn, shift, accelerate, and repeat** — leading platforms update OSs yearly with upgrades every few months (some daily/hourly), a cultural shift for regulated incumbents used to perfectionism. Adopt the tech-industry **MVP (minimum viable product)** — managing the tension between "minimum" and "viable." A digital product with only 80% of the analog's features can still win by being 10–20× faster; treat digitization as a continual process of finding the right 80% that helps customers now.
4. **Build talent — and your digital "factory"** — acquire digitally-oriented talent and streamline governance/budgeting. Solutions seen: acquiring tech companies (retailer), using a digital agency + hiring + training (telco), an internal academy teaching leadership/digital/execution (bank). Borrow lean's **"work cell"**: assemble all specialties for an end-to-end process (sales, underwriting, credit analysis, document production for mortgages) into one team; people may still report into their functions but get daily feedback from the work cell. A Southeast Asian telco reworked HR to create a clear path into work cells; ~a dozen specialists grew into a full **digital factory** that quadrupled program capacity (monthly cadence → weekly).
5. **Create a "game plan" to guide the factory** — the digital factory works like a design firm + software hothouse using **design thinking, zero-based process reengineering, and agile development**. A "game plan" is a set of standard operating guidelines/methodologies (deliverables, governance steps, escalation rules). It should: (a) set out _questions_ per stage rather than fixed answers; (b) provide templates for key artifacts (market-research summaries, CX design, economic modeling, interface mock-ups); (c) evolve through testing in a small-scale factory. One UK organization tested its game plan across two business units, cutting stakeholder friction and enabling new journey transformations in weeks instead of months.
   - **Approaches for execution (sidebar):** (A) one or two **"demonstration events"** to build momentum (when priority journeys are clear but cultural resistance is high); (B) a **full program** to build foundational capabilities (when there's a "burning platform" + executive belief — guard against "everything is a priority" and insufficient resources); (C) engage a **third-party vendor for build-operate-transfer** (when speed is paramount and internal ramp-up is too slow — one institution had a vendor supply half of each transformation while recruiting/training to finish the rest).
6. **Track it all the way** — metrics must change (short-term ROI can discourage risk-taking; reward managers who reassign talent to high-growth areas rather than count direct reports), reporting must speed up (real-time dashboards, network-operations-center style), and data feeds rapid test/revision cycles. A manufacturer built a single enterprise-wide real-time dashboard (batch quality, inventory, FTEs) so leaders could divert resources and fly in experts; transparency created constant tension to deliver.

**Putting it together — one of Europe's largest banks:** initial story focused on relieving retail customers' "irritating service requests" (lost debit cards, forgotten PINs). A small cross-functional team used standardized components to build a mobile solution in **six weeks**, redesigning commercial-client onboarding from ~**70 days to ~25 days** (Exhibits 2 & 3). The team grew into a combined UX center + digital factory of **100+ specialists**; the bank completed five of its most important journeys, the factory now runs two major journeys simultaneously (each 4–5 months), and customers report dramatically better experience and engagement.

## Article 6 — How to start building your next-generation operating model

_Authors: Joao Dias, David Hamilton, Somesh Khanna, Christopher Paquette, Rohit Sood_

Thesis: the operating model of the future combines digital technologies and process-improvement capabilities in an integrated, sequenced way to improve customer journeys and internal processes and radically reduce costs. Examples: a North American bank shifted **30%** of in-branch traffic to digital in under two years; a European cruise line relaunched five core products in nine months, lifting digital conversions **3–5×** and sales **150%**. Successful companies do two things well: put in place the **building blocks** of change, and select a **transformation path** suited to their situation.

**Four building blocks:**

1. **Autonomous, cross-functional teams anchored in customer journeys, products, and services** — fluid structures, smaller teams cutting across business lines/segments, empowered to own products/journeys and run experiments; building skills via "anchor hires," rotational and "train the trainer" programs, and weekly capability building. Insurers dismantle traditional claims/underwriting units and embed experts (lawyers, nurses) into service groups working daily with technologists. One credit-card company shifted IT from alignment around systems to alignment with **value streams**, lifting software-development productivity **200%** in three months.
2. **Flexible, modular architecture, infrastructure, and software delivery** — modular, reusable tech; BPM tools and external channels shared across journeys; cloud migration for systems that must move faster; heavy investment in **DevOps** to automate testing, security, delivery, and infrastructure changes.
3. **A management system that cascades clear strategies and goals with tight feedback loops** — principles, tools, and behaviors driving continuous improvement; top-line goals translated into specific metrics/KPIs at all levels; near-real-time performance management with joint incentives. Example: a North American insurer couldn't get frontline claims adjusters to adopt central predictive-analytics models, so it created daily feedback sessions between the central team and adjusters and embedded analytics specialists into customer-service teams, with shared customer-value goals — shortening cycle times and freeing leaders to coach.
4. **Agile, customer-centric culture at all levels, role-modeled from the top** — prioritize speed/execution over perfection, willingness to take appropriate risk. A credit-card company put law/compliance staff in with marketing for daily issue-resolution; law/compliance adopted agile methods, targeting **90%** reductions in time to market for some core processes while cutting operational risk. Senior leaders act as vocal champions and hold themselves accountable for value.

**Four transformation paths (a guide for the first 12 months; firms often migrate between them):**

- **Innovation outpost** — a dedicated unit fully separate from the main business's culture, bureaucracy, and infrastructure; creates inspiring products and new business models. A retailer's outpost drove next-gen analytics, CX focus, and the mobile interface, creating buzz and attracting talent. Works when executive alignment is limited, market pressure demands speed, and legacy-culture challenges are large; less effective as the "tip of the spear" for culture change and often low ROI. (Traditionally a common first move, now less common.)
- **Fenced-off digital factory** — a high-autonomy group partnering with businesses/functions, housing specialized capability groups (robotics, analytics) deployed to specific journeys; models new ways of working and integrates capabilities into the main business. One European bank built a factory in a campus building — lower floors per journey, top floor for reusable components (customer identification/verification, eSignature). The **most common starting point**; works with broad commitment to transformation and a need to incubate critical-mass internal capabilities; risks a "have/have not" split and needs significant C-suite support/funding.
- **Business-unit accelerator** — a scaled-down factory inside a single business unit, building its own process-redesign/robotics skills with control over investments (no central funding needed). A North American bank moved to this after its first few years, gaining control and closer customer/business-strategy connection. Works for large independent business units or where one unit is far ahead; must mitigate complexity/cost from divergent tools and harder cross-org capability sharing.
- **Full-scale evolution** — comprehensive reorganization around major journeys (the natural model for digital natives). Results measured by value streams and journeys; daily/weekly conversations about the next most important work. Initially like "shock treatment" but shakes up the traditional management system and achieves culture change quickly at scale. Works with a broad top-down mandate and no near-term economic pressure; must ensure best practices and scarce resources are shared and coordinated with IT.

**No-regret steps:** create clarity on enterprise strategy and where digital quickly enables value; challenge the board to be explicit about the transformation's importance and investment; build top-team excitement via visits to digital natives/incumbents; assess management-system maturity via benchmarking; invest in targeted capability building (esp. the top ~50 leaders) in digitization, agile, design thinking, advanced analytics; honestly assess talent vs. peers and cross-sector leaders; survey the cross-sector landscape; and assess how much change the organization can realistically absorb.

## Article 7 — The next acronym you need to know about: RPA (robotic process automation) (interview)

_Interviewer: Xavier Lhuer; interviewee: Leslie Willcocks (professor, London School of Economics)_

**Definition:** **RPA "takes the robot out of the human."** It's software that mimics a human carrying out a task within a process — doing repetitive, rule-based work more quickly, accurately, and tirelessly, freeing people for tasks needing human strengths (emotional intelligence, reasoning, judgment, customer interaction).

**Four streams of RPA** — described as a highly customized type plus a "three-lane motorway": (1) highly customized software for specific process types (e.g., accounting/finance); slow lane = **screen/web scraping**; middle lane = a **self-development kit** with templates configured by specialist programmers for a specific org; fast lane = **enterprise/enterprise-safe software** that scales and is reusable. RPA is "lightweight" — little IT involvement, business-operations people can learn to configure/apply robots; it addresses only the **presentation layer**, not the business logic or data-access layer.

**RPA vs. cognitive intelligence:** RPA handles simpler, physical, rule-codifiable tasks; **cognitive automation** impinges on a human's knowledge base — natural language, reasoning, judgment, context, meaning, insight. RPA is a mature technology; cognitive automation is not yet (few powerful tools/companies using them).

**Business benefits:** across **16 case studies**, ROI of **30–200% in the first year**, but it's a mistake to look only at short-term labor savings. Multiple benefits: cheap/fast compliance capability in regulated industries (insurance, banking); better customer service (freeing staff for complex questions); employee benefits (people welcomed it — they hated the tasks the machines now do; one online retailer measures success by "hours given back to the business"). Work is increasing exponentially (~50% more data each year) plus rising audit/regulation — automation relieves the stress.

**RPA and lean (sidebar):** one large financial institution built RPA into a broader lean-management solution for customer onboarding and product renewal — a small developer team released several robots in ten weeks for a **>5%** efficiency gain; onboarding time nearly halved, renewal backlogs down **>25%**, employee engagement up **>25%**.

**Example process:** one insurer used to take two days to handle 500 premium advice notes; now 30 minutes. RPA can't structure data or handle exceptions — humans structure the data at the front end (a pro forma ideal advice note) and engineers create rules for exceptions, gradually educating the RPA until it does 90–95% of the work.

**Adoption considerations (four):** (1) **Strategy** — using RPA as a broad strategic tool yields far more than tactical cost savings. (2) **Launch** — involve the C-suite, appoint a good project champion, pick the right process (stable, mature, optimized, rules-based, repetitive, usually high-volume); start with a controlled experiment on a visible bottleneck/pain point. (3) **Change management** — persuading the organization to adopt automation. (4) **Build a mature, enterprise-level RPA capability** — a **center of excellence** (usually within business operations) with people who assess feasibility, configure/install/develop robots, control switching them on/off, plus continuous-improvement capability and relationships with IT/governance/security. IT must be brought on board early (RPA's appeal to business units risks "shadow IT" disruption). Long-term, RPA means more interesting work — not whole jobs lost but parts of jobs, reassembled into different jobs. (Willcocks & Lacity are coauthors of _Service Automation: Robots and the Future of Work_, 2016.)

# THEME: How leaders do it — Drive the management system

Framing: once a new way of working is created, the danger is that it stops evolving. The management systems defining how people get work done must themselves evolve; leaders must assess and improve them. A lean expectation is that leaders **build more leaders.** This theme's articles: management-system maturity assessment, advancing lean leadership, daily management habits, lean & people (Denso), capability building at scale, and CBA's change management.

## Article 8 — Holding a mirror to the management system: How mature is it?

_Authors: Randy Cook, Stefan de Raedemaecker, Jacek Fabianowicz, Alessandra Fantoni_

Statistic: fewer than **30%** of organizations succeed in improving both performance _and_ long-term health. The difference in the successful 30%: they learn through repeated **problem-solving cycles** (identify issues → find root causes → implement countermeasures → reflect), and they understand their own weaknesses deeply enough never to be entirely satisfied — including how they improve themselves.

**How success can lead to failure** (paradoxical pitfalls):

- **Losing business purpose** — transformation increasingly happens "for transformation's sake"; a basic-materials company's diagnostic tools were so good at finding waste that people thought about where to apply the tools next rather than which challenges mattered most.
- **Focusing on tools, not ideas** — people haven't assimilated the _ideas_ behind the tools. Performance boards are the most visible evidence of a lean transformation, but what matters is the ideas they represent (measure what matters to customers/strategy; those measures define good performance; people use them to talk openly about improvement) — boards can be replaced by anything equally effective at reinforcing the same ideas.
- **Changing behavior but not mind-sets** — people often revert out of habit/instinct (old mind-sets persisting), not deliberate "resistance." One financial institution's early lean cut project time nearly in half and helped it weather a crisis, but gains eroded as leaders under stress reverted to command-and-control, solving problems _for_ teams instead of developing them; some leaders remained spectators rather than coaches. (Cites Kotter, "Leading change," HBR 1995.)
- **Building without balance** — root cause is an incomplete/unbalanced approach; the four disciplines must reinforce one another (Exhibit 1).

**How to assess a management system's maturity** — characteristics of a good assessment:

- **Comprehensive coverage** of all disciplines in equal depth (don't shortcut to suspected-weak areas).
- **Detailed standards** — for each new behavior (root-cause problem solving, systematic coaching/mentoring, in-person confirmation that processes work), provide examples of different performance levels. E.g., for root-cause problem solving: _basic_ = sessions frequently preempted by other priorities; _proficient_ = consistent schedule with senior follow-up and only rare preemption.
- **Aspirational top-level descriptions** — a "true north" perfect (unattainable) score that keeps people reexamining themselves; top-level standard = leaders always allocate sufficient time/resources so preemption never occurs.
- **Expert assessors** — must grade consistently _and_ offer practical suggestions. **Internal assessors** (from the transformation team or other parts of the org) are especially valuable — grounded in the culture, they help cross-pollinate best ideas.

**Enabling practical follow-up:** reports must lead to pragmatic action — clear implementation mechanisms so solutions disseminate; problem-solving expectations in role descriptions. Well-designed **digital innovations** help assessors evaluate and calibrate faster and make results more accessible (anyone with screen access; better data visualization), creating a sense of urgency that increases problems resolved.

**Assess, improve, repeat — the value-creation cycle:**

- _Assessment as teaching tool_ — continual exposure teaches leaders to view themselves objectively (especially with benchmarks from mature organizations; even internal unit-to-unit comparisons reveal patterns and best practices), encouraging balance across all four disciplines.
- _Examples:_ Breaking through plateaus (the basic-materials company focused its assessment on top sites to find common themes); jump-starting deeper transformation (mining/metals, industrial equipment); reinforcing an annual cycle (a European conglomerate now runs yearly per-unit assessments — Exhibit 2: an annual cycle of preparation → walkthrough by team → feedback session → improvement plan → development → automated KPI monitor → yearly scanning).
- _Recommitting to culture change:_ a large North American asset manager (5+ years building lean) launched its first systems assessment — four years post-transformation, 80% of sites showed continuing progress but 20% had stagnated, and some "progress" was more apparent than real (sites reverting to boosting results "the wrong way"). It recommitted to culture change, emphasizing line leaders' responsibility to encourage problem solving — problems identified/solved more than doubled (to **>50,000/year**), and a later assessment showed customer satisfaction, employee engagement, quality, and productivity all up. (Methods based on McKinsey's Operational Excellence Index.)

## Article 9 — Advancing lean leadership

_Author: Alison Jenkins_

Thesis: transforming performance usually means changing culture, which means leaders must change how they lead. Leaders have more years of old habits to unlearn and need sustained support — typically an **integrated learning journey** building understanding, conviction, and ability.

**Three fundamental behavioral shifts (exhibit):**

1. **From providing the right answer → asking the right questions.** Rests on three lean foundations: everyone at every level builds new capabilities; the people closest to a problem understand it best; a leader's primary role is to coach. Leaders often see their value as providing answers; learning to listen, reflect, and trust the team takes practice. (One US executive let her team try their ideas "so long as I'm there to give them the guidance," then realized her _questions_ were more valuable than her answers.)
2. **From looking for immediate fixes → digging for root causes.** Unsolved problems return, creating more waste; root-cause solving is demanding for busy leaders. A utility construction supervisor noted every minute off service calls is unrewarded work — but the utility built problem elimination into everyone's performance-development plans (frontline rewarded for solving problems, leaders rewarded for building problem-solving capability).
3. **From setting general goals for everyone → connecting the organization's goals to individuals' work.** Translate purpose and objectives into daily targets; understand how each person's work contributes to ambitions _and_ understand each person's own goals (work is more engaging when it has personal meaning).

**How to build better leaders:**

- _Understanding the need to change_ — external/internal "go-and-sees," but critically help **senior** leaders see how _they_ must change (not just front line). One firm starts every executive-learning journey with a diagnostic on current leadership performance (setting direction, solving problems, developing people).
- _Helping leaders learn_ — adults retain ~10% of lecture content but ~two-thirds of what they learn by doing, so programs mix prework, group sessions, and fieldwork with experienced internal coaches; some pair a technical-expertise coach with an executive coach.
- _Building supporting infrastructure_ — create transparency into how leaders spend time; adjust the formal talent system (competency models, ratings, development, compensation, promotions) to reward desired behaviors. One conglomerate made **51%** of a leader's annual evaluation behavioral, and ran a monthly all-employee pulse survey checking whether everyone got the standard two hours of one-on-one coaching monthly (results discussed in monthly management meetings).
- _Following good examples_ — leaders need visible role models. The CEO is an obvious focal point, but a McKinsey survey found the real differentiator in successful transformations is the engagement of **line managers and frontline employees**, not the CEO. (Cites Bucy/Hall/Yakola, "Transformation with a capital T," and "The people power of transformations.") One North American company started leadership-transformation deployments at VP/director levels so those leaders become role models before cascading down.

## Article 10 — Continuous improvement: make good management every leader's daily habit

_Authors: Andy Eichfeld, David Golding, David Hamilton, Kathy Robinson_

Thesis: continuous improvement at scale requires management discipline at scale; the obstacle is that leaders/managers haven't fundamentally changed how they lead daily. Good daily management rests on a few basic disciplines: understand how people are actually delivering for customers; give regular feedback and coaching; teach people to solve problems; create a physically and emotionally safe environment for meaningful dialogue.

Survey evidence: across **189,000 people at 81 organizations** (each 7,500–300,000 employees), four leader behaviors — **be supportive, focus on results, seek different perspectives, solve problems effectively** — accounted for almost **90%** of the variance in leadership quality between strong and weak organizations. The more consistently leaders turn these into a new standard ("**leader standard work**"), the more continuous improvement they achieve.

The problem: sponsors underestimate how hard these changes are, implicitly assuming leaders will figure out new routines in their "spare time" — making the effort a "new hobby, not a new habit." Without a clear link between the desired behaviors and business results, leaders revert. Two organizations are highlighted: **one of the world's largest banks** and a **multinational food manufacturer (100,000+ employees)**, both using digital/analytics to help leaders internalize routines.

**Leader standard work — four fundamental elements:**

1. **Define what leaders do — every day.** Specify behaviors very precisely for all levels. (Exhibit: a food-company plant manager's day — (1) review overnight/previous-day production, quality, maintenance reports; (2) join production manager and frontline supervisors for a shift-change walk-through; (3) attend shift huddles, visiting a different department daily; (4) mark cross-department issues for the weekly huddle and note one-on-one coaching opportunities; (5) attend a cross-functional problem-solving session on a recurring maintenance issue.) Since this fills much of the day, leaders must _stop_ doing some current things — a round of trade-off discussions where the top team protects leaders' time via synchronized calendars (a **WILO** or **MILO** — "week/month in the life") that cancel low-value activities (e.g., information-sharing meetings) in favor of real performance dialogues, problem solving, improving standard work, building skills, and coaching. At the bank, a **digital app** builds coaching sessions into calendars and development plans and prompts coach + team member to record how each went against best-practice questions, so senior leaders see gaps in real time.
2. **Help leaders use their time effectively.** Freeing schedules isn't enough — leaders need better information (rationalize data to the few critical metrics, report on a simple dashboard). "There's an enormous difference between leaders who walk the floor and leaders who walk the floor with intent." The food company moved to tablet-based reporting so executives analyze data during visits (hours, not weeks) and teams get updates in time for daily huddles.
3. **Double down on continuous improvement.** A new standard can congeal and impede the flexibility it was meant to support, so the standard itself must be continually examined and improved (sidebar: "Pause and take stock of 'leader standard work' every few months" — objective maturity assessments by unbiased examiners scoring practices against world-class benchmarks). The food company's early frontline problem-solving plateaued and felt like "empty rituals"; the real issue was leaders _up the hierarchy_ learning to ask the right questions and coach effectively (e.g., training frontline managers to see opportunity in _stagnant_ metrics, not just declining ones). A 10% downtime reduction in year one became 25% in year two, with all employees/management using the same digital app and full transparency into problems being solved.
4. **Change isn't just for everyone else — it's for you.** Leaders often think change is for other people; but past success won't keep working. Leader standard work matters most for senior executives (especially on a digital platform): it provides essential feedback on what's actually happening at every level (improving strategic/resource decisions) and guides executives' own time (high-level problem solving, developing reports, role modeling).

## Article 11 — Getting better than the tools we'd been taught: Lean and people (Denso interview)

_Authors: Erin Frackleton, Erin Ghelber, Christian Johnson; interviewee: Don Tracy (VP, Denso International America)_

Context: **Denso** (founded 1949, Japan) is one of the world's largest auto suppliers (>$40B revenue, FY ended Mar 2016; ~one-quarter from North America, 14,000+ US employees). Lean has been part of Denso's culture almost since the start; two North America units won the **Shingo Prize** (2007 and 2008). But then the pace of improvement accelerated.

Key insights from Tracy:

- Denso had long mastered the _tools_ of lean manufacturing but had a real **gap in developing leaders** — "we developed great process managers" (including himself); the process focus was limiting ("we couldn't get better than the tools we'd been taught"). The North America operation had been in a "child-parent" relationship with Japan; the aspiration became not just to follow but to lead.
- The turning point (Nov 2007): a person brought Tracy a question important to them but not to him; he was unaccommodating, then apologized and said "I really think this is something you can handle" — the person's result far exceeded expectations. From then he committed to stop managing by results. He researched **growth mind-set vs. fixed mind-set** (his old style was a fixed mind-set); a growth mind-set pushes people to think on their own and lets them struggle. In manufacturing it's all about problem solving — "you have to go through struggle to be a learning organization, not just a managing one."
- They hired industrial-coaching psychologists for **360-degree reviews**, then leadership-development classes; over four years the org transformed "from a triangle to more of a circle," establishing ownership at all levels rather than hierarchical approval.
- **HR's central role:** the head of HR sits on the weekly strategic leadership team; HR is the "voice and advocate of associates at all levels." Adopting a growth mind-set means first recognizing something isn't working ("walking through fire"), then finding insight, making changes, and morale follows.
- Managing negativity: even among 900 people, ~5 very negative long-tenured people have outsized impact; HR's answer was "communication, communication, communication," plus Tracy personally engaging negative people (the most vocal critic became the "number-one cheerleader," waving a Denso flag at a quality-breakthrough pep rally).
- Forward challenges: standardizing policies across North American plants (Tennessee, California, Mexico, Canada — a one-HR policy); avoiding complacency/fixed mind-set; developing frontline associates who now function at a former leadership level; and staying vulnerable — "willing to say 'I've made a mistake'" (the catalyst for change).

## Article 12 — Bringing out the best in people: Capability building at scale

_Authors: Stefan De Raedemaecker, Javier Feijoo, David Jacquemont, Elixabete Larrea Tamayo_

Thesis: service expectations are rising and large organizations face more (and riskier) customer interactions; the way to improve service is by adopting the four lean disciplines, with **enabling people** especially critical — at its core, a strong focus on **capability building at all levels** that becomes integral to how the business operates.

Example: a regional financial institution reached 15,000+ employees over four years; heavy capability-building investment raised customer satisfaction **+11 points**, cut the cost-income ratio **20%**, and lifted employee satisfaction (the share rating affiliation 4–5 on a 5-point scale rose **+15%**, to almost 80%).

Why capability building matters in services: **variability** — work varies in content (range of questions) and form (demand swings), and services rely on people (more variable than machines). Capability building goes beyond teaching day-to-day tasks to a broader set of skills that increase each employee's value (reaching root causes, giving effective feedback), tailored to the organization's context, culture, and value drivers. Example: a Latin American bank trained employees to anticipate customer needs from external circumstances, emotional cues, and customer history — building interpersonal awareness, faster information gathering, deeper product/process knowledge — and in one year rose from second to first in customer satisfaction across all three segments (corporate, small business, retail).

**Four success factors (exhibit shows capability-building transformations outperform "quick savings" transformations over time):**

1. **Engage every level of the organization** — capability gaps aren't only at the front line; capability building is needed up to the CEO, with role modeling from the top the single most important success factor. Coaching direct reports is essential for every executive (a global asset manager's CEO convened a recurring top-team problem-solving meeting). The **middle-management** level is where scale gets hard — too many for one-on-one coaching, and content differs: middle managers need "translation" skills to turn enterprise strategy into concrete frontline actions (e.g., a service-center manager recognizing the resource implications of a privacy-policy review — sidebar "The pressure for capabilities" tells the story of Jeanette, a group manager who wishes she could "clone" her best team leader Marco).
2. **Create excitement and pride** — frame capability building as a future opportunity, not a critique of the past; a clear value proposition beyond career advancement. Leaders celebrate programs (serving as faculty, hosting graduations); over time strong programs make the company a desirable employer (>90% of one insurer's participants satisfied).
3. **Apply a range of learning techniques** — classroom alone is a poor fit for adults, who need concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. As much learning as possible should happen in the actual workplace during real work ("sit withs" with immediate feedback). When not possible, use realistic settings: one European company (50,000+ employees) built a **"model office"** using real company data to train ~3,000 leaders/managers, with the IT system generating realistic emails/scenarios for role-play using lean techniques.
4. **Institutionalize through HR** — embed capability building in HR processes. A financial-markets company redefined required competencies (problem solving, daily-meeting facilitation, coaching) with HR follow-up and revamped compensation to reward lean-skills development, incorporated into all employees' performance objectives — eventually volume of completed work rose **30%**, errors fell **80%**, and client and employee satisfaction each rose **>10%**.

The regional financial institution ties all four together: the CEO launched by saying everyone (including him and his team) would learn new approaches; 500 middle managers plus 200 "change agents" went through intensive capability building; a new internal brand and comprehensive communications platform generated demand; an experiential-learning center trained ~1,000/year and certified managers — four years in, ROE rose and the firm cemented customer-service leadership.

## Article 13 — A package full of change: An interview with Ian Andrews of Commonwealth Bank of Australia

_Authors/interviewers: Christian Johnson, Jonathan Michael; interviewee: Ian Andrews (EGM, group sales & service, CBA)_

Context: **Commonwealth Bank of Australia (CBA)** retail-banking profits grew **8%** (Dec 2014 → Dec 2015), driven by customer-service performance — CBA's customer-satisfaction scores (per **Roy Morgan Research**) rose from lowest among Australia's four largest banks (mid-2011) to highest (early 2013), holding #1 for 12 consecutive months by May 2016. The article focuses on **change management** — making change easier/faster for the front line to absorb.

Key ideas:

- Customer expectations changed in two categories: how customers want to deal with the bank (balance of assisted — face-to-face/phone/instant message — vs. unassisted/digital interactions) and the bank's broader role in customers' financial well-being (long-term relationships).
- The industry shift: from **product- and channel-focused** to **customer-centric**, requiring the whole organization to operate cohesively to deliver true customer value.
- **The core innovation — "packages of change":** previously each program had its own change plan, communications, and definition of success; rolling out many large programs in succession overwhelmed the front line. CBA instead bundled major initiatives into single **"packages of change"** around a core theme, so the front line experiences one predictable change rather than many overlapping ones. A new governance process ensures the narrative supports the theme and aligns with strategy, delivered by leaders the front line knows and believes in.
- Each package covers all channels (branches, call centers, digital) and a complete set of transformation elements (transaction-migration initiatives, report templates, tools/technology, capability training, sales-operation changes). CBA aims for **two major packages a year**, checking frontline capacity each time and running a validation process after each rollout (quick self-assessment, then an annual reassessment of adoption); if adoption lags, support is provided and future packages may be deferred.
- Benefits to the front line: continuity and familiarity across components (same methodology, format, language, look-and-feel, training) — "it should feel like there's _less_ change happening to you because the change is more consistent and of higher quality."
- Deciding what goes in a package: include changes involving serious mind-set shifts or affecting the whole org/channel; narrower product/campaign changes may proceed separately. Senior executives had to learn to prioritize collectively rather than protect pet projects.
- Parallel vs. line-led change: from day one CBA believed change must be **led through line leadership** (frontline people adopt change when they see/hear it from leaders they know — more credible than head office), while centralizing and "industrializing" the practices/resources frontline leaders use.
- Lessons: get specific about how the new approach creates value (creating a "multiplier effect"); the two biggest frontline shifts are from "What products interest this customer?" to "How do I better understand this customer?" and from a product to a service point of view. Andrews redefined success from "handing over the program at implementation" to "the value change generates over an extended period." His own role had no direct authority — he had to influence rather than direct ("accepting people's diverse viewpoints... being very patient" while reminding people of the collective goal). It's a continual cycle of action, assessment, and improvement.

# THEME: Why leaders do it — The value of a single enterprise culture

Framing: as management systems mature, leaders extend lean well beyond assembly-line-like areas into the most complex parts of the enterprise, creating a single culture that guides how the whole organization works. This theme covers expert (B2B) organizations, law, procurement, HR/culture, and mining.

## Article 14 — Transforming expert organizations

_Authors: Albert Bollard, Clark Durant, Rohit Sood, Matt Tobelmann_

Thesis: improving operations and client experience is hard in B2B/expertise-heavy organizations (law firms, utilities, financial-information providers, risk-management departments) because they rely on highly skilled experts who see their work as a unique "art" and treat each project as bespoke. But there's also a _science_ to expertise — much of even bespoke work is actually standard, and the four lean disciplines can identify, understand, and improve those standard elements so experts spend more time on the genuine "art" while clients get a better experience.

Example: a financial-information provider cut its documentation-issue backlog by ~**70%** and time to market by **15–20%** in four months (exhibit also notes **18–22%** higher client satisfaction and **80%** employee support for the new way of working).

Problems with the expert mind-set:

- **"Right" and wrongs** — experts identify by expertise (not employer), pursuing deeper expertise for its own sake; being technically right can matter more than solving the larger issue. At one corporate lender, legal/finance/other experts focused on their own assessments so intently that deals backlogged for months even when a borrower's assets/credit clearly justified the credit line — each group weakly connected to the client's goal (getting the deal done); "the risk of being wrong was far greater than the risk of delay." The same bias shaped a North American utility, where autonomous local offices and "job owners" left too little flexibility for systemwide peaks/troughs (output varied 50–100% across areas with the same labor capacity).
- **The expertise silo** — because only experts can credibly lead experts, capability building follows ad hoc apprenticeship, automation is minimal, and the silo hardens. Experts feel frustrated by the bureaucracy (antiquated technology, opaque processes, cancelled vacations as the only one who can answer an emergency).
- **Automation lite (sidebar)** — an international bank created a simple online deal tracker pulling data into a status grid (color-coded red/yellow for behind/at-risk steps); cheap to build, it cut average deal time to completion by almost half — much of the benefit from simply making data visible (revealing deals actually took less time than assumed) and starting conversations about why Deal A finished faster than Deals B–E. But the tool only starts the discussion; experts needed confidence the goal wasn't blame, separating delays that add value (protecting the bank) from those that don't.

The solution — engage experts in solving the problem that mainly affects them, helping them become leaders who develop colleagues and connect their work to purpose and customers (sidebar "One culture, no exceptions" — don't exempt senior experts who resist; one problem-solver-in-chief manager undermined the whole program until he let a "mistake" run and saw the team fix it with a better solution).

**Three-attempt example (a US financial institution engaging expert financial analysts):**

- _First attempt (worse, not better):_ analysts identified "correcting offshore data errors" as the problem and added a quality-control step — which worsened delays and introduced more errors.
- _Second attempt (preconceived ideas):_ the analysts' leader blamed incompatible IT standards and proposed a new IT platform (an unacknowledged bias treating IT as the panacea).
- _Third attempt (uprooting the real cause):_ leaders required input from the offshore teams and IT, forming a cross-functional **"virtual work cell"** (offshore + onshore analysts, IT, relationship managers, compliance, legal) mandated to practice new behaviors (factual analysis, no blame, deference to those closest to the issue). They traced the data back to a different division where it arrived already compromised, and built a customized data feed needing little IT investment — eliminating the need for offshore checking. Results: process times **15–20%** faster, error rates down ~**70%**, and **>15%** capacity freed.

**Connecting to the customer:** align the interests of the expert, internal customer, and external customer. At a Latin American asset manager, claims-unit attorneys assembled elaborate paper trails before routine payouts (legally protective but causing months-long client hardships); returning to first principles, leaders broadened the categories of risk — long bureaucratic delays were a **reputational risk** at least as material as the fraud being prevented, and the work diverted lawyers from genuinely complex issues. By reinforcing the lawyers' value, leaders got them to prioritize so smaller predictable claims processed in days, not months.

## Article 15 — A new order for law (interview)

_Authors/interviewers: Alex D'Amico, Christian Johnson; interviewees from Seyfarth Shaw — Andrew Baker (then-director of legal products & technology), Kim Craig (then-director of legal process improvement / now managing director, lean solutions), Lisa Damon (executive committee), Steve Poor (chairman 2001–2016, now chairman emeritus)_

Context: **Seyfarth Shaw** (900-lawyer, Chicago-based international firm) has applied lean-management principles since 2005 to create predictability, transparency, and collaboration. It expanded beyond resolving legal problems into improving clients' law-department workflows and high-risk compliance training. Over four years, revenues grew **>20%** and profits **>25%**. (Lean program branded internally as **SeyfarthLean / Lean Six Sigma**.)

Key points:

- The structural problem (a decade ago): corporate legal departments faced demands to deliver higher value at lower cost, wanting to become value centers, not cost centers; firms weren't meeting the challenge. A client's framing: "he doesn't buy legal services so much as business solutions delivered by lawyers" — raising the paradox of raising value while controlling cost.
- Galvanizing leadership: an executive-training program with the **Kellogg School of Management** gave partners business literacy; client speakers (e.g., Tom Sager, who became DuPont's general counsel) showed how clients were bringing lean into legal departments.
- Two pilot projects: (1) conflicts-of-interest review (high-visibility, firm-wide pain point) — processing time cut **86%**, errors cut **90%**; (2) a real-estate lending practice struggling to match market rates (limited scope, well-defined team) — better resource allocation and higher fee recoveries. Results were used to tell partners "this is going to be part of who we are."
- **Process maps & standard work:** 500+ "process maps" for legal workflows. For each project type (e.g., a corporate acquisition) the best practitioners list all tasks and estimate typical times — a form of lean **"standard work."** Maps aren't exact scripts but add discipline (if a 2-hour contract looks like 8 hours, that's a signal to talk to someone) and include **"artifacts"** (model documents, checklists) at each step, continually curated.
- **Transparency:** standards make work visible to clients via a collaborative technology platform (to-do lists, tasks, status against agreed standards), letting clients adjust workloads among in-house and outside attorneys. Process-mapping done _with_ clients (putting the corporate-acquisition process on a wall and asking in-house counsel to add their activities) uncovers disconnects within the client's own department. Transparency is a powerful motivator.
- **Cultural shift for partners:** from "my work is magic / I'm the center of the universe" to "I'm integral, but not essential for everything." The firm became very flat — an example of **"adhocracy"** (authority based on actual expertise/knowledge, not title); often a secretary knows more about a process stage than the lead partner. Partners feared displacement (lawyers as "artisanal guild") but learned that the approach _raises_ their strategic value because it enables solving larger client problems. (Partners "always want to be Yoda"; training helps them feel comfortable asking general questions outside their expertise.) Example: an apparel client's licensing-tracking problem grew into a broader legal + technology + data-visualization solution and more projects. The key question one partner asked a general counsel — "What keeps you up at night?" — revealed he wanted better work-life balance, which better processes could deliver.
- **Client relationships & people:** the approach avoids the "vendor mentality" by creating a nuanced business relationship. Teams work the same way regardless of the client's situation. Resistance comes from clients wanting "the easy button"/one software package — but questioning reveals their complexity. The gap between law school and practice is bigger than ever ("being a really good 'classic lawyer' is no longer enough" — people need facility with technology, a different problem-solving approach, a changed environment). Seyfarth needs **multidisciplinary** candidates (law + business + process + project management + technology), works with law schools, invests in design thinking and systems design, hires law grads into project-management/technology roles, and bases promotion on **competencies**, not tenure. Clients increasingly want **"horizon expertise"** (helping them see what's coming). Leadership lessons: patience, persistence, taking pride in small victories, and listening to meet partners/clients/teams where they are.

## Article 16 — Purchasing power: Lean management creates new value in procurement

_Authors: Koen De Backer, Björn-Uwe Mercker, Marco Moder, Peter Spiller_

Thesis: most procurement functions have viewed lean only as a way to streamline/automate **procure-to-pay (P2P)** activities, ignoring its capacity to deliver strategic value — because procurement's operating costs are very low (typically **0.3–1% of spend**), so lean-as-efficiency seems low-potential. But properly applied, lean becomes a strategic weapon: aligning purchasing to internal customers' real interests, rethinking the end-to-end procurement process (suppliers → manufacturing → external customers), and transforming strategic procurement.

The opportunity: at one large industrial company, workload analysis found strategic buyers spent **<40%** of their time on core value-adding activities (market understanding, qualifying suppliers, negotiating); the rest was lost to administration, reporting templates, and operational tasks. Cutting that burden could free **>70%** of buyers' time for strategic work — "the equivalent of doubling the size of the strategic procurement function without adding a single person." Lean also enables a more integrated view (what to keep in-house, automate, near/offshore, outsource, or stop). Digital is set to transform procurement beyond P2P (standardized should-cost analyses, workflow portals, external supplier databases), and lean's structured processes are the ideal framework for integrating these tools.

**Applying a lean lens — five areas:**

1. **Understand the customer** — both internal business units and end customers; use structured interactions, business collaboration portals, voice-of-the-customer interviews, **strategic procurement councils**. (One company learned business-unit leaders didn't read centrally generated purchasing reports; scrapping the labor-intensive reports freed capacity for strategic raw materials. Best companies treat procurement as a "demand shaper.")
2. **Standardize processes** — use **value-stream mapping** (adapted from manufacturing) to separate worthwhile from wasteful steps.
   - _Strategic processes:_ standardize to reduce repetitive/duplicate activity (central databases to find suppliers, standard supplier-selection criteria, standard category-strategy templates, modular contract terms) — while distinguishing what can be standardized from what needs flexibility. Automation in strategic procurement is underdeveloped: **eRFX** (electronic request-for-X) tools, e-auctions, and cleansheets linked to raw-material price indexes support fact-based negotiation.
   - _Operational processes:_ value-stream analysis reveals efficiency opportunities (one industrial company found purchase orders per operational buyer varied 4×, captured top performers' practices into standard operating procedures, and coached the rest). Automate further (electronic catalogs, automated order placement/tracking).
3. **Build the organization and capable people** — a clear split between **strategic and operational** purchasing roles so expensive strategic buyers spend time on valuable work; heavy attention to capability building (central databases, communities, a procurement **"academy"** covering category knowledge, cleansheet cost modeling, structured negotiations _plus_ value-stream analysis, root-cause problem solving, interpersonal communication) reinforcing a single "way of working" that defines culture.
4. **Manage performance** — monitor suppliers (quality, cost, flexibility, service), individual buyers (efficiency + customer focus), and the function as a whole (cash and P&L contributions); the right metrics show where to improve and reduce overall tracking/reporting burden.
5. **Encourage the right mind-sets and continuously improve** — each purchaser questions and improves their own tasks; methods include a 15-minute daily huddle (one best-practice element + one critical problem and its resolution) and standardizing successful artifacts (e.g., a detailed RFQ template) into category standards, with leadership acknowledgement.

**Sidebar — how one leader brought lean to procurement (global insurer's head of procurement):** they tracked too many metrics ("so what?"); digging into "tickets" revealed low-risk/low-dollar requests (e.g., a $100,000 training consultant) and high-risk/high-cost ones (e.g., $50M outsourcing) going through the _same_ process — adding no value and delaying the requester while staff lacked capacity for big-deal due diligence. They created separate standards per ticket type (automation for low-value tickets), with improvements coming from the team itself (asked to solve their top five problems within 90 days; "even if the idea solves only 80%, because it came from them they'll implement it better than a 100% solution from someone else"). They sent expectation-setting emails within 24 hours, standardized/outsourced due diligence (a report in 15 minutes vs. hours/days), and reduced tickets in queues ~**40%** — the biggest change being mind-sets ("better to set an 80% target and achieve 60% than set a 25% target and achieve 32%").

Impact example: one large European industrial player adopted lean across diverse business units, raising direct-sourcing efficiency ~**20%** and indirect-sourcing ~**25%**, and — rather than cut staff — reassigned freed people to a center of excellence, raised savings targets, and reduced spend by double-digit percentages across many categories.

## Article 17 — Transforming HR and culture: An interview with Banco de Crédito del Perú's Bernardo Sambra

_Authors/interviewers: Rodrigo Chaparro Gazzo, Christian Johnson; interviewee: Bernardo Sambra (CHRO, BCP)_

Context: **Banco de Crédito del Perú (BCP)**, Peru's oldest and largest bank. A 2009 transformation cut customer wait times >50% and raised productivity. Later, BCP's share of sector growth declined and its **cost-income ratio** stayed stubbornly high vs. regional competitors, prompting a new call to become more efficient _and_ challenge the bank's culture. The effort actually raised employee satisfaction while cutting the cost-income ratio by almost one-fifth (from **49 to 41** over two years).

Key points:

- **Defining efficiency** — everyone had a different idea, so they aligned on a definition balancing four principles: simple austerity (do more with less); leadership/being number one at everything; deciding as if the resources were one's own; and an enterprise perspective (success for the bank as a whole, not each unit). These principles are in tension — aiming for #1 in a product makes sense if it benefits the whole bank, not if the benefit is narrow. Workshops involved **350+ managers/leaders** to build broad consensus on trade-offs.
- **Three fronts:** (1) deep **performance improvement** across support functions, IT/operations, product portfolio, commercial model; (2) **cost** discipline (cost-consciousness, a central team scrutinizing cost deviations, new governance committees reviewing each investment's business case above a threshold); (3) **culture** change — launched on all three fronts simultaneously.
- **Sambra's personal journey:** HR was among the first functions transformed. He initially resisted ("How can you ask me to change when BCP is the best employer in Peru?"), wanting to build a "world-class HR model" — then realized that ambition was _his_ ("I wanted to be number one"). The breakthrough: he could change his unit, manage differently, and still see the value he was creating. This defined the change-management approach: before a transformation team arrives in a unit, top managers and direct reports hold a **four-hour workshop** on why they must make these specific changes, ensuring managers _own_ the transformation.
- **Avoiding wholesale cuts:** instead of issuing targets with Gantt charts, managers start with a **funnel of ideas** (broad → sharper → specific committed initiatives), accepting months of uncertainty. To protect morale, each org runs a parallel change-management exercise on how people think about cost/efficiency (challenging "unavoidable" costs) — and, above all, **thanking people** repeatedly.
- **Results:** employee-climate-survey results rose for several years (satisfaction in transformed units held steady in 2014, rose in 2015, now 10% higher than 2011's already-70%+). New capabilities: in recruitment, a third-party handles low-value tasks (CV collection, interview logistics) so staff focus on strategic questions; pooling training resources (previously specialized by business line, causing demand peaks/valleys) improved capacity management; **visual-management tools** let leaders see projects, task status, and available/needed skills, making them better managers. A 15-minute daily morning meeting reviews yesterday's performance and finds improvements.
- **Sustaining it:** a new decision-making structure means even with budget available you must justify spending; an **efficiency division** is responsible for asking difficult questions ("a way of life now").
- **Six lessons:** (1) have a conversation without PowerPoint (think about the future); (2) get a clear CEO mandate; (3) give people a compelling story (without it, a mandate is just an order); (4) make managers the _owners_ of the process, not victims (HR supports); (5) be consistent no matter how painful — making exceptions destroys faith; (6) when results come, don't relax.

## Article 18 — Mining for leadership with lean management (interview)

_Authors/interviewers: Christian Johnson, Ferran Pujol; interviewees from SQM — Juan Carlos Barrera (VP potassium & lithium), José Miguel Berguño (VP people & performance), Carlos Diaz (VP nitrates & iodine)_

Context: **Sociedad Química y Minera (SQM)**, a Chilean miner operating brine ponds across 50+ km² in the Atacama Desert — among the world's largest sites for potassium (fertilizers) and lithium (batteries), plus iodine/nitrate ores; sales >$1.7B in 2015. The early-21st-century commodity-boom collapse left SQM vulnerable; it found a new growth source in its own people. Notably, this is presented as lean working _despite extreme variability_ (raw materials can change dramatically day to day — unlike standardized auto components).

Key points:

- **Trigger:** 2012 results were great but costs were rising, raising concern about a price fall. In early 2013 they launched **SQM-100**, a major cost-reduction effort that saved close to **10%** of the cost base. But they realized the organization wasn't ready to exploit its new competitive position — and that they kept having to repeat restructurings (every ~5–7 years) and cost-reduction projects (every ~3–4 years) because costs crept back. The new question: do something different so they don't keep repeating this.
- **The pivotal visit:** a US company with a long lean-manufacturing history showed two seemingly contradictory things — employees identified strongly with the company _and_ the company was far less dependent on individuals. SQM relied on "artists" (people whose expertise was unique), making it vulnerable; the visited company's people could substitute for one another easily.
- **Transforming leaders, not workers:** Diaz's realization — "the main challenge in a transformation is how to transform leaders... it's probably you who have the problem," not the workers. Advantages SQM had: a history of adapting quickly, liking challenges, and the unique geology forcing them to invent their own techniques.
- **Rollout:** to distinguish it from cost-cutting, they named it **"M1"** (not "SQM-200"), emphasizing culture change, and piloted at an old, stable nitrates plant (easy to see effects; a recent business integration created an opening; strong on-site leaders). Skepticism was high ("we've done this under lots of names, it'll fail in four months"); some took improvements (even 10–20% production increases) as criticism. They worked harder to include everyone in workshops/problem-solving so people felt the changes came from them.
- **Pacing:** potassium/lithium launched _two_ plants at once — too soon (natural internal competitiveness, one plant already at high production with little improvement headroom, managers/supervisors stretched too thin, defeating the goal of improving work life). They **paused** (a hard decision) to adapt lean concepts — reordering skill introduction to fit actual problems and simplifying the language — so people could see how lean helped them.
- **Change agents:** drawn from the **line organization** (not a parallel/duplicate structure), on the ground, high performers who understood the technically complex processes — supporting line leaders rather than creating parallel leadership.
- **Results (~3 years in):** improved performance (costs, production, safety) and greater flexibility. They transferred 15 people (~a quarter of a team, including manager and assistant manager) from a transformed plant to jump-start a new one — the first plant kept improving and the second progressed: "we can now move people to help performance in one area without sacrificing performance in the areas they came from."
- **Leadership change:** from controlling/deciding everything (being a "bottleneck") to developing people and asking questions so they decide; humility; being conscious of everyone's time (visiting performance-board discussions for informal conversations instead of special meetings). The dynamic flipped — "it's the people who are pulling the managers along," with managers facilitating their people's ideas. Next challenges: **innovation** ("the deeper meaning of continuous improvement"), while keeping flexibility/responsiveness and the new way of working.
