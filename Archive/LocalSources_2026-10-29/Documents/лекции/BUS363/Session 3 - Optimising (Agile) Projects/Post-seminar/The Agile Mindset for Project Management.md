## Synopsis

The authors describe Agile in **three ideas: satisfy the customer, engage the team, and simplify everything.** All ideas from a recently updated Agile Manifesto fit this three-idea framework. They also took the ideas from the _PMI Agile Certification Outline_ and had Agile experts prioritize them; the resulting important ideas also all fit the three-idea framework. Collectively, the framework explains _why_ Agile is useful and _how_ to perform it. The paper concludes by explaining the values, benefits, and actions of Agile.

## Research design

Multiple parts:

- Extensive practice using Agile while studying for and passing several Agile certification exams.
- In 2016 the authors wrote an **update to the Agile Manifesto** aimed at current practice in both IT projects _and_ a variety of other (non-software) contexts, to broaden relevance. The updated manifesto keeps the original structure: **four values and 13 processes/principles.**
- They brainstormed and created the simple **three-part model** (customer, team, simplification).
- To validate, they used the **PMI Agile Certification Outline** as a widely accepted standard. They de-duplicated repeated items, leaving **37 items** used as survey items. They asked experts (each with at least 20 years of PM experience, both traditional and Agile) to prioritize them, ending up with **23 items** considered important by at least half of the experts.

## Relevance to practice and education

- **Practice:** Agile is often described in various ways and poorly understood. Many practitioners focus on one or a few aspects rather than the totality, and are disappointed with results. Agile may be at a point — like **TQM a generation ago** — with great popularity but poor understanding, and could suffer the same fate (better organizations get substantial benefits, many others get poor results) unless made easy to understand. This paper aims to help Agile be better understood.
- **Education:** It is a simple organizing framework shown to incorporate all the more important Agile ideas — teachable as a primer on Agile or incorporated into a broader PM course.

## Main findings

- All four values and 13 principles from the updated Agile Manifesto fit neatly into one of the three categories (customer, team, simplification).
- All 23 prioritized items from the PMI Agile Certification Outline map directly into one of the three categories.
- The updated-manifesto items largely explain **why** Agile is useful; the prioritized PMI-outline items largely explain **how** to perform Agile.

## Research implications

This was a small-scale pilot study. A larger study might surface more nuance about how to perform Agile and why some items are more valuable, or reveal which items are most important to perform quickly when adapting to Agile, or are better suited to particular industries/contexts.

## What Agile is (definition + methods + mindset)

Agile is a form of **adaptive or change-driven project management** in which PM largely _reacts_ to what happened in early project stages rather than planning everything in detail up front. Documentation is minimal early and becomes progressively more complete. Understanding Agile requires knowing both the **methods** and the **mindset**.

**Methods:** a project vision is developed and shared early. Teams plan in short bursts (generally **one to four weeks**), called **sprints or iterations**. Details are planned for the upcoming iteration, with very little change allowed during it. Products are defined and delivered one iteration at a time, each iteration finishing an output that has business value; then the next iteration is planned.

**Mindset:** empowering, engaging, and enabling open communication.

**The problem the paper addresses:** "Agile" has become synonymous with change in tech organizations, but there's a growing misconception about using it for significant change in complex endeavours. Most books focus on the _mechanics_ of a particular practice (e.g., **Scrum, XP, or Kanban**) but fail to define the overarching _thinking_ that makes them effective — they don't address the **mindset shift** needed. The misconception is worsened by using traditional PM words to explain similar Agile concepts without drawing clear distinctions. Without a firm, deliberate transition to a new way of thinking about teams, Agile devolves into a "semantic equivalency" that frustrates executives and fails to unlock performance potential and team members' individual knowledge.

**The TQM cautionary parallel:** Agile (like TQM in the 1990s) emerged from frustration with current management techniques. Difference: TQM was oriented toward managing an _ongoing organization_, whereas Agile aims at managing a _software project_. The old quality approach was to produce a product then test it (acceptable or not); TQM advocates rightly saw this as wasteful and proposed an approach emphasizing **stakeholder satisfaction, empowered performance, process improvement, and management by facts** (citing Kloppenborg, _Contemporary Project Management_ 3e). But there was no consensus on what exactly constituted TQM (some emphasized statistics/management-by-facts; others emphasized front-line decision-making), so better-managed organizations benefited while many wasted effort and gave up. The same risk applies to Agile: it has multiple aspects, you cannot cherry-pick, and leaders need to understand the whole of it. Hence the three simple ideas.

**The three simple ideas:**

1. Satisfy the customer.
2. Engage the team.
3. Simplify everything.

The authors then go in two directions to see how they converge: (1) rewrite the Agile Manifesto updated for current practice and broad relevance, and (2) prioritize and group the PMI Agile Certification Outline tasks.

## Exhibit 1 — Updated Agile Manifesto (described as text)

**Overarching statement:** "We are developing better ways of _creating complex products_, by doing it and helping others do it." (Based on The Agile Manifesto 2001, modified by F. Forte 2016.)

**The four values (X _over_ Y):**

1. Individuals and interactions _over_ process and tools.
2. Working _product over_ comprehensive documentation.
3. _Customer collaboration over_ contract negotiation.
4. _Responding to change over_ following a plan.

**The 13 principles (numbered as in the exhibit):** 5. Build teams around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need. Trust them to get the job done. 6. Deliver working product frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference for the shorter timescale. 7. At regular intervals, teams reflect on ways to become more effective, then adjust behaviour accordingly. 8. Working product is the primary measure of progress. 9. Agile processes promote sustainable delivery — the sponsors, people doing the work, and people using the work should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely. 10. Simplicity — the art of maximizing the amount of _work not done_ — is essential. 11. The most effective method of conveying information to and within a team is facilitated face-to-face conversation with visualization. 12. Continuous attention to outcomes and good design enhances Agile processes. 13. The best solutions and designs emerge from self-organizing teams. 14. Everyone involved _must_ work together regularly throughout the process. 15. The highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable product. 16. Embrace changing requirements, even late in the process. 17. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage.

## Deep explanation 1 — Satisfy customers (delivering value)

This is a broad view of what Agile calls **delivering value**, further defined by what "value" means. For complex products this usually means **software** — deliver _working software_ with every iteration. A document or test plan is not itself "value" if the product is software; items needed for regulatory reasons are a "cost of doing business" to be minimized, with the primary focus on working software roughly every two weeks.

**The building metaphor:** models and drawings of a building have value but are not the building — they are a _cost_ of getting one. Spending more time/effort on drawings than required is **waste**, no longer adding value. Easy to grasp physically, less clear for complex (software) products because we often don't understand how we get to good software.

**The Standish statistic:** the Standish survey on software product value indicates up to **80% of software functions created are never used** — a large number of hours on functions adding no value. Agile addresses this by asking the customer what would add value _next_, in short intervals; the customer may pause to digest and get feedback from other users before requesting more features. This is very different from the traditional **requirements-driven** model.

**The physical-to-virtual metaphor:** Agile applies physical-world PM thinking to the virtual world of software. The metaphor was useful in early software constrained by physics (real-time systems like missile guidance; limits of computing power, memory, disk). As those constraints fell away, software proliferated, spawning increasing complexity — but not necessarily a better understanding of delivering working product cheaper/better/faster. The opportunity now: think differently about delivering value. **Summary:** deliver value in very short intervals and get feedback before building more, which lets creators of complex products **pivot** along the product life cycle.

## Deep explanation 2 — Engage the team (empowerment, cooperation, knowledge sharing, servant leadership, visible/continual communication)

**Empowerment & servant leadership:** Without **servant leadership** there cannot be empowered teams. You cannot empower anyone — people must empower _themselves_, and won't while the risk of empowerment exceeds the risk of not. The environment must create structure and set learned behaviours, then servant leaders guide/coach teams toward self-organization — this journey _is_ "being agile." Empowered teams are an _outcome_ of the process, not a starting point — so although empowerment is one of the first items in the grouping, it is the _result/benefit_ of the other areas.

**Cooperation:** key to developing self-organizing teams that act empowered over time. Hard to achieve in today's corporate structures — matrixed teams come together briefly, often without enough time for the team-development process, and are broken up as soon as work finishes instead of staying together to grow with the product. There must be **trust before cooperation**, and true cooperation needs **space for conflict** — so the servant leader must be comfortable facilitating and even drawing out conflict; this is how teams progress and mature.

**Knowledge sharing:** becomes organic in high-performing, cross-functional teams that want to break down barriers and become product experts — but only when the team has a sense of product ownership and team commitment. Needs a team identity, ways of working, and good-natured fun/accountability. Sharing happens for the **product, the process, and the team**; the more knowledge in these three areas, the more Agile can be practised — and as teams become more agile, they need _less_ process.

**On servant leadership (deeper):** _All_ leadership done properly is servant leadership (this excludes leaders who want to be served, and doesn't mean leaders merely do the team's bidding). The common "remove impediments/blockers" answer is only a _trivial_ example — worthwhile only until the leader helps team members learn to remove their _own_ impediments. Distinction: **we manage things but lead people**; you are only leading people as long as they are following (following is voluntary). So Agile has a higher-level form of leadership than traditional PM (where "leaders" were often really managers tracking tasks via reporting).

**Diffused leadership:** in the Agile organization there is **no single servant leader** — traditional roles split into focus areas (process, product, the "how," etc.). With more leadership opportunities, people must "stay within the swim lane" they're assigned to and refrain from commenting outside their remit. Done well, this creates a "safety net" of servant leadership that supports and empowers the team — shifting focus from the leader onto the led.

**Conversation + visual communication + transparency:** creating together requires conversation, and conversation quality rises with visual aids. Agile brings the physical realm into communication (moving a story card on a **kanban board**, using whiteboards/visual means). Humans have communicated with drawings longer than words. Visual communication supports **transparency** — exposing lack of understanding/clarity so the team can address real issues. Lack of transparency stems from lack of trust, which stems from how teams are formed. Even benign steps (document work on a 3×5 card, talk about it every morning, allow questions) markedly improve team performance. A **diagnostic by observation:** if team members look at the kanban board instead of at each other when discussing work, it signals lack of trust/openness. The value of "visual" is seeing the state of information, work, and people that impact the product; visibility then enables experimentation to improve effectiveness.

**Why efficiency in conversation backfires:** short conversations work only when people work mostly independently. Complex, emerging creative work can't be deterministically broken into fully independent discrete tasks — the person asking for creation doesn't yet know what "good" looks like, so neither can the team without continual conversation. The **ceremonies** of Agile (e.g., **scrum**) let the team plan and reflect; the **system demonstration** reveals what the customer thinks and wants next; and the team reflects on how the prior iteration's work was done (typically two weeks) and how to improve — again, a conversation.

## Deep explanation 3 — Simplify everything (sustainable pace/cadence + process improvement)

**Minimal process** is a hallmark of Agile. The Agile Manifesto's first draft called this "lightweight process" (changed because signatories didn't want to be called lightweights). A key principle is figuring out what _not_ to do — **work not done.** The more you communicate **face-to-face**, the less process you need (obvious in personal life, but business life eliminated face-to-face in the name of "efficiency"). For complex, hard-to-describe products you actually need _less_ efficiency and _more_ conversation.

**Pruning the process tree:** start with a few principles and values, add only what's needed, and reduce process as the team matures. A long-lived, stable self-organizing team needs less process. The "process tree" must be continually pruned to keep processes as **lightweight as possible** ("minimal viable process") — otherwise you slow value delivery with unnecessary process.

**Estimation as potential waste:** teams often try to get _better at estimating velocity_ rather than _eliminating the need to estimate_. If a team can assure the customer it will reliably produce what it commits to and keep improving, the obsession with estimating becomes waste. A process that has outlived its usefulness becomes an impediment to delivering value faster.

**Pace (how fast):** must be measured/metered to be optimal. Too much time between idea conception and delivery lets work expand and violates "work not done" — producing "too much" that the customer won't value (= waste). Pace prevents waste by letting the **team determine its own pace** (don't tell the team how much to do in an interval; let it continually ask how to improve via process improvement). This is the opposite of legendary software **"death marches."** Once a sustainable pace exists, periodically reflect on improving it (invest in tools, change/eliminate process, cross-train, experiment), then expose it to inspection/reflection/improvement like everything else.

**Cadence (how often you coordinate):** distinct from pace. When scaling to larger efforts, you have dependencies on other cross-functional self-organizing teams, so you need a **common cadence** to ensure everyone builds in the same direction. Coordinate at predictable, predetermined times so teams can focus on their own commitments without constant conversation.

**The apparent contradiction resolved:** want _constant_ conversation **at the team level**, but **not** constant conversation **outside** the team. The creative unit is the team — ideally **seven members, plus or minus two** ("where the magic happens"). As communication channels grow or aren't continual, you need **highly coordinated communication** instead of continual conversation. **Marching-band metaphor:** no cadence → no music; the music is the creative process, cadence lets it happen synchronized. The common mistake: not knowing dependencies well enough, so exposing all information at higher levels expecting others to identify your dependencies (not productive). The mechanism that prevents this is **commitment** — after cadence events, teams commit to producing working product, demonstrating it to the customer, and eliciting feedback; serious commitment drives transparency and improvement.

**Continuous improvement + relentless reflection:** woven through all three areas is process improvement and the ultimate desire to _eliminate the need for process_. It combines **kaizen** ("continuous improvement") with the other side of the same coin, **hansei** ("relentless reflection" — stopping and reflecting on how to get better as a team). In Agile this is done via the **retrospective** ceremony and, at larger scales, **inspecting and adapting**. These concepts aren't new in software, but the **discipline** around them is. Wrapping well-known concepts in a cadence and pace lets teams make changes at a very **small batch size** — experiment with the smallest change that shows a discernible difference, see the result, then **pivot** in the next iteration without huge time/effort/reputation at risk.

## Conclusion

There are constraints on generalizing the three groups to _all_ process and product creation, so the discussion stays within the context of **creating complex products that do not have a deterministic representation of "good."** The paper defines how to shift an organization's mindset to capitalize on what Scrum and Kanban suggest about organizational constructs, and how to shift terminology to deliver the promise of "being agile." Caution: the longer the manifesto is "preached" without a shifted mindset, the more likely the organization loses the geometric benefits of the new constructs. If an organization truly understands and implements Agile principles in the intended context, benefits are notable, including increased value delivery by self-organizing teams and unlocking the combined knowledge of each individual. How long the transition takes for established organizations isn't well defined — it depends largely on how quickly **leaders at all levels** understand and embrace Agile thinking.
