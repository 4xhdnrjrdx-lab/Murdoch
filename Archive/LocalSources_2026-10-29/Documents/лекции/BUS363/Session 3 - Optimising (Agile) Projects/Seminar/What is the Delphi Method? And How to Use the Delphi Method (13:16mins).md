## Lecture framing (Session 3 slide)

The Delphi method is a structured way to pool the opinions of many experts to reach a group solution. It was developed in 1969 by the **RAND Corporation** to facilitate technological forecasting. It overcomes the bias that results from some voices being more dominant than others — by virtue of personality or eminence. Its primary uses are forecasting and decision-making; it tends to produce robust predictions based on experience by eliminating the wilder contributions. However, when the past is a poor guide to the future and there's a possibility of massive, discontinuous change, Delphi is not suitable — making it inappropriate for leading-edge projects.

## Part 1 — Introduction and Historical Context

- The Delphi method is one of the best yet underutilised tools for finding the right answer to a difficult question.
- Name origin: the **Oracle at Delphi**, an ancient Greek temple where people sought to discern the future.
- Developed in **1969** in the US by the **RAND Corporation**, initially for **technological forecasting** (identifying future events/developments).
- Mike Clayton notes forecasting is possible to a degree if circumstances are right and the process is properly facilitated.

## Part 2 — Overcoming Group Biases

Traditional in-the-room group discussions suffer from biases the Delphi method is designed to remove:

- **First-speaker bias:** everyone frames their argument for or against whatever the first person says.
- **Charismatic / high-profile bias:** perceived greater experts or charismatic personalities move opinion through deference, not correctness.
- **Fear of losing face:** once people state an opinion publicly, they struggle to change it for fear of appearing incompetent — and the higher the perceived expertise, the higher the stakes.
- Delphi lets participants be influenced by compelling ideas and change their minds without risk of losing face.

## Part 3 — Applications and Suitability

- Two primary purposes: **decision-making** (the right thing to do) and **forecasting** (what's going to happen). It harnesses the "**wisdom of a crowd**" without letting participants bias one another.
- Limitations: won't work amid **massive discontinuous change** (unpredictable future), or if no one with **sufficient expertise** is available.
- In PM terms: best for **evolutionary projects** (small, contained change); not for **radically new** projects.

## Part 4 — Roles and Preparation

- First step: **identify a panel of experts** who understand the situation; seek a **breadth** of diverse perspectives beyond a narrow focus.
- Three roles (can all be one person, e.g. a project manager): **Facilitator** (leads the process), **Basic expert** (helps frame initial questions), **Administrator/Coordinator** (manages paperwork/records).

## Part 5 — Question Design and the First Round

- Design the first question set carefully — "**garbage in, garbage out**"; poor questions yield poor answers. Questions must be clear and precise, not vague.
- First-round rules: **identical briefing** (every expert gets exactly the same briefing and questions); **requirement for reasoning** (experts give answers _and_ explain their reasoning — the data, facts, evidence and interpretation behind them).

## Part 6 — Iteration and Anonymity

- After round one, the facilitator tabulates, analyses and segments the data to prepare the next questions.
- **Second round onward:** the same questions are re-posed, now accompanied by the **anonymous** answers and reasoning of colleagues.
- **Anonymity is crucial:** experts evaluate evidence without bias from who provided it; they can see if their answer is an **outlier** and decide whether to hold it or revise; they can change their minds on the evidence without losing face.

## Part 7 — Reaching Convergence and Reporting

- Rounds repeat until **convergence** — a significant number (ideally all) reach a **consensus view**.
- A **final report** should: summarise all viewpoints and evidence; state the consensus; explain why certain views were neglected/ignored; and be shared with the experts as a courtesy.

## Part 8 — Benefits and Weaknesses

**Benefits:** global reach (experts needn't be co-located); bias elimination via anonymity; flexibility (easier to change one's mind).
**Weaknesses:** time-intensive (many rounds); resource-heavy (facilitator/admin workload); expert attrition (drop-out if too long); community bias (a view prevalent across the whole expert community can still bias results — hence diversity of thinking is critical).

## Part 9 — Top Tips for Success

1. Choose a diverse group, possibly including thinkers from outside the normal expert community.
2. Ask questions in a way that doesn't inadvertently introduce bias.
3. Keep experts engaged and informed throughout.
4. Prepare high-quality reports/resources for experts to assess each round.

## Key terms & references

- **Delphi method:** structured expert-panel technique, originally for forecasting.
- **Oracle at Delphi:** namesake.
- **RAND Corporation (1969):** developed the modern method.
- **Biases addressed:** first-speaker, charismatic/high-profile, fear of losing face.
- **Wisdom of a crowd; anonymity; convergence; consensus; outlier.**
- **Discontinuous change** (where Delphi fails) vs **evolutionary projects** (where it fits).
- **GIGO** (garbage in, garbage out).
- **Roles:** facilitator, basic expert, administrator/coordinator.
