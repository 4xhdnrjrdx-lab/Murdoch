## Premise: the multicultural productivity trend (and its catch)

The author opens by referencing his earlier piece on the productivity gains organisations see when employees work outside their home country. The broader move toward **multicultural teams** benefits both individuals and the collective through diversity.

But there is a tension: prior research has shown that **as diversity increases, coordination costs also rise**, which can hamper collaboration and effective teamwork. This implies a **"cultural sweet spot"** at which peak productivity sits — too little or too much friction both reduce output.

A recent study examined how team members' cultural backgrounds shape how they work together, finding that **people from a multicultural background can play a crucial brokering role**, forging the connections diverse teams need to thrive.

---

## Cultural brokerage

The study identified **two types of "cultural broker":**

- **Cultural insiders** — individuals who share a cultural background with at least one other team member.
- **Cultural outsiders** — individuals who share a background with _no_ other team member.

**Key (counter-intuitive) finding:** it's tempting to assume insiders make better brokers, but the evidence did not support that. What mattered most was simply the **presence of a multicultural individual at all**, regardless of whether they were an insider or an outsider. Teams with that added diversity **consistently outperformed mono-cultural teams**.

**The two broker types connect teams differently:**

- **Cultural insiders → integrate** ideas from different cultures. They combine the team's information and ideas into something new and unique.
- **Cultural outsiders → elicit** ideas from different cultures. They use a more **participatory** approach, drawing information and ideas _out of_ their teammates.

**Headline result:** having a multicultural team improved team performance by **roughly 28%**.

## The right personalities (selecting for overseas assignments)

Given that multicultural teams are more creative and productive, the practical question is how organisations capitalise on this. An obvious starting point: **encourage employees to take overseas assignments** — but finding the right candidates is hard.

A study from **Florida Atlantic University** found that **specific personality types tend to thrive on overseas deployments**. The authors note expatriates often struggle to adjust — poor well-being, work–life conflict, poor performance, and turnover — and set out to understand which personal characteristics make expatriates more or less able to adjust effectively.

**Framework used — the "Big Five" personality traits:**

1. Emotional stability
2. Openness
3. Extroversion
4. Conscientiousness
5. Agreeableness

Of these five, **three had a considerable impact** on subsequent expatriate success.

**The traits that predicted successful adjustment:** expatriates who adjusted best tended to be **extroverts who were also emotionally stable and open to new experiences.**

- **Extroversion** — extroverts are better at forming new **social networks**, which support both the **informational** and **emotional** sides of adjusting to a new culture.
- **Emotional stability** — adapting to a new culture is highly stressful; strong emotional reactions act as a barrier to effective adjustment. Emotionally stable people are less affected by **culture shock** and the various stressors of an assignment, and are more even-tempered, so they adjust better.
- **Openness to new experiences** — people high in openness treat unfamiliar behaviours (which are easy to misinterpret) as **novel and enjoyable** rather than threatening.

---

## Good return on investment

- Sending employees overseas is a **significant investment**, so organisations should only deploy people well-suited to it — those who can adapt to new environments while still performing in their role.
- Practical recommendation: include **personality tests** as part of **career-development** processes to select the right people.
- The findings are also useful for **individuals** weighing up whether to pursue an overseas assignment themselves.
- The authors conclude the stakes are high, which is why they looked at the **dispositions** of people on foreign assignment: expatriates bring their own characteristics, and those characteristics shape how they react to stressors and behave overseas — with direct implications for adjustment.
- **Closing point:** developing multicultural teams can deliver tremendous results, but it requires **careful management** to get right.

## Key terms & references

**Core concepts / frameworks:**

- **Cultural sweet spot** — the diversity level balancing creativity gains against rising coordination costs.
- **Cultural brokers** — _cultural insiders_ (share a background with ≥1 teammate; **integrate** ideas) vs _cultural outsiders_ (share with none; **elicit** ideas via a participatory approach).
- **Big Five personality traits** — emotional stability, openness, extroversion, conscientiousness, agreeableness. **Three** predict expatriate success: **extroversion, emotional stability, openness.**
- **Culture shock** / stressors of overseas adjustment.
- **Expatriate adjustment** — informational and emotional dimensions.

**Key figures:**

- Multicultural teams improved performance by **~28%**.
- Diversity → higher coordination costs (the trade-off behind the "sweet spot").
