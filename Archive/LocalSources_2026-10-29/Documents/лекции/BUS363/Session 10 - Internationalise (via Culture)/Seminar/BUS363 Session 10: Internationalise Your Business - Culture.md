## What is Culture?

### Definitions

- "learned beliefs, values, rules, norms, symbols, & traditions that are common to a group of people" (Northouse, 2016: 492).
- "shared qualities of a group that make them unique" (ibid).
- "is the way of life, customs, & scripts of a group of people" (Gudykunst & Ting-Toomey, 1988).
- General reference: Adler, N.J. (1997). _International Dimensions of Organisational Behaviour._ Cincinnati: South-Western College Publishing.

### Schein's Culture Iceberg (Schein, 1985 — diagram described in text)

Culture modelled as an iceberg with three layers (top = visible above the waterline; lower two = hidden below):

- **Observable Artifacts** (above the surface) — manifestations of culture that can be easily observed, e.g., institutions, behaviour, clothing.
- **Values and Norms** (just below the surface) — widely accepted beliefs about right and wrong; widely held expectations for how people "should" think and act.
- **Underlying Assumptions** (deepest) — taken-for-granted beliefs and values so deeply ingrained that individuals are not conscious of holding them.
  (Source: Schein, E.H. (1985) _Organisational Culture and Leadership._)

### Key terms I

- **Ethnocentrism**: "tendency for individuals to place their own group (ethnic, racial, or cultural) at the center of their observations of the world" (Northouse, 2016: 492–493).
- **Multicultural**: "approach or system that takes more than one culture into account."
- Framing statement: _"Everyone is ethnocentric to some degree — a major obstacle to effective leadership"_ (Porter & Samovar, 1997).

### Key terms II

- **Diversity**: "existence of different cultures or ethnicities within a group or organisation" (Northouse, 2016: 492–493).
- **Prejudice**: "a largely fixed attitude, belief, or emotion held by an individual about another individual or group" — based on faulty or unsubstantiated data.
- Framing statement: A skilled business leader needs to find ways to negotiate with followers from various cultural backgrounds.

## Dimensions of Culture (the four major models)

### Dimensions of Culture I — Hall & Trompenaars

- **Hall (1976):** a primary characteristic of cultures is the degree of focus on the **individual (individualistic)** vs. the **group (collectivistic)**.
- **Trompenaars (1994)** — survey of 15,000 people in 47 countries; an organisation's culture is classified on **2 dimensions**:
  - **Egalitarian–hierarchical**: degree to which cultures exhibit shared power vs. hierarchical power.
  - **Person–task orientation**: extent to which cultures emphasise human interaction vs. focusing on tasks.
- Supporting visual: a **"Collectivism – Individualism" world map** shading countries from collectivist (light) to individualist (dark), with North America, Western Europe, and Australia/NZ shown as most individualist.

### Dimensions of Culture II — Hofstede

- **Hofstede (1980, 2001)** — questionnaires from 100,000 respondents in 50+ countries. Benchmark research identified **5 major dimensions** on which cultures differ:
  1. Power distance
  2. Uncertainty avoidance
  3. Individualism–Collectivism
  4. Masculinity–Femininity
  5. Long-term–Short-term orientation

### Dimensions of Culture III — House et al. / GLOBE

- **House et al. (2004)** research on the relationship between culture and leadership produced the **GLOBE research program**.
- Initiated in 1991; more than 160 investigators.
- Quantitative data: 17,000 managers in 950+ organisations across 62 different cultures.
- Developed a classification of **9 cultural dimensions**.

#### The 9 GLOBE Cultural Dimensions (House et al., 2004)

1. Assertiveness
2. Institutional Collectivism
3. In-Group Collectivism
4. Gender Egalitarianism
5. Humane Orientation
6. Power Distance
7. Performance Orientation
8. Uncertainty Avoidance
9. Future Orientation

#### GLOBE Cultural Dimensions — Explained

- **Assertiveness:** degree to which people in a culture are determined, assertive, confrontational & aggressive in their social relationships.
- **Institutional Collectivism:** degree to which an organisation or society encourages institutional or societal collective action.
- **In-Group Collectivism:** degree to which people express pride, loyalty, and cohesiveness in their organisations or families.
- **Gender Egalitarianism:** degree to which an organisation or society minimizes gender role differences and promotes gender equality.
- **Humane Orientation:** degree to which a culture encourages & rewards people for being fair, altruistic, caring, and kind to others.
- **Power Distance:** degree to which members of a group expect and agree that power should be shared unequally.
- **Performance Orientation:** extent to which an organisation or society encourages and rewards group members for improved performance and excellence.
- **Uncertainty Avoidance:** extent to which a society, organisation, or group relies on established social norms, rituals, and procedures to avoid uncertainty.
- **Future Orientation:** extent to which people engage in future-oriented behaviours (e.g., planning, investing in the future, delaying gratification).

## Four Dimensions Critical for Doing Business (synthesis model)

A summary framework cross-attributing four practical dimensions to their originating theorists (described diagram — four columns):

| Dimension                     | Focus           | Attributed to |
| ----------------------------- | --------------- | ------------- |
| **Power distance**            | Hierarchy       | Hofstede      |
| **Universalism**              | Relationships   | Trompenaars   |
| **Monochronic / Polychronic** | Time management | Hall          |
| **High / Low context**        | Communication   | Hall          |

### Power Distance (Hofstede)

Spectrum from **Egalitarian (low power distance)** to **Hierarchical (high power distance)**. Country placement along the scale (low → high): Israel, UK, Netherlands, USA, Japan, Spain, Greece, Turkey, France, Singapore, Arab countries, Philippines, Malaysia.

- **Egalitarian:** expect relatively equal distribution of power; small or no differences in power and voice; no emphasis on symbols of status.
- **Hierarchical:** expect unequal distribution of power; large differences in power and voice; large emphasis on symbols of status; emphasis on not "losing face."

**Case Study I — Power Distance (Hofstede)** _(paraphrased from Adler, 1997)_
A Uruguayan doctor at a major California hospital works with a Filipina nurse. He notices she is using a patient-treatment machine incorrectly, instructs her on the correct procedure, and asks if she understands; she says yes. Two hours later the patient is doing poorly because she has kept administering the treatment incorrectly. He questions her again and she again affirms she understands. Discussion: _What went wrong? How would you resolve this situation?_ (The teaching point: in a high-power-distance culture the nurse may not contradict or admit non-understanding to a higher-status doctor.) Source: Adler, N.J. (1997) _International Dimensions of Organisational Behaviour._

### Universalism vs Particularism (Trompenaars)

Spectrum from **Universalism** (Northern Europe, UK, North America, Australia/NZ) → middle (South Africa, Latin Europe, Central/Eastern Europe, Chile, southern Brazil, northern Mexico, Hong Kong, Singapore) → **Particularism** (the Arab World, most of Africa, Asia, and Latin America).

**Case Study II — Universalism (Trompenaars)** _(paraphrased)_
You're a passenger in a car driven by a close friend who hits a pedestrian. You know he was doing at least 60 km/h in a 40 km/h zone; there are no witnesses. His lawyer says that if you testify under oath that he was only doing 40 km/h, it may save him from serious consequences. Question: given your obligations as a sworn witness vs. your obligation to your friend, would you testify he was going 40 km/h?

**"Would YOU Testify?" — Trompenaars & Hampden-Turner (2011) data**
Percentages who would **NOT** testify falsely (Universalistic / rule-oriented): Switzerland 97%, United States 93%, Canada 93%, Ireland 92%, Sweden 92%.
Percentages who **WOULD** testify falsely (Particularistic / relationship-oriented): Venezuela 68%, Nepal 64%, South Korea 63%, Russia 56%, China 53%.
Source: Trompenaars, F. & Hampden-Turner, C. (2011) _Riding the Waves of Culture: Understanding Cultural Diversity in Business_ (2nd ed.).

**Universalism vs Particularism — contrasts**

- **Universalism (rule-focus):** willing to do business with strangers; relationships can be agreed quickly; rely primarily on written agreements; impersonal and contract-based (detailed contracts); achievement is important.
- **Particularism (relationship-focus):** prefer doing business within a personal network; relationships require time; often require personal contact to obtain a reply/action; personal and trust-based (flexible contracts — too much detail signals lack of trust); trust/cooperation are important.

### Time Management — Monochronic vs Polychronic (Hall)

Spectrum from **Monochronic** (Nordic & Germanic Europe, North America, Japan) → middle (Australia/NZ, Russia & East-Central Europe, Southern Europe, Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan, China, South Korea) → **Polychronic** (the Arab World, Africa, Latin America, South & Southeast Asia).

- **Monochronic:** linear, sequential approach to tasks; punctuality valued (keeping someone waiting can be an insult); planning is important but sometimes too rigid; acceptable to cut a conversation short for an appointment; business meetings rarely interrupted.
- **Polychronic:** non-linear, simultaneous approach to tasks; schedules flexible/loose to respond in the moment; insulting to cut a conversation short for another appointment; events occur in parallel (meetings-within-meetings).
  Source: Gesteland, R.R. (2005) _Cross-cultural Business Behavior._ Copenhagen Business School Press.

### Communication — Low vs High Context (Hall)

Spectrum from **Low Context (direct)** (Northern Europe, UK, North America, Australia/NZ) → middle (South Africa, Central & Eastern Europe, Hong Kong, Singapore) → **High Context (indirect)** (the Arab World, most of Africa, Asia, and Latin America, Mediterranean Europe).

**Miscommunications illustration** — the same request ("close the door") expressed along a low-context → high-context gradient: "Close the door." → "Can you close the door?" → "It might help to close the door." → "Would you mind awfully if I asked you to close the door?" → "Did you forget the door?" → "It's getting cold in here." → "I don't want the cats to get out of the house." (Top = most direct/low-context; bottom = most indirect/high-context.)

- **Low Context (direct):** deal-focused (gets to the point quickly); precise and transparent; direct questions; criticism can be blunt; expect instructions to be clear and specific.
- **High Context (indirect):** relationship-focused (does not like to say "no"); indirect and ambiguous, especially if the message is negative; avoids direct questions; criticism is often disguised; instructions that are too specific can be insulting.

## GLOBE Study — Leadership Attributes

- **GLOBE** aim: to describe how different cultures view leadership behaviours in others.
- GLOBE respondents identified **22 valued (positive) leadership attributes**.
- **8 leadership attributes** viewed as obstacles to effective leadership.
- Source: House, R.J., Hanges, P.J., Javidan, M., Dorfman, P.W., & Gupta, V. (Eds.) (2004). _Culture, Leadership, and Organisations: The GLOBE Study of 62 Societies._ Sage.

### Universally Positive Leader Attributes (House et al., 2004)

Trustworthy, Just, Honest, Foresight, Plans ahead, Encouraging, Positive, Dynamic, Motive arouser, Confidence builder, Motivational, Dependable, Intelligent, Decisive, Effective bargainer, Win–win problem solver, Communicative, Informed, Administrative skilled, Coordinator, Team builder, Excellence oriented.

### Universally Negative Leader Attributes (House et al., 2004)

Loner, Asocial, Noncooperative, Irritable, Nonexplicit, Egocentric, Ruthless, Dictatorial.

## Applied Considerations

### Incentive systems in business (cultural fit)

American MNCs and MBAs often implement reward systems that reflect American culture:

- Individual, not group rewards.
- Measurable achievement, not seniority or loyalty.
- Money and prestige, not job security.
- High-risk and high-reward (variable pay), not stability.
  **Key caution:** _Do not assume that a widely-used system is suitable for all cultures._
  Source: Sanchez-Runde, C.J., & Steers, R.M. (2003). "Cultural influences on work motivation and performance." In Porter, Bigley & Steers (Eds.), _Motivation and Work Behaviour_ (7th ed.), pp. 357–374. McGraw-Hill.

## In-class Reflective Activities

### "HUMAN" empathy activity (Yann Arthus-Bertrand)

Framing from the film _HUMAN_ (Yann Arthus-Bertrand collected real-life stories from 2,000 people across 60 countries). Prompt: _What is it that makes us human?_ — that we love? that we fight? that we laugh? that we cry? our curiosity? our quest for discovery?

**Activity (5 mins):** In silence (no speaking), for 30 seconds each: (1) find a person you feel you're completely opposite to and simply observe each other; (2) find a person you feel is most like you and do the same; (3) walk around the room, and on "stop" turn to the nearest person and do the same. The point is to notice what you become aware of without words.

### "Cultural Differences" — Fears & Meaning of Life
