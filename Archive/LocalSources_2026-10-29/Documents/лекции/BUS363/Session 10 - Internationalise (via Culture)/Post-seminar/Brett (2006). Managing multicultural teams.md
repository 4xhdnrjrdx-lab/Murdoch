## Premise

Multicultural teams are assembled to capture real gains — knowledge of different product markets, culturally sensitive customer service, 24-hour work rotations — but cultural differences can create subtle obstacles to teamwork that go unrecognised until significant damage is done.

The article's framing example: a major international software developer built an India–US team to ship a product fast. The two sides disagreed on the timeline (Americans: 2–3 weeks; Indians: 2–3 months). The Indian members were reluctant to report production setbacks, which the Americans only discovered at handoff. Conflict over dates and feedback turned personal and disrupted even routine communication. The manager intervened — and the unintended result was that **both sides became dependent on him for minor operational decisions** the team should have handled itself. The manager got bogged down, the schedule collapsed, and the team never learned to work together.

**Central thesis:** the wrong kind of managerial intervention can sideline valuable members or create resistance and poor performance. The real challenge is to (a) recognise the **underlying cultural causes** of conflict, and (b) intervene in ways that both get the team back on track _and_ empower members to handle future challenges themselves. Cultural challenges are manageable if managers and members pick the right strategy and avoid imposing single-culture approaches on multicultural situations.

## Model 1 — The Four Challenges (barriers to team success)

People assume the issue is just communication style, but the research identifies **four** categories of barrier.

### 1. Direct vs. indirect communication

- Western communication is typically **direct and explicit** — meaning is on the surface; the listener needs little context. Many other cultures use **indirect** communication, where meaning is embedded in how the message is delivered.
- In cross-cultural negotiation, a non-Westerner can usually decode the Westerner's direct style, but the Westerner struggles to read the non-Westerner's indirect cues (e.g. inferring preferences from small changes — or no change — in a proposal rather than from direct "A or B?" questions).
- **Example (US–Japan customer-data interface):** the American manager learned that a Japanese "yes" often meant "I'm listening," not agreement. When she flagged system flaws bluntly by email (to her US boss and Japanese colleagues), her boss valued the directness but the Japanese colleagues were embarrassed — she had violated their norm about how problems are surfaced. They responded by giving her **less access** to people and information, and ultimately **isolated her** (literally relocating her to a storeroom). The direct approach launched the project problem-free but damaged the relationship and her ability to catch later problems. The indirect alternative would have been to raise issues as hypothetical questions.
- Communication challenges harm teamwork by **reducing information sharing**, **creating interpersonal conflict**, or both.

### 2. Trouble with accents and fluency

- Even though English is the language of international business, accents, limited fluency, and translation/usage problems cause misunderstanding and frustration — and can distort perceptions of **status or competence**.
- **Example:** a Latin American consultant felt the language gap robbed him of the words to express good ideas, so the US colleague led interviews despite them being peers. In a US–Japan retail-expansion team, one American dismissed less-fluent Japanese consultants as unintelligent and valueless — risking overestimating opportunity and underestimating challenges by ignoring their expertise.
- The most expert member may be the least fluent; if teammates grow impatient, conflict arises, non-native speakers contribute less, and the organisation's investment in the team fails to pay off.
- **Positive use of language differences:** a US–Latin American buying team negotiating in Korea (in English) noticed the Koreans caucusing in Korean. They responded by pretending to caucus in Spanish (discussing only sports/news in case any Korean understood). This **indirectly** signalled that the side conversations were annoying — and both teams cut back on sidebars.

### 3. Differing attitudes toward hierarchy and authority

- Teams are designed to be **flat**, but members from **hierarchical** cultures can be uncomfortable on flat teams. Deferring to higher-status members looks appropriate to a hierarchical majority but can **damage credibility and cause humiliation** if the majority is **egalitarian**.
- **Example:** a manager of Mexican heritage on a bank credit/underwriting team framed everything as open-ended questions out of cultural humility/respect — which Americans misread as not knowing the material.
- **Example (Korea–US due diligence):** struggling to get information, the American team complained **directly to higher-level Korean management**, nearly wrecking the deal. In Korean organisations hierarchy is strict — the lower-level Korean members, not the Americans, should have raised it with their bosses, and those members were mortified at being bypassed. It took senior US managers travelling to Korea (showing respect) to resolve it.

### 4. Conflicting norms for decision making

- Cultures differ on **how fast** decisions are made and **how much analysis** comes first. US managers tend to decide quickly with relatively little analysis.
- **Example:** in a Korea-bound negotiation, after agreeing three points the US–Spanish side wanted to move to point four, but the Korean side wanted to re-discuss points one to three first ("My boss almost had an attack").
- **Lesson:** the American pace can't be imposed; others may withhold information until they grasp the full project scope. Best fix = **minor concessions on process** and mutual respect. US managers learned to keep impatient bosses out of meetings and give brief frequent updates; managers from other cultures learned to state needs explicitly ("we have to see the big picture before details").

## Model 2 — The Four Strategies (for dealing with the challenges)

There's no single right fix. **Step 1:** identify the type of challenge. **Step 2 (more crucial):** assess the **"enabling situational conditions"** — e.g. does the project allow flexibility for change, or do deadlines forbid it? Are extra resources available? Is the team permanent or temporary? Does the manager have autonomy to change the team? Only then choose a strategy.

### 1. Adaptation (the ideal strategy)

Work with or around the challenge **without** changing membership or assignments. Works when members **acknowledge and name** their cultural differences and take responsibility for living with them. Preferred because it uses **less managerial time** and members **learn from solving it themselves**.

- **Example:** a US software engineer in Ireland, stressed by the in-your-face Israeli arguing style, adapted by imposing some structure (staying thoroughly prepared) and realising the confrontation was **cultural, not personal** — the Israelis confronted each other too yet worked well together.
- **Example:** an American on a pre-merger team came to see that involving all the French managers (even those not directly involved) mattered for the project to work.
- **Fusion example (US–UK):** the US side wanted to "forge ahead," the UK side wanted to "think this through" (the US muttered about "analysis paralysis"). The team did **both** — moving not quite as fast as the US wanted nor as thoroughly as the UK wanted. "**Fusion**" (a concept also studied by political scientists/governments managing multicultural populations who want to protect cultures rather than assimilate them) let the team catch pitfalls the US-only approach would have missed and avoid the over-analysis the UK-only approach would have caused.

### 2. Structural intervention

A deliberate **reorganisation or reassignment** to reduce friction or remove a conflict source. Effective when **obvious subgroups** demarcate the team (e.g. HQ vs national subsidiaries) or when members are proud, defensive, threatened, or clinging to negative stereotypes.

- **Example (3 "tribes": Europe/UK/US investment research team):** the manager held **twice-yearly face-to-face** meetings — not for daily problems but to **agree shared values**. Noticing everyone "shut down" when he (the boss) spoke, he hired a **consultant** (no hierarchical threat) to run meetings, which boosted participation.
- **Example (Japan retail team):** female Japanese consultants wouldn't speak up in a large group or in front of their male superior, so the manager split the team into **smaller mixed subgroups**, rotating membership so everyone got to know and respect each other.
- **Risk of subgrouping:** it can buffer people who aren't participating, and the pieces must be reassembled later — so it relies on **someone acting as a mediator** to fit the parts together.

### 3. Managerial intervention

The manager makes decisions / sets direction. If a manager acts as **arbitrator/judge** with no team involvement, neither side gains insight into the stalemate — but intervention can work if done well.

- **Setting norms early** is the high-value form: e.g. a software team's manager told members up front they were chosen for **task expertise, not English fluency**, and the team would work around language issues; at customer-service stage he had them pre-empt accent issues by inviting customers to ask them to repeat.
- **Respecting hierarchy while escalating:** unlike the Americans who breached Korean etiquette, an American refinery-safety expert in China escalated **through the proper levels in both organisations** (her Beijing office ↔ the refinery's upper management), so the Chinese side understood she was helping, not insulting — and the fire/safety issues got resolved.

### 4. Exit (last resort)

Removing a member when other options fail — voluntary or by management request. Rare in **project-based** teams (unhappy members often just wait out the project); used in **permanent** teams when **emotions are too high and too much face has been lost** to salvage the situation.

- **Example:** a Greek woman and a Polish man (senior consultants) clashed over approach; professional differences became personal; the Greek consultant ultimately left the firm — a poor fit.

## The Strategy-Selection Exhibit ("Identifying the Right Strategy")

Match the strategy to the **representative problem**, the **enabling situational conditions**, and watch the **complicating factors**:

| Strategy                    | Representative problems                                                                                    | Enabling situational conditions                                                                                                                                     | Complicating factors                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Adaptation**              | Conflict from decision-making differences; misunderstanding/stonewalling from communication differences    | Members can attribute a challenge to **culture rather than personality**; higher-level managers unavailable or the team would be embarrassed to involve them        | Members must be exceptionally aware; negotiating a common understanding takes time                                                        |
| **Structural intervention** | Emotional tensions from fluency issues or prejudice; members inhibited by **perceived status differences** | The team can be **subdivided** to mix cultures/expertise; tasks can be subdivided                                                                                   | If members aren't carefully distributed, subgroups can **strengthen pre-existing differences**; subgroup solutions must fit back together |
| **Managerial intervention** | Violations of hierarchy causing **loss of face**; absence of ground rules causing conflict                 | The problem has produced **high emotion**; the team has reached a **stalemate**; a higher-level manager is able and willing to intervene; the team is **permanent** | The team becomes **overly dependent on the manager**                                                                                      |
| **Exit**                    | A member cannot adjust and can no longer contribute                                                        | **Emotions beyond the point of intervention**; **too much face has been lost**                                                                                      | Talent and training costs are lost; members may be sidelined or resistant                                                                 |

## Closing arguments

- Cultural challenges, serious in their own right, can also **unmask fundamental managerial problems**.
- The teams that did best: managers who **intervened early and set norms**; teams/managers who **structured social interaction** to engage everyone; and teams that saw problems as **stemming from culture, not personality** and approached them with humour and creativity.
- Managers who only step in at stalemate may restart the team but rarely **empower it to self-correct** next time.
- **Two self-solved examples:**
  - _Financial-services Spanish-language call centre (North + Latin Americans):_ one Latin American agent took twice as long because of cultural **chit-chat** and didn't know how to end calls politely. Confronted as a "free rider," she acknowledged it; teammates **rallied**, breaking into her overtime calls to "rescue" her — a short-term fix, and she improved long-term.
  - _Companywide IT project (Indian manager + Singapore teammate, Japanese members):_ the Japanese said "yes" but under-delivered. Rather than escalate up the hierarchy, the manager built **consensus with the whole Japanese IT team** via an "**eBusiness road show**" (lunch presentation of success stories aligning with company priorities). The Japanese team wanted to be spotlighted in future road shows — and the team gelled with **no higher-level manager involved**.

## Key terms & references

**Two core frameworks:**

- **Four challenges:** (1) direct vs indirect communication; (2) trouble with accents & fluency; (3) differing attitudes toward hierarchy & authority; (4) conflicting norms for decision making.
- **Four strategies:** (1) **Adaptation** (work around, members self-solve — the ideal); (2) **Structural intervention** (reshape the team / subgroups); (3) **Managerial intervention** (set norms early / escalate respectfully); (4) **Exit** (remove a member — last resort).

**Other concepts:**

- **Enabling situational conditions** — the circumstances that determine which strategy fits (flexibility, resources, permanence, manager autonomy).
- **Fusion** — combining cultural approaches (vs integrate/assimilate) to reach higher-quality decisions.
- **Loss of face**, **culture as cause vs personality as cause**, **subgrouping + mediation**, **norm-setting**.
- **Egalitarian vs hierarchical cultures**; **direct vs indirect communication**.

**Cultural contrasts illustrated:** US (fast decisions, direct, egalitarian), Japan (indirect, harmony, hierarchy), Korea (strict hierarchy, deliberate decisions), Israel (argumentative/direct), UK (deliberative), France (hierarchical, inclusive meetings), Mexico/Latin America (humility, indirect, relational chit-chat).
