## What's actually visible (opening paragraph — paraphrased)

- The value of learning from failure is beyond dispute, yet organisations that do it well are extremely rare — and the gap isn't due to lack of commitment to learning.
- Across 20 years studying many sectors — pharmaceuticals, financial services, product design, telecommunications, construction, hospitals, and NASA's space shuttle program — managers genuinely wanted their organisations to learn from failures to improve future performance.
- Many had spent hours on **after-action reviews, postmortems**, and similar exercises, but these painstaking efforts repeatedly produced no real change.
- The reason Edmondson identifies: those managers were **thinking about failure the wrong way.**

## The article's core framework [from general knowledge of the article — verify against full text]

The following is the widely-cited structure of this well-known HBR piece. It is **not** extracted from the paywalled text — treat as a study pointer and confirm details if you cite them:

- **Not all failures are created equal.** Edmondson argues organisations should stop treating "failure" as uniformly bad. She presents a **spectrum of reasons for failure**, ranging from **blameworthy** to **praiseworthy** — e.g. deliberate deviation / sabotage, inattention, lack of ability, process inadequacy, task difficulty, process complexity, uncertainty, hypothesis testing, and **exploratory experimentation**.
- **Few failures are truly blameworthy;** many are not. Yet managers tend to treat most failures as blameworthy, so people hide failures rather than reporting and learning from them.
- **Building a learning culture** requires **psychological safety** — an environment where people feel safe to report and discuss failures honestly.
- **Detecting, analysing, and deliberately producing failures:** organisations should get better at _detecting_ failure early, _analysing_ it rigorously (overcoming the natural reluctance), and even _intentionally experimenting_ to produce "intelligent failures" at the frontier of knowledge.
- **Context matters:** the appropriate stance toward failure varies by context — routine/predictable operations (manufacturing) vs complex operations (hospitals) vs novel/frontier work (R&D), with different mixes of blameworthy-to-praiseworthy causes.
