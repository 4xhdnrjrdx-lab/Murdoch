## Core message

- When we slip up we're often filled with shame and our instinct is to hide. Edmondson's argument: people and businesses should instead **applaud smart risk-taking** even when it doesn't work out, and **closely examine failures to learn from them**.

## Edmondson's own founding failure (the origin of "psychological safety")

- ~30 years ago, as a Harvard doctoral student (1993), Edmondson studied two hospitals, hypothesising that **better-functioning teams would make fewer medical errors**. The data showed the **opposite** — better teams appeared to have _more_ errors.
- It felt awful (disappointment, shame, fear, embarrassment); she briefly considered dropping out.
- Instead of giving in to avoidance, she looked harder at the data. One survey item stood out: _"If you make a mistake in this unit, it won't be held against you."_ The more a team agreed with that, the higher its **reported** error rate.
- Insight: the good teams weren't making more errors — they were **reporting** more, because members felt safe admitting mistakes. This led her to develop the concept of **psychological safety**, which launched her career. (Born from a failure.)

## Feeling safe to fail

- Many people cling to **perfectionism** and won't give themselves permission to fall short. Failure triggers aversion, confusion and fear — emotions that distract from understanding _why_ it happened and recovering.
- These reactions are **evolutionary**: we're risk-averse and care about others' regard because, long ago, group rejection could mean death. But in today's uncertain, interdependent marketplace those old habits don't fit — failures are inevitable.
- This isn't a licence to slack: still strive and do your best work, but don't beat yourself up over the inevitability of sometimes falling short. "Take a deep breath and be OK with not being perfect."

## Normalising failure ("We are going to fail all day!")

- Because everyone is fallible, we don't have to fail alone. The best workplaces create a **psychologically safe space** to speak up about failures.
- Example — **Jen Heemstra** (chemistry professor, Emory University): shaped by the low of failing to get tenure earlier, she now constantly normalises failure in her lab, telling students **95% of experiments fail** and cheerfully saying "We are going to fail all day!" This lets people laugh off silly mistakes, stop self-punishing over thoughtful failed experiments, and focus energy on new solutions (e.g. a grad student cycled through wrong methods to isolate a type of RNA before finding a novel reagent that worked).
- Manager's role: **make failure discussable.** Don't belittle or dismiss people; encourage transparency so that when things go wrong, the team learns the most it can. Also **reward smart risk-taking** even when experiments don't pan out, rather than punishing inevitable disappointments.

## Four steps to failing successfully (reframing failure)

1. **Persistence** — Example: **Sara Blakely**, whose father asked her and her brother nightly what they'd _failed_ at that day, turning each stumble into something to celebrate. Met with rejection on her footless body-shaping stockings, she kept retooling marketing and the patent application until someone took a chance — becoming **Spanx**, one of the most successful fashion startups ever. "Accepting fallibility… is about putting you on the hook to do the very best you can."
2. **Reflection** — There's a fine line between persistence and stubbornness; reflection bridges it. The best failure-learners don't wait to fail before reflecting — they keep a **practice journal** or similar to gather data during projects _even when things go well_, so they can see what went right/wrong and keep improving.
3. **Accountability** — Shame tempts us to blame others; instead, focusing on **your own contribution** helps lead to a solution. Ask: "What did I do or not do that could have made a difference in preventing the undesired outcome?" Hard, but empowering — it gives you agency to redeploy going forward.
4. **Apologising** — When your failure harmed someone (intentionally or not), apologise: keep the focus on your own contribution, express remorse, accept responsibility, and offer to change or make amends — rather than making excuses or digging in.

## Key terms & references

- **Psychological safety:** an environment where people feel safe to admit/report mistakes — Edmondson's signature concept, born from her 1993 hospital study.
- **Reframing failure / four steps:** persistence, reflection, accountability, apologising.
- **Normalising failure / "make failure discussable";** rewarding smart risk-taking; perfectionism as a trap; fallibility as fact.
- **Examples:** Edmondson's hospital-teams study; Jen Heemstra (Emory, "95% of experiments fail"); Sara Blakely / Spanx.
- **Author/source:** Michael Blanding for HBS Working Knowledge, featuring Amy C. Edmondson; book _Right Kind of Wrong_ (2023).
- **Related pieces:** "Failing Well: How Your 'Intelligent Failure' Unlocks Your Full Potential"; "Four Steps to Building the Psychological Safety That High-Performing Teams Need."
