# BUS293 — Topic 8 Podcast: Power, Politics and Influence

> Lecture/podcast transcript. Topic looks at the meaning of power and politics and their effects at both the **interpersonal** and **organisational** levels. Power, politics and influence are important managerial tools, but their use can also cause detrimental effects.

## What is Power?

- **Power** is the capacity to influence others. It exists when someone perceives that, to gain something of value, they are **dependent** on another.
- The dependent person also holds **countervailing power**. Example: a manager has greater power to provide a job and pay, but the employee has countervailing power by providing skills and knowledge the company and manager need.
- Power also requires some level of **trust** between the parties.

## Sources / Bases of Power

Sources (bases) of power are categories for the perceived things of value that a power-holder holds. **Five main sources**, sorted into two categories:

**Position power:**

- **Legitimate power** — an agreement that people in certain roles can request certain behaviours of others (e.g. managers telling employees what tasks to perform; one department withholding information from others). Legitimate power is greater when:
  - the person has increased levels of trust with the power-holder;
  - the person values conformity and tradition;
  - the culture has **high power distance**.
  - It also exists when someone owes you a favour — the **norm of reciprocity** (social obligation/expectation to help someone who has already helped you) creates an informal rule of conduct.
- **Reward power** — derived from the ability to control the allocation of rewards valued by others and to remove negative sanctions (e.g. managers controlling promotions, pay, vacation schedules).
- **Coercive power** — the ability to apply punishment. Extends beyond the threat of dismissal to badgering employees for promised work or criticising work. These actions carry risks such as lack of future cooperation.

**Personal power:**

- **Expert power** — the capacity to influence others by possessing knowledge or skills they value; many people respond to expertise just as they respond to authority. An important form is the (perceived) ability to **manage uncertainties** in the business environment. You can gain expert power more quickly by being seen with someone whose expertise is respected.
- **Referent power** — when others identify with, like, or otherwise respect you. Generally associated with **charisma**.

## Four Contingencies of Power

Determine whether power sources translate into **real** power:

1. **Substitutability** — availability of alternatives. Individuals/work units are more powerful when **non-substitutable** (lack of alternatives). Can be reduced by controlling tasks, knowledge, and labour, and by differentiating from competitors.
2. **Centrality** — the power-holder's importance in terms of interdependence with others. High when the number of people affected is large and people are quickly affected by the power-holder's actions.
3. **Visibility** — power increases to the extent that a person's or work unit's competencies are known to others (e.g. why professionals display degrees and certificates on their office wall).
4. **Discretion** — the freedom to exercise judgment. Power increases when people have freedom to use their power. Explains the general lack of power of **front-line supervisors**: they have some sources of power but generally lack permission (organisational policy/procedures) to use it, so in real terms have little actual power.

## Consequences of Power

Depend on the type of power held:

- **Power over yourself and your work (empowerment)** — tends to increase motivation, job satisfaction, organisational commitment and job performance. However, the feeling of being in control can lead to **automatic thinking** rather than mindful thinking: increased reliance on stereotyping, difficulty empathising, and generally less accurate perceptions than people with less power.
- **Power over others** — the legitimate, reward, and coercive power managers have over employees can produce a sense of **duty or responsibility** towards the people they have power over, usually making them more mindful of their actions and less likely to engage in stereotyping.

## Influence

- Power is only the **capacity** to influence; it doesn't automatically create influence.
- **Influence** is the behaviour attempting to alter someone's attitudes or behaviour.
- Influence occurs in **all directions**: managers → employees (complete tasks); workers → co-workers (help with tasks); employees → managers (improve chances of career success).

### The 8 Influence Tactics (Hard vs Soft)

**5 hard influence tactics** (supported by **position power**):

- **Silent authority** — the most common form of influence in high power distance countries; occurs through using legitimate power without specifically referring to that base (e.g. complying with a request from your manager).
- **Assertiveness** — actively applying legitimate and coercive power to change an attitude or behaviour (e.g. reminding an employee of their obligations, or checking their work). Extreme assertiveness can, in certain situations, be considered **workplace bullying** — use with caution.
- **Information control** — selective distribution of information to cause others to change attitudes/behaviour (e.g. CEOs influencing their board through selective withholding of information).
- **Coalition formation** — used when an individual lacks sufficient power alone; works by pooling resources and suggesting broad support, tapping into the **social identity formation** process (from an earlier topic). Unions use this to influence employers on a range of issues.
- **Upward appeal** — relying on the support of a higher authority to validate your position (e.g. stating that something should be done because organisational policy supports that method).

**3 soft influence tactics** (supported by **personal power**):

- **Persuasion** — one of the most effective strategies for career success; uses facts, logical arguments, and emotional appeals to change beliefs, attitudes and behaviours. Increased when the person is seen to have **expertise and credibility**, doesn't profit from the persuasion, and acknowledges counter-arguments.
- **Impression management** — actively shaping the perception others have of us through self-presentation and other means. Common for workplace advancement; involves developing a **personal brand** (competitive advantages — what sets you apart). A subcategory is **ingratiation** (e.g. complimenting bosses or asking them for advice), which only works at low levels — **excessive ingratiation is seen as insincere and self-serving**.
- **Exchange** — transactional influence involving the promise of benefits or resources in exchange for compliance. **Negotiation** is integral, as is the **norm of reciprocity** (social expectation of returning a favour).

**Choosing a tactic:** 'Soft' tactics (friendly persuasion, subtle ingratiation) are generally more acceptable than 'hard' tactics (upward appeal, assertiveness). The most appropriate tactic depends on:

- the influencer's **power base**;
- whether the target is **higher, lower, or at the same level** in the organisation;
- **personal, organisational and cultural values** regarding influence behaviour.

## Organisational Politics

- **Definition:** influence tactics that others perceive to be **self-serving** behaviours at the expense of others and sometimes contrary to the organisation's interests.
- **Positive** if they do not harm others, promote organisational interests, and contribute to organisational justice, decision making, conflict resolution, and relationship maintenance.
- **Negative** (diverting resources away from the organisation) → can lower job satisfaction, organisational commitment, morale, and cause **retention issues**.
- Use of influence is generally seen as positive if the individual has high **political skill** — socially astute, interpersonal influence and networking ability, and appearing sincere.
- **More common when:** ambiguous decisions allocate scarce resources, and when the organisation tolerates or rewards political behaviour. Individuals with a **high need for personal power** have a higher propensity to use political tactics.
- **Minimised by:**
  - providing clear rules for resource allocation;
  - establishing a free flow of information;
  - using education and involvement during organisational change;
  - supporting team norms and a corporate culture that discourages dysfunctional politics;
  - having leaders who model **organisational citizenship** rather than political savvy.

## Power in Social Networks

- People can receive power through **social capital**, with the main sources being **referent power** and **legitimate power** (via the norm of reciprocity).
- The contingency of **centrality** becomes relevant: having **weak ties** with people across many diverse networks can be more valuable than a few **strong ties** with people in similar networks.
- The power social networks create is a natural element of organisations, but can be a formidable barrier to those not already connected — "boys clubs" in certain managerial areas are an example.

> Power and influence are important to recognise and navigate in an organisation, as their effects on both the interpersonal and organisational levels are consequential.

## Key terms / frameworks

- **Power** (capacity to influence), dependence, countervailing power, trust.
- **Five sources of power:** legitimate, reward, coercive (position power); expert, referent (personal power).
- **Norm of reciprocity**; high power distance.
- **Four contingencies of power:** substitutability, centrality, visibility, discretion.
- **Consequences:** empowerment (power over self) vs power over others; automatic vs mindful thinking.
- **Influence** (all directions).
- **8 influence tactics:** Hard — silent authority, assertiveness, information control, coalition formation, upward appeal. Soft — persuasion, impression management (incl. ingratiation, personal brand), exchange (negotiation, reciprocity).
- **Organisational politics**; political skill; need for personal power; organisational citizenship.
- **Social capital / social networks:** weak ties vs strong ties; centrality.
