Welcome back to BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour. This topic examines the how
the structural elements of an organisation can be selected and combined into a unique design
to improve the performance of an organisation and the individuals within it.
We previously looked at job design and how individual jobs are structured. Division of labour
is the level above that, how the work is subdivided into separate jobs assigned to different
people. Once the work is divided, putting it all together requires coordination, and this is
achieved through informal communication, formal hierarchy, and standardisation.
Informal communication is sharing information on mutual tasks and forming common mental
models to synchronise work activities. It is relied on by all organisations and is vital in non-
routine and ambiguous situations. As the number of employees in an organisation increases,
informal communication becomes chaotic, and so liaison roles and temporary cross-functional
teams are created to help structure the communication. In larger organisations, formal
hierarchies are used for coordination, assigning legitimate power to people to direct work
efforts. In the past, managers believed that formal hierarchies were the best method for
coordinating large organisations, however it makes them inflexible and in the increasingly
complex and novel situations of todays world, a formal hierarchy can quickly lead to problems.
Additionally, as the business grows, more and more layers of management are needed to
coordinate this hierarchy, and it becomes a costly bureaucratic process, and reduces employee
autonomy. Coordination through standardisation involves creating routine patterns of
behaviour or output, and depending on the nature of the organisation or task, standardising
either processes, outputs, or skills may be more effective.
How the work, that is the employees and their activities, are grouped together is
departmentalisation. It establishes the chain of command, focuses people around common
mental models and encourages coordination through informal communication among people
and subunits.
A simple structure is evident in most small, owner-operated business, where there are few
people and products or services offered, and employees perform broadly defined jobs reporting
to the owner. It depends on the owner providing direct supervision and becomes incredibly
difficult to continue with as an organisation grows.
A functional structure organises employees around specific knowledge or other resources. This
structure fosters greater specialisation and improves direct supervision, but it weakens the focus
on serving clients or developing products.
A divisional structure groups employees around geographic areas, clients or outputs. This
structure accommodates growth and focuses employee attention on products or customers
rather than on tasks. However, this structure also duplicates resources and creates silos of
knowledge.
Team-based structures are very flat, with low formalisation, and organise self-directed teams
around work processes rather than functional specialties. The matrix structure combines two
structures to leverage the benefits of both types. However, this approach requires more
coordination than functional or pure divisional structures, may dilute accountability, and
increases conflict. A network structure is an alliance of several organisations for the purpose
of creating a product or serving a client.
The basic elements of organisational structure are span of control, centralisation, and
formalisation . span of control is the number of people directly reporting to the next level in
the hierarchy, and the optimal span of control depends on the presence of coordinating
mechanisms other than formal hierarchy, whether employees perform routine tasks, and how
much interdependence there is among employees within the department.
Centralisation occurs when formal decision authority is held by a small group of people,
typically senior executives. Many companies decentralise and delegate decision making
authority as they become larger and more complex, but some sections of the company may
remain centralised while other sections decentralise.
Formalisation is the degree to which organisations standardise behaviour through rules,
procedures, formal training and related mechanisms. Companies become more formalised as
they get older and larger. Formalisation tends to reduce organisational flexibility,
organisational learning, creativity and job satisfaction.
A mechanistic structure is characterised by a narrow span of control, high centralisation and
high formalisation. Mechanistic structures have many rules and procedures, limited decision
making at lower levels, tall hierarchies and vertical communication flows. They operate better
in stable environments because they rely on efficiency and routine behaviours.
An organic structure is characterised by a wide span of control, low centralisation and low
formalisation. Organic structures are better in dynamic, rapidly changing environments
because they are more flexible and responsive to the changes. They are also more compatible
with organisational learning and high-performance work practices because they emphasise
information sharing and an empowered work force. Organic structures are reliant on employees
having well developed roles and expertise, so they are able to coordinate effectively.
There is no one size fits all for effective organisational structure, and what will be the best for
a specific organisation will depend on their external environment, size, technology, and
strategy. This is consistent with the open-systems perspective of organisational effectiveness.
If the environment is dynamic, not stable, a more organic structure will be needed so the
organisation can adapt more quickly to change. If the environment is complex, there will need
to be decentralisation, so the people and subunits with the necessary information are able to
make the most informed choices. If the environment is diverse in its products, services, clients,
or regions, then the organisation will need a divisional structure aligned with that diversity.
And if the environment is hostile, with resource scarcity and increased competition, then again,
an organic structure tends to do better as they are able to adapt.
Another contingency is size. As organisations grow, they tend to need to decentralise decisions
to those who have the information to make the best decisions, and they make greater use of
standardisation as a coordination mechanism, however informal communication is regaining
its importance in large firms through an increased emphasis on empowerment and emerging
information technologies
The contingency of technology refers to the mechanisms or processes an organisation relies on
to make its products and services. If there is low variability and high analysability, that is if
there are few exceptions to standard procedure and it is reasonable predictable, such as an
assembly line, then a mechanistic structure would be more suitable. However if there is high
variability and low analysability, such as in a research setting, then an organic structure should
be introduced.
The previous contingencies influence but do not necessarily determine structure. Instead,
corporate leaders formulate and implement strategies that shape both the characteristics of
these contingencies and the organisation’s resulting structure. For example, the strategy
determines the organisations growth targets, and thus size, or if the strategy is to compete
through innovation, then this would recommend an organic structure to increase creativity.
Over time, there has been a trend for organisations to move away from hierarchical
organisational structures to more flat, agile, team-based structures in many large organisations.
This is possibly due to the increasingly dynamic and unpredictable external environment
requiring organisations to be flexible and responsive, however every organisation needs to be
aware of their specific contingencies, and ensure their structure is fit for purpose. Thank you.
