**SLIDE 4 — Critical Thinking Questions (section)**
McShane and Von Glinow (2024) Chapter 13, p491 — Questions 3, 4, 5 & 7.

**SLIDE 5 — Chapter 13 Question 3**
Administrative theorists concluded many decades ago that the most effective organisations have a narrow span of control. Yet today's top-performing manufacturing firms have a wider span of control. Why is this possible? Under what circumstances, if any, should manufacturing firms use a narrow span of control?

**SLIDE 6 — Ch13 Q3**
Flatter structures:

- less overheads (fewer middle managers, more people directly involved in the production of goods and/or services)
- greater autonomy and employee involvement (mostly due to managers having a wider span of control — more direct employees/teams means less close supervision, therefore employees/teams are given more freedom to make decisions without management review)
  A narrow span of control would be most appropriate when the workforce is: low skilled; and made up of workers with a high power distance (as the close supervision and control would be less likely to be viewed as a lack of trust).

**SLIDE 7 — Chapter 13 Question 4**
Leaders of large organisations struggle to identify the best level and types of centralisation and decentralisation. What should companies consider when determining the degree of decentralisation?

**SLIDE 8 — Ch13 Q4**
As organizations grow they tend to diversify, and as their environments become more complex it is necessary to decentralize — the burden on senior executives who must process all decisions becomes overwhelming, so dispersing decision-making authority (decentralizing) is the best response. Contingencies to consider:

- **External environment.** Decentralized structures suit complex rather than simple environments; it is best to temporarily centralize when operating in a hostile environment.
- **Organizational size.** When the organization grows larger, with more specialized jobs, more elaborate coordinating mechanisms, and greater formalization, it is better to decentralize.
- **Technology.** Where technology requires many exceptions to standard procedures (high variety) and it's difficult to standardize the transformation of inputs to outputs (low analyzability), rely on decentralized decision making.
- **Organizational strategy.** All the above contingencies are mediated by the organization's overall strategy. E.g., Johnson & Johnson's decentralized divisional structure fits its strategy of developing and delivering a diverse array of health-related products worldwide.

**SLIDE 9 — Chapter 13 Question 5**
Mechanistic and organic structures are two organisational forms. How do the three types of coordination mechanisms operate through these forms?

**SLIDE 10 — Ch13 Q5**
Three coordination mechanisms: **informal communication, formal hierarchy, standardisation.**

- **Mechanistic structures** have high formalisation and centralisation, limited lower-level decision making, and rigidly defined tasks; therefore **formal hierarchy and standardisation** are the coordination mechanisms used.
- **Organic structures** operate with a wide span of control, decentralised decision making, and little formalisation, and hence make more use of **informal communication.**

**SLIDE 11 — Chapter 13 Question 7**
From an employee perspective, what are the advantages and disadvantages of working in a matrix structure?

**SLIDE 12 — Ch13 Q7**

- **Advantages.** When properly managed, matrix structures enhance communication efficiencies, provide flexibility to balance workload with other project team members, and allow employees to interact and coordinate with others who have similar technical specialties. They require (and develop) enhanced communication and interpersonal skills, so employees may enjoy enhanced training and development opportunities. _(Note: the source slide contains a garbled clause — "lend further evidence against the argument that all of the key features of organizational structure… are found in descriptions of the several forms of departmentalization" — appears to be an editing error in the deck.)_
- **Disadvantages.** May result in increased stress, conflict, and organizational politics due to having **two bosses** with potentially divergent expectations. Some employees may struggle to adapt to the relatively fluid nature of a matrix vs. a more structured bureaucracy.

**SLIDE 13 — Case Study Questions (section)**
McShane and Von Glinow (2024) Chapter 13, Case Study: 'SLOAN & WU, INC'.

**SLIDE 14 — Case Study Question 1**
Describe the proposed changes at Sloan & Wu in terms of the four elements of organizational structure.

**SLIDE 15 — Case Study Question 1 (1/2)**

- **Span of Control** — Sloan & Wu now introduces a _narrower_ span of control at the district level but a much _wider_ one at the regional level. District managers will be responsible for an average of 10 stores (vs. 15 previously), so each manages fewer store managers, enabling closer/more effective work with each store. Merchandising and operations decision makers have been moved from regional to district offices. The new structure creates a wider regional span of control. Previously: five regions, 30 districts (avg six districts per region), plus assistant regional managers as a management layer (which may have narrowed the regional span — e.g., two assistant regional managers per region → span of 3 district managers; or one per region → six direct reports while the regional director had just one direct report). The new structure consolidates into **three regions**, removes the **assistant regional manager** layer, and expands districts from 30 to 45 — so each regional manager is now responsible for ~15 district managers.
- **Centralization/decentralization** — When the three regional retailers were acquired, Sloan & Wu centralized many activities (esp. merchandising and marketing), a key reason products were shipped to regions without demand (e.g., winter coats in Florida and Southern California). The new structure clearly **decentralizes** power — most importantly decentralizing merchandise and marketing to district and store managers ("gives more autonomy to local districts and stores to personalize merchandise and marketing for customers in that area"). The CEO calls it a "devolved" structure.

**SLIDE 16 — Case Study Question 1 (2/2)**

- **Formalization** — Increased when Sloan & Wu acquired the regional chains (standardized store designs, merchandising, marketing across all acquired stores). The new structure _reduces_ formalization to some degree because district/store managers gain autonomy (less systems control) to understand and quickly deliver what locals want ("localization"), and will have more flexible processes for working with vendors. At the same time, formalization may _increase_ through technology tracking which products are in stock at each store and documenting items, brands, garment sizes, and colors preferred by local customers — managers must abide by this increased systems feedback (a type of formalization).
- **Departmentalization** — Changes mostly in the number of units at each level: previously 30 districts across five US regions; new structure is 45 districts in three regions (East, South, West). Most other aspects of the reorganization would NOT be evident in the org chart (e.g., decentralized power, shift of merchandising/marketing/planning staff from regions and HQ to districts).

**SLIDE 17 — Case Study Question 2**
What contingencies suggest that Sloan & Wu's new organizational structure is appropriate or inappropriate?

**SLIDE 18 — Case Study Question 2 (1/3)**

- **Dynamic vs stable environment** — Customers have varied needs across regions/districts and preferences change relatively quickly (hence merchandising/marketing decentralized to district/store levels). But the environment is relatively stable in many respects (customer behavior — visiting stores, buying online, returning merchandise — is predictable over the medium term, as are supplier/vendor processes). Sloan & Wu needs a structure **between organic and mechanistic**, achieved through decentralization and somewhat less formalization at district/store levels where dynamics are felt.
- **Complex vs simple environment** — Department store chains operate in moderately complex environments (different products, diverse customers); large size and nationwide location add complexity; wide variety of products/services (clothing to white goods to restaurants) adds more. The more complex the environment, the more decentralized — which is what Sloan & Wu is doing.

**SLIDE 19 — Case Study Question 2 (2/3)**

- **Diverse vs integrated environment** — Diverse environments have greater variety of products/services, clients, regions. Sloan & Wu is diverse (diverse geography, customers with diverse needs); main diversity is likely geographic, while customer variations (age cohorts, product types) may be less distinct (clothing buyers also buy kitchen appliances). Decentralizing makes sense to accommodate area-specific variation. Less clear whether to organize into separate divisions/store brands by product or customer group. The more diversified the environment, the more a **divisional structure** aligned with that diversity is needed — but a geographic divisional structure probably isn't required here: (1) customer variation is at district/local level, not regional, and 45 divisions (one per district) would be chaotic/inefficient; (2) customers aren't "diverse" across districts in every way (vary in clothing by climate/regional style, but similar nationwide for items like kitchen appliances). So decentralization is probably sufficient without a divisional structure.

**SLIDE 20 — Case Study Question 2 (3/3)**

- **Hostile vs munificent environment** — No evidence of extreme resource scarcity/competition; lost market share due to overly standardized buying but remains competitive. Probably not an important contingency; decentralizing for other reasons also addresses any hostility.
- **Organizational size** — Relatively large, so it uses **standardization** as a coordinating mechanism (e.g., inventory management work-process systems) and elaborate formal authority (emphasis on district managers) — generally appropriate for its size, though presenting opposing challenges.
- **Technology** — Task variability and analyzability vary across employees/levels, but most employees perform tasks with fairly low variability and medium-high analyzability (predictable customer behavior and buying processes). Some task variability from changing fashion/consumer trends for some products, but trends don't dramatically alter predictability of buying/stocking/selling. With moderately low task variability and medium-high analyzability, Sloan & Wu operates best with a structure **somewhat more mechanistic than organic** — similar to the new structure.
- **Organizational strategy** — The CEO suggests a strategy shifting toward **creativity and innovation** rather than high efficiency/low cost, calling for a structure closer to **organic**. Whether the new structure is sufficiently organic is unclear, but it's moving in the recommended direction.

**SLIDE 21 — Case Study Question 3**
What problems do you think Sloan & Wu might experience with the new organizational structure? Explain why these problems might occur.

**SLIDE 22 — Case Study Question 3**
Two potential problems are stated by two retail analysts:

1. The new structure may **inflate costs** — 50% more districts than before, and merchandising/marketing now occur more at district level (likely requiring more people overall than in the regional offices). District managers have a narrower span of control ("10 vs. 115 direct reports, on average" — _в исходном слайде стоит «115», что выглядит как опечатка; по логике кейса должно быть «15»_), suggesting higher payroll costs.
2. The company's **emphasis on formal authority** as a coordinating mechanism: "It seems that centralized control is just being replaced with district manager control over the store managers and their staff" — i.e., rather than empowering district/store managers, the new structure just creates closer supervision of store managers.
   Another potential problem: decentralized decision making can create **too much variability** across the organization. Localization is valuable for some products but probably not all/most; customers visiting a store in another region may be less receptive to significant variations in store format, marketing, merchandising. Localized decision making also increases the risk of **bad and potentially unethical decisions** — beyond district-manager oversight, it isn't clear how Sloan & Wu will help managers make informed/legal decisions (e.g., district managers working directly with vendors could lead to inappropriate arrangements). Districts might also **compete with each other** for supplier merchandise; presumably HQ staff do major buying, otherwise Sloan & Wu may pay higher prices.

**SLIDE 29 — Club Ed Exercise (discussion questions)**

- How does Club Ed's organisational structure change as it grows and diversifies? What factors drive these structural changes?
- How can Club Ed structure itself as an adaptive organisation? Does it always have to react to environmental changes or are there some ways it can be proactive?

**SLIDE 30 — Club Ed Exercise — Scenario #1 (answer)**
A **functional structure** is usually developed. A couple of teams might suggest a simple structure (assuming a very small resort), but 75 employees makes a simple structure difficult. Occasionally a team recommends a team-based structure where everyone is multiskilled — possible, but the contingencies of structure suggest a more **mechanistic than organic** form works better here.

**SLIDE 31 — Club Ed Exercise — Scenario #2 (answer)**
Most teams form a **geographic divisional structure**. A divisional structure adds a management layer (usually three people leading each region). This may be reasonable with 17 properties (~five per region), but question whether the regional layer is required at all — why can't the 17 resort owners report directly to the CEO or COO? (The exercise is derived from "Club Med," which at one time had 50 resort managers reporting directly to the CEO/COO.) If a divisional structure is justified, explain why it should be geographical — possibly these resorts differ more by clientele (some more "party" oriented, some relaxed, some catering mainly to families).

**SLIDE 32 — Club Ed Exercise — Scenario #3 (answer)**
By 30 resorts, a **divisionalized structure around geography** generally makes sense; but justify why all 30 resorts couldn't report directly to the CEO (discussion is mostly about span of control and coordinating mechanisms other than direct supervision enabling such a wide span). As in Scenario #2, justify geography over customer or function (e.g., why not a VP responsible for all food services across resorts?). The **charter airline** adds complexity: it might stand alone as a division (minimal integration needed — carries resort guests but also non-guests). Some teams recommend more closely integrating the airline with the resorts; others recommend a **matrix structure** (for the resorts, not the airline) organized by geography and function (food services, housekeeping, etc.).

**SLIDE 33 — Club Ed Exercise — Scenario #4 (answer)**
Many reorganize Club Ed into a **product-based divisional structure** with three products: resorts, airline, and adventure travel — typically with a geographic divisional structure within the resorts division and a functional structure for the other two. Consider whether the structure should support **organizational integration** (leveraging resort strengths with adventure travel and the airline), or whether the three "divisions" should be mostly independent businesses with limited interdependence (i.e., Club Ed becomes a **holding company**). Answers depend on whether the businesses need to be interdependent and, if so, how Club Ed can build synergies across divisions.
