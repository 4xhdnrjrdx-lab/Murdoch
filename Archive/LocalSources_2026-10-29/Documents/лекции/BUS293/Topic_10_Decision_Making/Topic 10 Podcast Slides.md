## Slide 3 — Decision Making (definition)

The conscious process of making choices from one or more alternatives with the intention of moving towards some desired state of affairs.

## Slide 4 — Rational Choice Decision-Making Process

- Assumes that people are efficient and logical information-processing machines
- Focuses on logical thinking
- **Limitations:** ignores emotions; people have difficulty recognising problems and failures; affected by **'Bounded Rationality'** — people cannot simultaneously process huge volumes of information, so they process limited and imperfect information and rarely select the best choice.

_Podcast notes:_ The rational choice paradigm suggests effective decision makers use logic and information to choose the best possible alternative. It follows the logical process of identifying problems and opportunities, choosing the best decision style, developing alternative solutions, choosing the best solution, implementing the selected alternative, and evaluating decision outcomes. The model assumes people are efficient/logical information-processing machines, focuses on logical thinking, and ignores emotions. In reality people struggle to recognise problems/failures and can't process huge volumes of information — **bounded rationality**.

> **Six steps of the rational choice process** (from notes): 1) identify problems and opportunities; 2) choose the best decision style/process; 3) develop alternative solutions; 4) choose the best solution; 5) implement the selected alternative; 6) evaluate decision outcomes.

## Slide 5 — Issues in Problem Identification

- **Solution-focused problems:** describing the problem as a solution and failing to diagnose underlying causes
- **Stakeholder framing:** information is highlighted or hidden
- **Decisive leadership:** announcing the problem before logically assessing the situation
- **Perceptual defence:** bad news blocked out / limited options
- **Mental models:** blind us from seeing unique problems and opportunities

**To minimize the issues:** be aware of the problem-identification issues; fight against pressure to look decisive; do not accept complacency; discuss the situation with colleagues to see different perspectives.

_Podcast notes:_ …Maintain **'divine discontent'** (aversion to complacency), and discuss the situation with colleagues to see different perspectives. (Notes refer to "the five problem identification biases.")

## Slide 6 — Choosing the Best Decision Process

Facets:

- It is a **meta-decision**: deciding how to decide
- **Decision maker:** Who decides? Solve the problem alone or involve others; if involving others, to what extent? (use the contingencies of employee involvement to determine this)
- **Type of decision:** Programmed or non-programmed?
  - **Programmed (Routine)** decisions have been resolved in the past, so an optimal solution is already identified and documented
  - **Non-programmed (Non-routine)** decisions need to pass through all steps of the decision-making process as they are new/complex/ill-defined

_Podcast notes:_ Choosing the best decision process is a meta-decision (deciding how to decide) — who makes the final decision, alone or involving others and to what extent, and whether the decision is programmed or non-programmed. For programmed/routine decisions, following every step may not be required; non-programmed need all steps.

## Slide 7 — Employee Involvement

- The degree to which employees influence how their work is organised and carried out (also called **participative management**)
- **Improves:** decision-making quality; commitment to the decision; identification of problems, opportunities, and solutions; evaluation of alternatives
- **Levels/forms:** decide alone → receive information from individuals → consult with individuals → consult with the team → facilitate the team's decision

_Podcast notes:_ Employee involvement improves decision-making quality and commitment, identification of problems/opportunities/solutions, and evaluation of alternatives. Forms range from no involvement (manager decides alone) to the manager just facilitating the team's decision.

## Slide 8 — Employee Involvement: Four Contingencies

| Use low involvement when                                                                                | Contingency             | Use high involvement when                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Problem is common/routine/programmed                                                                    | **Decision structure**  | Problem is new and complex (non-routine/non-programmed)                                       |
| Leader has all relevant knowledge                                                                       | **Knowledge source**    | Employees have relevant knowledge beyond the leader                                           |
| Employees would be committed regardless of the decision                                                 | **Decision commitment** | Employees would lack commitment unless involved                                               |
| Risk of conflict is high – norms do not support organisational goals and employee agreement is unlikely | **Risk of conflict**    | Risk of conflict is low – norms support organisational goals and employee agreement is likely |

_Podcast notes:_ Higher involvement works better when there is low decision structure (new/complex/non-routine problem), relevant knowledge held by employees beyond the leader, commitment needed that would be lacking without involvement, and low risk of conflict (employee norms support organisational goals; agreement likely).

## Slide 9 — Issues in Discovering, Valuing, and Selecting Choices

- **Goal conflict/ambiguity:** goals are often ambiguous or in conflict; ambiguous goals make it difficult to know which alternatives have greater/lesser value (**valence**) to the organisation
- **Information processing problems:** similar to perception issues/biases
- **Implicit favourite:** a preferred alternative used repeatedly as a comparison with other choices, which could undermine effective decision making
- **Biased decision heuristics:**
  - **Anchoring and adjustment:** initial information (e.g. opening bid) influences evaluation of subsequent information
  - **Availability heuristic:** we estimate probabilities by how easily we can recall the event, even though other factors influence ease of recall
  - **Representativeness heuristic:** we estimate probabilities by how similar they are to something else (e.g. stereotypes), even when better probability information is available

_Podcast notes:_ (Restates the above — ambiguous/conflicting goals affecting valence; implicit favourites; the three biased heuristics — anchoring and adjustment, availability, representativeness.)

## Slide 10 — Issues in Discovering, Valuing, and Selecting Choices (continued)

- **Problems with maximisation:**
  - **Satisficing:** choosing a 'good enough' alternative rather than one with the highest value
  - Lack capacity and motivation to process information
- **Problems with valuing choices/opportunities:**
  - Decision makers are less likely to make any decision at all as the number of options increases, or to see that the opportunity is the solution
  - Emotional attachment to the opportunity
  - Emotions form early preferences
  - Moods and emotions influence how well we follow the decision process
  - We 'listen in' on our emotions and use that information when evaluating alternatives

_Podcast notes:_ (Restates — satisficing due to limited capacity/motivation; decision avoidance as options increase; emotional attachment; emotions forming early preferences and influencing how well we follow the process; "listening in" on emotions.)

## Slide 11 — Intuitive Decision Making

- The ability to know when a problem or opportunity exists and select the best course of action **without conscious reasoning**
- Different from emotional decision making — gut feelings are emotional signals, but not all emotional signals are intuition
- Rapid non-conscious analysis — uses **action scripts** based in mental models established through past experience
- Best for **programmed/routine** decisions

**To minimize issues in discovering, valuing, and selecting choices:**

- Systematically evaluate alternatives against relevant factors
- Be aware of effects of emotions on decision preferences and the evaluation process
- Use **scenario planning:** a systematic process of thinking about alternative futures and what the organisation should do to anticipate and react to those environments

_Podcast notes:_ Intuition begins (like emotion) as a gut feeling/emotional signal, but is based in mental models from past experience; this tacit knowledge informs the course of action. Because it relies on mental models it works best for programmed/routine decisions; gut feelings can't always be relied on as the best alternative. Mitigate by systematic evaluation, awareness of emotional effects, and scenario planning.

## Slide 13 — Issues in Decision Effectiveness Evaluation

- **Post-decisional justification:** the tendency to inflate the quality of the selected option and forget/downplay rejected alternatives (**confirmation bias**); caused by the need to maintain a positive self-concept; initially produces an excessively optimistic evaluation of the decision
- **Escalation of commitment:** repeating an apparently bad decision or allocating more resources to a failing course of action. Four main influences:
  - self-justification effect
  - self-enhancement effect
  - prospect theory effect
  - sunk costs effect

**To minimize the issues:**

- Separate decision choosers from evaluators
- Establish a pre-set level to abandon or re-evaluate the project
- Find sources of systematic and clear feedback
- Involve several people in the evaluation process

_Podcast notes:_ Post-decisional justification continues the 'implicit favourite' bias; driven by the need to maintain a positive self-concept; produces optimistic evaluation via confirmation bias, addressable only with clear, irrefutable contrary information. Escalation of commitment = repeating a bad decision / over-allocating to a failing course; four influences (self-justification, self-enhancement, prospect theory, sunk costs). Mitigate as listed.

## Slide 14 — Creativity (Creativity and Decision Making)

- **Creativity** = the development of original ideas that make a socially recognised contribution
- Potential for creativity is based in individual differences (Topic 3)
- To identify creative potential, look for individuals with:
  - **Independent imagination** — high openness to experience; moderately low need for affiliation; strong personal values of self-direction and stimulation
  - **Cognitive and practical intelligence** — ability to synthesise, analyse, and apply ideas; ability to evaluate potential usefulness of ideas
  - **Knowledge and experience** — relevant foundation knowledge and experience; not locked into a fixed mindset
  - **Persistence** — high need for achievement; strong task motivation; moderately high self-esteem and optimism

_Podcast notes:_ Creativity is valuable throughout the decision-making process — imagining opportunities, alternatives, and solutions. Four creative potential clusters: independent imagination, cognitive/practical intelligence, knowledge/experience, persistence. Activities that encourage creativity: **redefining the problem, associative play, cross-pollination, and design thinking.**

## Slide 15 — Creativity (creative process + organisational support)

**The creative process model.** To encourage and support organisational creativity, organisations need:

- Employees with strong creative potential
- Learning orientation and forgiveness for mistakes
- Motivating, enriched jobs
- Open communication and sufficient resources
- A reasonable level of job security
- Support from leaders and co-workers

_Podcast notes:_ The creative process model: develop knowledge about the topic to understand the problem; encourage **divergent thinking** by putting the problem aside (incubation) while returning to it frequently; awareness of sparks of ideas in the **illumination** stage; then flesh out and **test** ideas. Plus hiring/retaining employees with strong creative potential. _(Stages implied: preparation → incubation → illumination → verification.)_

## Slide 16 — Topic 10 Summary

- Decision making is the conscious process of making choices from one or more alternatives with the intention of moving towards some desired state of affairs.
- The rational choice decision-making process helps identify the best choice through a logical process, but has limitations; emotions and intuition also play an important role.
- In the right conditions, employee involvement can contribute greatly to the decision-making process, as well as to the employees and the organisation.
- Creative potential is valuable throughout the decision-making process and can be facilitated through both individual and organisational strategies.

**Rational Choice Assumptions vs Organisational Behaviour Observations:**

| Rational Choice Assumptions                                       | Organisational Behaviour Observations                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Goals are clear, compatible, and agreed on                        | Goals are ambiguous, in conflict, and lack full support             |
| Decision makers can calculate all alternatives and their outcomes | Decision makers have limited information-processing abilities       |
| Decision makers evaluate all alternatives simultaneously          | Decision makers evaluate all alternatives sequentially              |
| Decision makers use absolute standards to evaluate alternatives   | Decision makers evaluate alternatives against an implicit favourite |
| Decision makers use factual information to choose alternatives    | Decision makers process perceptually distorted information          |
| Decision makers choose the alternative with the highest pay-off   | Decision makers choose the alternative that is good enough          |

_Podcast notes:_ Rational choice assumptions are inconsistent with OB observations (see table). Emotions and intuition both assist and disrupt the rational choice process. Employee involvement and creativity (individual + organisational support) improve decision-making capacity. Decision making is a vital management skill and a fundamental activity for all employees; awareness of the conditions for involvement and creativity, and of the limitations of rational choice, is essential for sustainable organisations.
