1.0  PURPOSE.

Success in winning the approval and support of others for your thinking is not
just a product of the ideas themselves.  Of equal importance will be the way you
present your ideas.

Business reports sell your thinking to both managers and academics, so it is in your best interests to

digest  the  contents  of  this  outline  and  embrace  them.    While  the  writing  requirements  may  seem

stringent, it is recommended that you bear in mind that these guidelines will serve you well throughout

life, not just here at Murdoch. Adopt them as your own and you will have taken an important step in

improving not only the "packaging" of your ideas, but the value of the ideas themselves.

Additional  standards,  such  as  the  Chicago  referencing  system,  are  also  included  to  address

fundamental  academic  requirements.    While  referencing  may  seem  a  burden  to  some,  it  should  be

remembered that any business report will require the writer to state the sources of the information in

the  main  body  of  the  report  (or  in  appendices  where  appropriate).    So,  it  should  be  understood  that

we're  not  actually  asking  any  more  of you  than  would  be  the  case  in  the business  world.   Note  that

your report does not require a 'purpose' section. The following content outline will provide current

business students with some precise specifications for their report.

2.0  OUTLINE OF A REPORT FOR BUS293.
The contents of each specific element of a report are provided here in Section 2.0 to assist student in

preparing and structuring their major assessment.

2.1  Cover Page

This should include all standard requirements for a paper, which include;

1.  Unit code and title.

2.  Report title (Topic should be in large font).

3.  Student name and number.

4.  Workshop day and time.

5.  Name of the workshop facilitator (tutor).

It is important to remember that this is a cover page.  So give some thought to its presentation.  If you

cannot  find  a  snappy  graphic  that  is  appropriate,  at  least  ensure  that  some  thought  is  given  to  the

format of these elements.  Clearly the second element is most important and should be formatted with

the largest font, centre aligned.  Other elements are free to be presented in a fashion of your choosing,

but at least make an effort to have a cover that looks professional.

2.2  Report Contents Page

2.2.1  Characteristics

  This should be titled "Contents".

  It  should  have  a  comprehensive  index  showing  the  structure  of  the  report  and  its  various

elements.

  Typically, there are two to four levels of titles in the index to reflect the structure (note there are

three in this document's table of contents).

  It should be the last thing you add to the document before printing.

It will be critical for writers to know the basics of how to build an index using Microsoft (MS) Word,

a function that will enable you to build an index in the blink of an eye.  See Appendix 3.1 for a guide.

2.3  Executive Summary (ES) (Commercial format) – keep to 1 page only

In the commercial world, the ES is the most important page in the entire report.  Put yourself in the

shoes of a Business Analyst or Management consultant for a moment; you have to sell your thinking

in  this  one  page because  many  managers  do  not  have  the  time  to  read  the  entire  report.    It  is  high

impact stuff with every word chosen to ensure the "Big Picture" is summed up in one page.  Typically,

the contents of an executive summary are written last after the whole structure, content and scope of

the report is defined.  However, it does not hurt to have a draft written early as this may help define

goals  for  the  rest  of  the  report  while  it  is  being  constructed.    Furthermore,  as  new  core  ideas  of

significance  emerge,  you  can  amend  the  draft  to  reflect  these  revelations  that  naturally  occur  in  the

construction  stages.    Ultimately,  its  final  form  should  be  decided  on  after  you  are  satisfied  that  all

elements  in  the  discussion  sections  and  end  report  sections  are  finished.    The  following  elements

outline the typical contents of an ES.

Note:  Justified alignment looks best - i.e., even margins on both sides as shown here; however left

alignment will be accepted. What matters is that you are consistent throughout the report.

2.3.1  The Three Main Elements of the Executive Summary.

1.  The  topic  and  its  significance:    This  introduces  the  issue  in  question  and  its  importance  to

management.  Usually topics are linked with the key issues.

2.  The findings (conclusions):  This provides the reader with your high level evaluation on the issue

in question.  In essence, your informed opinion  - always stay  third person (No "I", "my", etc).

Here, you make it clear what the key findings of your analysis are.

3.  The recommendations and their benefits:  This provides the reader with solutions to the issue in

question along with some benefits.

The  entire  executive  summary  should  be no more  than  one  page.    To  cover  the  above  areas  in  one

page  will  be  challenging  for  most  writers  because  writing  a  good  ES  will  provide  a  reader  with  a

concise, high level understanding of all the core issues.  You do not need subsections here.

2.3.2  Benefits of an Executive Summary (ES)

As already stated, managers are very busy.  If you  cannot get the main messages across in a page,

chances are you will lose their attention.  The ES should be able to act as a stand alone, powerful

page that sells the report.  However, there are also other benefits, such as improving reader memory

retention  by providing  a  reader  with  a  broad  understanding of  an  issue  that  creates  a  framework

around which the detailed information can be more readily interpreted.

2.4

Introduction

This section ideally should not go over  one page in length.  There must be four subsections in this

page that cover specific requirements.  Their names and content descriptions are listed below:

1.  Topic:    Here,  you  introduce  the  topic  and  elaborate  on  its  significance  briefly.    Do  not  provide

findings; simply define the key purpose (which is to arrive at the findings).

2.  Limitations:    Here,  you  discuss  the  scope  and  constraints  of  the  report  by  elaborating  on  any

weaknesses or omissions.  This is often due to topic complexity  and problems relating to limited

info provided in the case  (A common problem when examining cases  is that people often distort

information for their own ends).

3.  Sources  of  Data:    State  briefly  what  sources  of  data  have  been  obtained.    Are  they  secondary

only, such as books, journals, papers, magazines and websites, or have you obtained primary data

such as direct interviews (note: primary data is NOT to be collected for this assignment).

4.  Report Organisation:  Here, simply elaborate on the structure of the main body of the report so

the reader knows what to expect and comment on whether you will provide any recommendations

and conclusions.  Do not mention the Intro or ES here.

2.5  Main Body of the Report

This  section  requires  the  writer  to  present  all  the  research  uncovered.    The  characteristics  of  the

discussion section are;

2.5.1  Characteristics

  Third person, factual, objective and impersonal.  You simply present the evidence uncovered

by research and draw attention to information of greatest significance.

  Split  into  numerous,  logical  sections  and  sub/sections  to  help  structure  the  dialogue.  The

sections  of  the  question  and  the  research  may  help  structure  the  discussion  (main  body)  of  the

report.

  The sections and subsections should have titles that reflect the content (as this guide demonstrates)

and  organise  information  logically.  The  key  issues/researched  findings  not  only  inform  your

research  effort  but  also  help  structure  the  report  discussion  itself.    The  precise  format  may

vary from topic to topic as the circumstances vary, but in BUS293 you will nearly always find it

best to have titles in major discussion sections that reflect the key issues in the topic.

  You  do  not  draw  attention  to  the  reader  or  yourself  (keep  your  writing  to  third  person

perspective).  Just present the facts found in your research (using citations/references/sources).

  Define the core theories and concepts of organisational behaviour and how they contribute to the

organisations  current  performance,  sustainability  and  alignment  to  at  least  one  of  the  Ten

Principles  of  the  United  Nations  Global  Impact  (note  ‘Guideline  for  Success’  in  BUS293  Unit

Guide, for expected coverage of sustainability).

  The  main  discussion  section  is  heavily  referenced.    You  are  presenting  the  information

uncovered in your research, so this section must be heavily referenced.

The last point here regarding referencing seems to catch out  many people.  This is not a section to

talk about your opinion; it is a section to present the data found to help make logical conclusions or

support the teams opinion/decisions using credible sources.  Additionally, some discussion of possible

solutions  that  you  may  have  found  ensures  that  when  the  reader  gets  to  the  recommendations  and

conclusions,  your  key  findings  and  solutions  are  more  likely  to  be  seen  as  credible.    A  key  point

though, is to ensure this section is thoroughly researched and has sources cited per the  Chicago

method.

2.5.2  Tips on when to reference

Aside from the referencing standards comprehensively defined in the unit outline, there are some basic

rules that can help you understand when the source needs to be cited.  These include;

  If it is not your idea - cite your source (per the Chicago format).

Note: For a comprehensive list of Chicago referencing examples, see Murdoch Library referencing

guides

  If  it  is  not  clear  by  the  wording of  the  dialogue  that  you  are  still  presenting  facts  from  a  source

cited previously - cite it again.

  Do not leave it till the end of the paragraph to cite a source. It must be cited as soon as the idea is

used (generally this is done at the end of a sentence).

  When using the same reference several times in a row, if it is not apparent to the reader by the tone

and  flow  of  the  discussion  that  you  are  still  talking  about  that  source.,  then  to  reduce  citation

frequency, you can use the authors name in the actual discussion (eg:  Vernon Smith (2002) notes

how ...) early in the first sentence where the ideas are used.

  If you are quoting word for word, put the quote in "inverted commas" and cite a page number if it

is available.  Pagination number will suffice for online databases.

2.6  Recommendations Section

Ideally you should have discussed some potential remedies and solutions in the discussion section and

thoroughly referenced these.  So when you get to this section you must outline the recommendations

you have arrived at as a result of your research.  This can be done in numbered bullet point form, but

you should at the very least briefly  discuss the benefit/s of the recommendations, or a paragraph for

each recommendation, each with a sub-heading.

If you have done your research, you may have many recommendations; the team needs to choose the

most relevant five recommendations. These should be detailed and include the benefits and not simply

state  the  recommendation;  this  will  allow  the  recommendation  to  be  explicitly  linked  to  business

needs,  a  critical  component  of  business  report  writing.  Your  recommendations  must  be  useful  and

practical for managers. Generally you do not need to reference here, as potential solutions should have

already been discussed in the discussion/appendices sections (However, you should note references if

this is not the case). Remember to stay 3rd person.

2.7  Conclusion – one page only
Here  you  provide  your  core  high  level  findings  in  no  more  than  a  page  (Stay  3rd  person).    These

should  draw  together  the  facts  found  in  research  and  your  recommendations  to  demonstrate  a  well

thought out plan of attack.  This should be a powerful section, which should end on a high note or a

note of urgency to generate reader support.  Touching on the benefits of the proposed course of action,

or  the  consequences  of  not  acting  in  accordance  with  recommendations  is  usually  a  good  way  to

accomplish this.  Generally, this section is not heavily referenced as this was done in the discussion

section.  However, if you introduce new information, you must reference it here as well (note: if

something  is  mentioned  in  the  conclusion,  it  should  be  backed  up  with  your  research  and  findings,

hence  any  new  information  in  the  conclusion  is  likely  to  warrant  scrutiny  by  the  reader/marker).

Additionally, if there are critical qualifications to your argument, raise them here to frame your ideas

appropriately.

2.8  Reference List

This  should  contain  an  alphabetical  list  of  all  sources  used  in  the  report.    It  must  comply  with  the

Chicago referencing standards. (see Murdoch Library). https://libguides.murdoch.edu.au/Chicago

2.9  Appendices - optional

▪  Look  at  the  example  appendix  at  the  end  of  the  document  titled,  Building  a  Table  of  Contents.

You will note it is in a section called "Appendices" with each appendix under its own subheading

(With name and number).  You should also remember that  citing the sources of data here is a

must if it is not an analysis or research you have conducted yourself.

▪  Do not simply copy and paste large amounts of discussion into an appendix.  Appendices should

use tables, graphs, figures and interesting formatting to help aid reader comprehension.

Remember to use appendices to keep under the size limit set (up to  3000 words) as this word count

includes all text within the discussion section.
