**SLIDE 4 — Critical Thinking Questions (section)**
McShane and Von Glinow (2024). Chapter 5, page 190 — Questions 3, 5, 7, 8, & 9.

**SLIDE 5 — Chapter 5 Question 3**
Everyone who works as an electronic game developer has extrinsic sources of motivation, and most also experience some degree of intrinsic motivation. Considering the dynamics of extrinsic and intrinsic motivation, what should companies in this industry do to ensure that their game developers are highly motivated at work?

**SLIDE 6 — Ch5 Q3 (1/2)**
Two elements: (a) how to motivate intrinsically and extrinsically; (b) how to incorporate extrinsic motivation that does not undermine intrinsic motivation.
**Intrinsic motivation** occurs when people seek need fulfillment from doing the activity itself — particularly when tasks fulfill their drive for competence and autonomy. In practice, companies need to design jobs where employees can fully apply their skills and experience freedom from external controls (micromanaging bosses, highly formalized rules). Chapter 6 discusses autonomy as a job design strategy. Fulfilling the drive for competence calls for jobs aligned with employees' skills and knowledge, that make full use of those abilities, and possibly stretch capabilities so competencies are further developed.

**SLIDE 7 — Ch5 Q3 (2/2)**
**Extrinsic motivation** occurs when people want to engage in an activity to receive some form of reward (specific financial and general reward strategies — see Chapter 6). It takes three forms:

1. Clearly external sources (pay incentives, recognition rewards)
2. Internal pressure associated with external sources (not letting down the team)
3. Internalized value of external sources (alignment with company values)
   All three can motivate and have value in specific situations. The third option has particular appeal because it feels more internally sourced and is less likely to diminish autonomy — the employee identifies with the company's values and feels motivated more from an internal source (personal values).
   **Effect of extrinsic on intrinsic motivation:** Extrinsic motivators unlikely to adversely affect intrinsic motivation are those that are unexpected (a surprise bonus), have low value relative to the intrinsic motivator, and are not contingent on specific behavior (such as fixed salary). Aim to identify rewards/extrinsic motivation that satisfy some or all of these characteristics.

_Speaker notes (Slide 7):_ Even so, when employees are engaged in intrinsically motivating work, employers should be careful about the potential unintended effect of undermining that motivation with performance bonuses and other sources of extrinsic motivation.

**SLIDE 8 — Chapter 5 Question 5**
The opening case study for this chapter (pg164) describes how many companies have replaced or supplemented traditional annual performance appraisals with frequent, constructive, and future-focused development reviews. Apply expectancy theory to explain why the more frequent feedback and more strengths-based future focus of the new performance review process might motivate employees more than the traditional judgment-oriented, problem-focused, annual performance appraisal process.

**SLIDE 9 — Ch5 Q5 (1/2)**
Several motivational benefits of the emerging developmental performance process vs. traditional appraisal systems. Most obviously, developmental reviews provide more timely feedback, giving employees more accurate and salient information about whether their effort is resulting in desired performance — strengthening (or clarifying) the **E-to-P expectancy** linkage. This is also indirectly explained by OB Mod and social cognitive theory — employees experience more timely, frequent information about the consequences of their behavior and that of coworkers than in traditional reviews. Developmental reviews also help managers better understand what specific employees value (the **valences** of desired outcomes), so managers can guide employees to work activities and career paths with more positively valent outcomes, and in some circumstances choose alternative outcomes (pay, time off, recognition, etc.) for desired behaviors.

**SLIDE 10 — Ch5 Q5 (2/2)**
Developmental reviews enable managers to more quickly and clearly clarify the outcomes that will result from employee behavior and performance. These conversations develop more accurate, salient, clearer **P-to-O expectancies** regarding key performance goals. On **strengths-based feedback/coaching**: by focusing on their strengths, employees have stronger E-to-P expectancies compared to focusing on work activities they perform poorly and have limited interest/capacity to improve. Strengths-based coaching also directs employees toward work that is most rewarding to them — outcomes with higher valence than work less aligned with their self-concept and competencies.

**SLIDE 11 — Chapter 5 Question 7**
Using your knowledge of the characteristics of effective goals, establish two meaningful goals related to your performance in this class.

**SLIDE 12 — Ch5 Q7**
Seven characteristics of effective goals — **SMARTER**:

- **Specific.** Should state what needs to be accomplished, how, and where, when, and with whom.
- **Measurable.** Should state the deadline and the performance level required.
- **Achievable.** Sufficiently but not overly challenging. Goals that are too challenging undermine motivation.
- **Relevant.** Relevant to the job and within the individual's control.
- **Time-framed.** Should specify when the objective should be completed or assessed against a standard.
- **Exciting.** More effective when individuals are committed (not just compliant), where they have been involved in setting them.
- **Reviewed.** Motivational value depends on receiving feedback about reaching goals; effective feedback requires measurement, including reflection and discussion with others.

**SLIDE 13 — Chapter 5 Question 8**
Most people think they are "worth more" than they are paid. Furthermore, most employees seem to feel that they exhibit better leadership skills and interpersonal skills than others. Please comment on this human tendency.

**SLIDE 14 — Ch5 Q8**
When people believe that the "average is above average," this is not rational! When asked if they are "better than average" or below average, most respond better than average — not statistically likely. Studies of the military show 90 percent of US Navy officers think they're in the top 10 percent — statistically improbable. The same holds for leadership and interpersonal skills; try finding anyone who thinks they're below average in interpersonal skills. This nonrational nature is what makes many motivational programs demoralizing and dehumanizing: if I think I'm better than average and you try to "correct" my self-concept, the likely outcome is frustration and disbelief. Extrapolating to pay, **equity theory** is spot on in explaining why we think we're underpaid vis-à-vis our coworkers.

**SLIDE 15 — Chapter 5 Question 9**
You are an external consultant hired by a large organization to investigate possible causes of employee perceptions of procedural and interactional injustice regarding various management decisions (promotions, vacation rostering, assigned tasks, office location, etc.). Many employees complain management is unfair in how it makes these decisions. Even those who get a fair deal agree the process is suspicious and subject to doubt by those who receive less than expected. In a few instances, employees complained about the information (or lack of information) they receive about how the decision was justified, and how they have been treated when trying to discuss it with management. Identify specific activities and issues you would investigate to pinpoint ways management can improve employee perceptions of procedural and interactional justice.

**SLIDE 16 — Ch5 Q9 (1/2): Procedural Justice**

- Decision makers are not biased by self-interest or restrictive doctrines — Managers must not create incidents others may misinterpret as favoritism (e.g., spending more time with one or two employees, seeing some socially after work).
- Allocation decisions are based on a full complement of accurate information — Managers must demonstrate familiarity with all aspects of the decision, including counterarguments.
- Decision makers consider the interests of all groups affected — Managers must listen to all sides and demonstrate attentiveness to everyone who presents complaints, comments, or ideas.
- Decisions and procedures are compatible with ethical principles.
- Decision criteria and procedures are applied consistently across persons and over time — Avoid exceptions to the rules of resource/reward allocation.
- Employees have the opportunity to present their evidence and opinions to decision makers (voice) — Give the under-rewarded employee a chance to speak up and offer reasons.
- Questionable decisions/procedures can be appealed and overturned — Under-rewarded employees should be able to provide arguments to a higher authority (e.g., the boss's boss) if the above don't resolve the matter.

**SLIDE 17 — Ch5 Q9 (2/2): Interactional Justice** _(slide labeled "1/2" in error)_
Interactional justice has four main rules:

- Employees need to be treated with respect.
- Employees need to be treated in a polite manner. ("Treated with respect" means maintaining the individual's sense of self-worth — managers should preserve employees' self-concept if they don't receive expected benefits. E.g., if an employee doesn't win a promotion, the manager might point out the positive features that were considered and ways the person's shortcomings can be developed for a better chance of future progress.)
- Employees should receive well-justified information about decisions affecting them.
- Employees should receive honest, candid, and timely information about decisions affecting them. (Managers should provide sound explanations and communicate them soon after the decision. Consider "well-justified" explanations from the employee's point of view — managers need to understand employee assumptions and expectations regarding promotions and other allocation decisions.)

**SLIDE 18 — Case Study Questions (section)**
McShane and Von Glinow (2024) – Chapter 5, Case Study: 'Cincinnati Super Subs', page 191.

**SLIDE 19 — Case Study Question 1**
What symptom(s) in this case suggest that something has gone wrong?

**SLIDE 20 — Case Study Question 1 (answer)**

- High level of food wastage.
- Morale problems — employees dissatisfied with their benefits (no more free food allowed) and management.
- Poor employee–management relations; valued employees quit.
- Lower productivity due to inexperienced staff hired and staff shortages.
- Very low employee loyalty — employees remain only due to continuance commitment.

**SLIDE 21 — Case Study Question 2**
What are the main causes of these symptoms?

**SLIDE 22 — Case Study Question 2 (1/3): Lack of Motivation to Minimize Food Costs**
Food wastage was high because employees had little motivation to reduce food costs. Positive outcomes from high wastage: they enjoyed eating the free food (+); enjoyed peer support for taking food (+); fulfilled social needs (drive to bond) and possibly need for status (drive to acquire) by giving free ingredients to friends (+); felt inequity in pay relative to the labor market and their previous rewards (better free food allowance), redressing it by taking food (+). Few negative consequences of keeping costs high: the manager's threat of dismissals wasn't effective because some employees didn't value the job (some quit) and others didn't believe (low P-to-O expectancy) the manager could fire them (couldn't fire everyone). Almost no positive outcomes of keeping food costs low — no contingent rewards, no praise (and relations so poor that praise would have low valence). Negative outcomes of keeping costs low — peer pressure and criticism from friends, leaving relatedness needs unfulfilled.

**SLIDE 23 — Case Study Question 2 (2/3): Team Leaders Lacked Motivation (and Power)**
Team leaders had legitimate power from the company, but employees continued to eat/give away food because they had more power over the team leader (ostracizing, school context) than vice versa. (NOTE: refers to concepts in Chapter 10.) Team leaders didn't exercise authority due to risk of ostracism, and employees rejected their legitimate power. They had reward and coercive power (could write up food-wastage reports) but weren't motivated to. Expectancy theory explains this: the night manager received no positive outcomes for writing reports, no bonus, was paid less than minimum wage (likely felt inequitably rewarded, possibly took food), and faced negative outcomes — valued friendships with employees, so relatedness needs would be threatened if reports were written.

**SLIDE 24 — Case Study Question 2 (3/3): Ineffective Use of Punishment**
Illustrates the problems with using punishment to change behavior and its negative consequences. The manager's use of punishment strained relations with employees. Punishment is usually effective only when the source is nearby — as soon as the manager stopped working in the restaurant, employees returned to eating and giving away free food. Punishment also creates disruptions: when employees quit due to the reduced food allowance, managers had to help with food preparation and train new people, disrupting workflow and increasing production costs.

**SLIDE 25 — Case Study Question 3**
What actions should Cincinnati Super Subs' managers take to correct these problems?

**SLIDE 26 — Case Study Question 3 (answer)**

- Most important: link food costs (wastage) to employee rewards to motivate reduction and reduce feelings of inequity. Offer a salary bonus based on achieving monthly food-wastage targets; if precise per-shift data is available, link wastage on each shift to bonuses for that shift's employees (strengthening P-to-O expectancy).
- Bonuses would work best because employees feel underpaid; but the manager might first use token awards (T-shirts, free subs coupons, or challenges with rewards donated to the school or a cause).
- Hard to fully restore employee–manager relations short term, but removing threats and punishment (except for the most serious infractions) would help.
- Let employees create special social events on/off the job (e.g., a "top hat" sub promotion — employees wear top hats, customers get a discount on a premium sub). This improves social bonding and loyalty, and improves employee–management relations if the manager gets involved and is seen as the source of positive events.
- Rethink the team leader's role: peer pressure and lack of legitimate power make reporting food wastage unreasonable. A performance-based reward system minimizes the need for supervision. If a power base is needed, hire people less closely associated with employees, paid more, given a bonus for minimizing wastage, with reward and coercive power.
