## Slide 3 — Leadership (definition)

The ability to influence, motivate, and enable others to contribute towards the effectiveness of the organisations of which they are members.

_Podcast notes:_ Leadership is one of the most researched topics in business. Leadership theories are categorised into four perspectives: transformational, managerial, follower-centric, and competency. The most popular is the transformational perspective.

## Slide 4 — Transformational Leadership

- One of the most popular perspectives and theories of leadership
- Views leaders as change agents
- Differs from charismatic leadership

_Podcast notes:_ Transformational leadership views leaders as change agents who develop, communicate, and model a shared vision for the team/organisation and encourage experimentation. They create and champion an image of the company's projected positive future state, which motivates and bonds employees. The vision is communicated by framing the message around a grand purpose, using symbols, metaphors, and stories to create a shared mental model. Transformational leaders model the vision by "walking the talk," which increases employee trust, and encourage experimentation by questioning current practices and supporting a learning orientation. Commitment is built through communicating/modelling the vision and through employee involvement in shaping it. Some models say charismatic leadership is essential, but research suggests charisma differs from transformational leadership — charisma is a personal trait providing referent power, does not necessarily attempt to change the organisation, and is associated with narcissism.

## Slide 5 — Transformational Leadership (benefits & limitations)

- **Benefits:** higher employee satisfaction; higher performance; increased organisational citizenship; increased creativity.
- **Limitations:** some models have circular logic; does not recognise or account for situational differences, especially across cultures.

_Podcast notes:_ The theory is limited by defining transformational leaders by their success rather than their behaviour, leading to circular logic with no predictive value. It is also a universal theory that doesn't account for situational/cultural differences.

## Slide 6 — Managerial Leadership

- States that effective leaders help employees improve to support current objectives and practices
- Depends on transformational leadership to set the right direction
- Is comprised of many leadership theories

|             | Managerial leadership             | Transformational leadership                         |
| ----------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Assumptions | Objectives are stable and aligned | Current direction is misaligned and needs to change |
| Focus       | Continuous development            | (setting direction)                                 |
| Orientation | More micro-focused and concrete   | More macro-focused and abstract                     |

_Podcast notes:_ Managerial leadership states effective leaders help employees improve performance and wellbeing to support current objectives/practices. It depends on transformational leadership to set direction; the perspectives differ in assumptions of organisational stability and level of focus. Generally, the higher the managerial position, the more transformational behaviours are required, but all managers use varying degrees of both.

## Slide 7 — Leadership Style Orientation

- Categorises leadership style into either task-oriented or people-oriented
- Neither orientation is more effective; each has advantages and disadvantages
- Recent research shows effective leaders rely on both styles, but in different ways

**Task-oriented leaders:** assign work and clarify responsibilities; set goals and deadlines; evaluate and provide feedback on work quality; establish well-defined best work procedures; plan future work activities.

**People-oriented leaders:** show interest in others as people; listen to employees; make the workplace more pleasant; compliment employees for their work; are considerate of employee needs.

_Podcast notes:_ Task-oriented behaviours define and structure work roles (assigning tasks, clarifying duties/procedures, ensuring rules are followed, pushing to reach performance capacity); people-oriented behaviours focus on developing relationships (mutual trust and respect, genuine concern for needs, looking out for welfare). Neither is more effective; effective leaders use both in different ways.

## Slide 8 — Servant Leadership Theory

An extension of the people-oriented style; the view that leaders:

- serve their followers and help employees fulfil their needs
- are coaches, stewards, and facilitators of employee development
- have a natural desire or 'calling' to serve others
- do not view leadership as a position of power
- have decisions/actions bounded by ethical principles and practices
  Servant leadership relies heavily on the principles of authentic leadership. Recent research found an association between higher organisational performance and CEOs demonstrating servant leadership behaviours.

_Podcast notes:_ Servant leadership is the view that leaders serve followers rather than vice-versa — helping employees fulfil needs, acting as coaches/stewards/facilitators, with a 'calling' to serve, not viewing leadership as power, bounded by ethical principles. Relies heavily on authentic leadership; research links it to higher organisational performance when CEOs demonstrate it.

## Slide 9 — Path-Goal Leadership Theory

- A contingency theory, based on **Expectancy Theory**, proposing that effective leadership depends on choosing the most appropriate leadership behaviour for the employee and situation
- The leader's job is to provide followers with the information, support, and/or other resources needed to achieve their goals
- E.g. where employees have high skills/experience, an external locus of control, a non-routine task structure, and low team cohesion → a combination of **participative** and **supportive** leader behaviours is suggested

**Contingency table — suggested leader behaviour:**

|                          | Directive      | Supportive   | Participative  | Achievement-oriented |
| ------------------------ | -------------- | ------------ | -------------- | -------------------- |
| **Skill and experience** | Low            | Low          | High           | High                 |
| **Locus of control**     | External       | External     | Internal       | Internal             |
| **Task structure**       | Non-routine    | Routine      | Non-routine    | ?                    |
| **Team dynamics**        | Negative norms | Low cohesion | Positive norms | ?                    |

(Skill/experience and locus of control = employee contingencies; task structure and team dynamics = environmental contingencies.)

_Podcast notes:_ Path-Goal proposes effective leadership depends on choosing the most appropriate behaviour for the employee and situation, to influence employee expectations about desired results and positive outcomes — by providing information, support, and resources. Contingencies: employee skills/experience and locus of control; task structure and team dynamics.

## Slide 10 — Path-Goal Leadership Theory (benefits & limitations)

- **Benefits:** higher employee motivation and satisfaction; leader acceptance; comparably significant research support.
- **Limitations:** some contingencies have limited research support; some contingencies and leadership styles have not been researched at all; as the theory expands it may become too complicated for practical use.
- Leader Behaviours: (directive, supportive, participative, achievement-oriented).

_Podcast notes:_ Despite comparatively significant research support, a few contingencies have limited support, some have not been investigated at all, and as the theory expands it may become too complex for practical use.

## Slide 11 — Situational Leadership Theory

A contingency theory: effective leaders vary their style with the **ability** and **motivation/commitment** of followers.

**Contingency table — suggested leader behaviour:**

|                         | Telling | Selling | Participating | Delegating |
| ----------------------- | ------- | ------- | ------------- | ---------- |
| **Follower Ability**    | Low     | Low     | High          | High       |
| **Follower Commitment** | Low     | High    | Low           | High       |

- **Benefits:** popular with practitioners; easy to use.
- **Limitations:** only empirical support for the **telling** style (when employees lack motivation and ability — also documented in path-goal theory); the rest of the theory is not supported by research.

_Podcast notes:_ Situational leadership specifies four styles — telling, selling, participating, delegating — varying with follower ability and commitment. Despite popularity, only the 'telling' style (low motivation + ability) has empirical support; the rest is unsupported.

## Slide 12 — Fiedler's Contingency Theory

- A contingency theory: leader effectiveness depends on matching leadership style (people-oriented or task-oriented) to the level of **situational control**
- Examines the situation via three factors of situational control

**Contingency table — suggested leader behaviour:**

| Leader–member relations | Good                                                                                                                     |      |        |      | Poor   |      |        |      |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---- | ------ | ---- | ------ | ---- | ------ | ---- |
| **Task structure**      | High                                                                                                                     |      | Low    |      | High   |      | Low    |      |
| **Position power**      | Strong                                                                                                                   | Weak | Strong | Weak | Strong | Weak | Strong | Weak |
| **Suggested style**     | Task-oriented → … → People-oriented → … → Task-oriented (style varies across the eight situational-control combinations) |      |        |      |        |      |        |      |

- **Benefits:** recognises the importance of leader power; recognises that changing leadership style may be difficult due to the leader's personality and values.
- **Limitations:** lacks research support due to the limited focus on two leadership styles, and flaws in the questionnaire used to measure leadership style.

_Podcast notes:_ Fiedler's model matches style (people- or task-oriented) to the situation via three situational-control factors (leader–member relations, task structure, position power). It recognises the importance of leader power and that style is hard to change (tied to personality/values), but lacks research support due to the two-style focus and flaws in the measurement questionnaire.

## Slide 13 — Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) Theory

- Focuses on the **relationship** between leaders and members, rather than the leader's style
- Views relationships as a series of exchanges, differentiated by quality
- **Low LMX** = economic exchanges (e.g. leaders providing organisational resources in return for task performance)
- **High LMX** = social exchange of resources (e.g. liking, trust, commitment)
- **Benefits:** high LMX leads to increased employee commitment and OCBs, enhanced job performance, and employee retention.
- **Limitations:** potential to alienate some employees; does not consider effects of team dynamics and social identity; does not provide specific advice on how leaders can develop high-quality relationships.

_Podcast notes:_ LMX focuses on leader–member relationships as exchanges that may develop trust and mutual commitment, differentiated by quality (low = economic; high = social). High-quality LMX improves commitment, OCBs, performance, and retention. Limitations: may alienate some employees, ignores team dynamics/social identity, and gives no specific guidance on building high-quality relationships.

## Slide 14 — Leadership Substitutes Theory

- Identifies variables (contingencies/substitutes) that limit a leader's influence or make a particular leadership style unnecessary
- Substitutes include employee ability, intrinsically satisfying tasks, unambiguous tasks, and cohesive work groups
- **Benefits:** explains the effectiveness of self-management and autonomous work groups.
- **Limitations:** empirical evidence is mixed; research suggests substitutes help, but do not completely replace, real leadership.

_Podcast notes:_ Identifies contingencies that limit a leader's ability to influence subordinates or make a style unnecessary (e.g. training and experience can replace task-oriented leadership). Substitutes help but do not completely replace real leadership.

## Slide 15 — Follower-Centric Perspective

- Recognises that leaders don't operate in a vacuum
- Proposes that leaders emerge because of the attributional and cognitive processes of their followers
- Tends to be seen in flat-structure organisations where leaders and followers are treated equally and employees have a 'voice' that greatly impacts decision-making
- **Romance of Leadership Theory:** followers want to believe leaders make a difference, so they engage in **fundamental attribution error** and other perceptual distortions to support this belief; supported by the Western belief that life events are generated more by people than by uncontrollable natural forces.
- **Implicit Leadership Theory:** followers have preconceived beliefs about effective leaders (**leadership prototypes**) used to evaluate effectiveness; followers are more likely to think a leader is effective when they fit the prototype.

_Podcast notes:_ Leaders emerge through followers' attributional/cognitive processes. The romance-of-leadership effect (leaders seen as sole cause of success) stems from wanting to believe leaders matter — fundamental attribution error and perceptual distortions to minimise cognitive processing (topic three), supported by the Western person-centric belief. Implicit leadership theory: people use leadership prototypes to evaluate leaders. "Leadership exists in the mind of the followers."

## Slide 16 — Competency Perspective (table)

- Identifies the characteristics of effective leaders
- An improvement on the 'great man theory' (popular before WWII)
- Early research: few 'traits' predicted effective leadership; recent research: several competencies are key influences on leadership potential and can predict effective leaders

| Competency                           | Description                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Personality                          | Higher extraversion (outgoing, talkative, sociable, assertive) and conscientiousness (careful, dependable, self-disciplined)                        |
| Self-concept                         | Self-beliefs and positive self-evaluation of their leadership skills and ability to achieve objectives                                              |
| Drive                                | Inner motivation to pursue goals                                                                                                                    |
| Integrity                            | Truthfulness and tendency to translate words into deeds                                                                                             |
| Leadership Motivation                | Need for socialised power to accomplish team or organisational goals                                                                                |
| Business Knowledge                   | Tacit and explicit knowledge about the company's environment enabling more intuitive decisions                                                      |
| Cognitive and Practical Intelligence | Above-average cognitive ability to process information and solve real-world problems by adapting to, shaping, or selecting appropriate environments |
| Emotional Intelligence               | Ability to monitor own/others' emotions, discriminate among them, and use the information to guide thoughts and actions                             |

_Podcast notes:_ The competency perspective identifies characteristics of effective leaders — an improvement on the pre-WWII 'great man theory'. Early research found few predictive traits; recent research suggests several competencies (skills, knowledge, values) influence leadership potential and predict effectiveness.

## Slide 17 — Charismatic Leadership Theory

- Using **referent power** gained through personal traits or relational quality to influence followers
- Charisma is both inherent and learned; charismatic leaders tend to be extraverted, self-confident, achievement-oriented
- **Benefits:** charismatic behaviours (confidence, dominance, high self-esteem) appear in many mental models of effective leaders, so via implicit leadership theory followers rate them positively; potential for high performance/achievement.
- **Limitations:** produces dependent followers; may lead to focus on self-interest rather than common good; charismatic traits also associated with **narcissism** (greater need for personal power, lack of empathy, overconfidence, unethical behaviour, inability to accept criticism).

_Podcast notes:_ Charismatic leadership (one of the older theories) can be inborn or learned, achieved through referent power, with potential to raise performance. Implicit leadership theory explains some effectiveness (many hold a 'heroic masculinity' mental model). But it produces dependent rather than empowered followers, and charismatic traits are associated with narcissism and its negative traits.

## Slide 18 — Competency Perspective (benefits & limitations)

- **Benefits:** simple to use, very popular in the past.
- **Limitations:** competencies refer to leadership **potential**, not performance; assumes leadership is within the person, but leadership is also about relations with followers; implies a universal rather than contingency approach; alternative combinations of competencies might work just as well.

_Podcast notes:_ Competencies refer to potential, not performance; the perspective assumes leadership is within the person (though it's also about relations with followers), implies a universal approach, and alternative competency combinations might work equally well.

## Slide 19 — Ethical Leadership

- Vital for organisational conduct and effectiveness
- Determined by individual characteristics and demonstrated through leaders' behaviours
- At its heart: a strong sense of social obligation, awareness of one's impact on others, and concern for followers
- Characterised by **four virtues: prudence, courage, temperance, and justice**
- **Benefits:** highly ethical leaders rated positively; followers have increased satisfaction and OCBs, lower turnover and counterproductive work behaviours; ethical leadership can change norms, increase willingness to speak up about ethical issues, and reduce interpersonal conflicts.

_Podcast notes:_ Ethical leadership sets the ethical climate, conduct, and effectiveness of an organisation; requires social obligation, awareness of impact on others, and concern for followers; characterised by the four virtues. Rated positively by followers; increases satisfaction and OCBs, lowers turnover and counterproductive behaviours; changes norms, increases speaking up about ethical issues, and reduces interpersonal conflict.

## Slide 20 — Authentic Leadership Theory

- The extent to which leaders are aware of, feel comfortable with, and act consistently with their self-concept
- As people learn more about themselves, they gain greater understanding of their inner purpose and behave consistently with their self-concept and value system
- **Benefits:** creates positive effect that heightens teamwork, team productivity, and performance; followers develop strong affective commitment to leaders who are open, candid, and practice what they preach, increasing OCBs.

_Podcast notes:_ Authentic leadership = how well leaders are aware of, comfortable with, and act consistently with their self-concept; greater self-knowledge → understanding of inner purpose and consistent behaviour with self-concept/values.

## Slide 21 — Shared Leadership

- The view that leadership is broadly distributed rather than assigned to one person
- Present in self-managed work teams
- Employees are leaders when they champion change in the company or team
- Encourages self-leadership activities
- Shared leadership calls for: formal leaders willing to delegate power; a collaborative culture (employees support each other); employee ability to influence through persuasion.

_Podcast notes:_ Shared leadership views leadership as a role rather than a formal position, so employees informally act as leaders as occasions arise (e.g. championing change). Requires formal leaders willing to delegate power, a collaborative supportive culture, and employee ability to influence through persuasion.

## Slide 22 — Virtual Leadership

- Necessary due to: globalisation increasing multinational corporations; working across the globe; increasing virtual teamwork; adoption of flexible working arrangements
- Leaders in virtual settings need to: foster team trust and cohesion; provide support; provide access to resources

_Podcast notes:_ Working in virtual settings adds challenges not present face-to-face. Virtual leaders must communicate regularly and provide support and access to resources to build trust and cohesion, preventing feelings of dislocation and disengagement from working in isolation.

## Slide 23 — Diversity and Leadership: Culture

- Societal cultural values and practices: shape leaders' values and norms; influence decisions and actions; shape follower prototype of effective leaders
- Some leadership styles are universal, others differ across cultures: **'charismatic visionary' seems universal**; participative leadership works better in some cultures than others
- For effective global leadership: lead with a culturally compatible or universally endorsed style; match or exceed culture-specific expectations of leaders

_Podcast notes:_ Societal cultural values shape leaders' values/norms, decisions/actions, and follower prototypes. Some styles are universal ('charismatic visionary'), others vary (participative). For effective global leadership, use a culturally compatible and/or universally endorsed style and match or exceed culture-specific expectations.

## Slide 24 — Diversity and Leadership: Gender

- Male and female leaders have similar task- and people-oriented leadership, despite stereotypes that women are more people-oriented
- Must be sensitive to follower expectations — the mental model of **'heroic masculinity'** is still widely prevalent, although female executives score higher on effectiveness and satisfaction in their leadership approach
- Participative and transformational leadership are used more often by female leaders
- Evaluating female leaders: still receive negative evaluations due to prototypes and gender stereotypes, but evidence they are good at emerging-leadership styles

_Podcast notes:_ Male and female leaders have similar task/people orientation; due to implicit leadership theory they must be sensitive to follower expectations. Female leaders use the participative style more often, still receive negative evaluations due to prototypes/stereotypes, but research shows they are good at emerging-leadership styles.

## Slide 25 — Topic 9 Summary

- Leadership is the ability to influence, motivate and enable others to contribute towards the effectiveness and success of the organisations of which they are members.
- Leadership perspectives include:
  - **transformational** — looks at leadership on a macro-scale, visioning for the future
  - **managerial** — focuses on day-to-day requirements of a leader
  - **follower-centric** — recognises that leaders do not operate in a vacuum and that followers' beliefs and expectations impact whether a leader is seen as effective
  - **competency** — acknowledges that there are some key predictors (competencies) of effective leaders
- Leadership theories try to predict what determines effective leadership, based on situation, people, or other factors. Each has benefits and limitations.
- Both culture and gender affect leadership effectiveness, however this is mostly based in leader prototypes, not actual ability to lead or perform leadership behaviours.

_Podcast notes:_ Summary of the four perspectives and their relationships; culture and gender (plus other contingency factors) affect perceived effectiveness. Important to reflect on the context of leaders — situation, team contingencies, work contingencies, relationship with followers — when judging effectiveness.

## Key terms / frameworks

- **Definition of leadership**; **four perspectives:** transformational, managerial, follower-centric, competency (+ emerging).
- **Transformational leadership** (change agents, vision; vs charismatic).
- **Managerial leadership**; **leadership style orientation** (task- vs people-oriented).
- **Servant leadership**; **authentic leadership**; **ethical leadership** (four virtues: prudence, courage, temperance, justice).
- **Contingency theories:** Path-Goal (based on Expectancy Theory; directive/supportive/participative/achievement-oriented), Situational (telling/selling/participating/delegating), Fiedler (leader–member relations, task structure, position power), Leadership Substitutes.
- **LMX Theory** (low = economic exchange, high = social exchange).
- **Follower-centric:** Romance of Leadership (fundamental attribution error), Implicit Leadership Theory (leadership prototypes).
- **Competency perspective** (8 competencies); **great man theory**; **charismatic leadership** (referent power, narcissism risk).
- **Emerging:** shared leadership, virtual leadership.
- **Diversity:** culture (charismatic visionary = universal; participative varies) and gender (heroic masculinity prototype; emerging-leadership styles).
