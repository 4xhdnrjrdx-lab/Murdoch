Site audited: The Coffee Place (hugvsc.wixsite.com/bus279website)  ·  built on Wix
This sprint is about telling the signal from the noise on a real site. Most of my thinking comes back to one balance from class: a customer pays a tax (time, effort, a bit of risk) to get the thing they want, and the site’s job is to keep the friction low enough that they’re still willing to pay it. The Coffee Place does some things well, but there are also several issues that hurt the buying journey, including one that breaks the whole funnel.

Task 1: The Conversion Elements
Before judging anyone’s site, here’s why each of these elements earns its place. The way I think about it: every one of them is a “scratch” for a specific “itch” a shopper feels, usually some version of “can I trust this?”, “is this worth it?” or “is this going to be a hassle?”
Hero Section. The first thing the eye lands on, and you get about a second to land the message. It has to say what you sell, who it’s for, and what to do next. Get it wrong and people bounce before scrolling.
Stylised Photos. People can’t touch the product online, so the photo does the selling. A clean, well-lit, lifestyle shot makes them picture owning it and signals the brand is legit, not a dropshipping front.
Guides to an App. Pushing the app matters because app users come back more and buy more (browsing in bed, on the train), so it lifts repeat purchase and lets the brand re-engage through notifications.
Peace of Mind. This is pure friction-removal. Returns, warranty, secure-payment badges all take away the fear of “what if it doesn’t arrive / doesn’t work,” which is often the real thing stopping the click.
Free Shipping. A huge deciding factor, and partly psychological. People would rather pay $99 than $89 plus $10 shipping, because a separate fee feels like a penalty. Folding it in removes a sting at the worst possible moment.
Personalisation. Recommendations and “picked for you” make the shopper feel seen and humanise the brand, which lifts engagement and loyalty. It’s the difference between a shop that knows you and one that shouts at everyone.
Descriptions. They answer the quiet questions (material, size, how it works) so doubt doesn’t stall the sale. Keep the key selling points short and put the deep specs in a dropdown for the people who want them.
Reviews. Social proof is one of the strongest trust signals there is: other people already took the risk and it worked out. Worth remembering that when people don’t leave reviews the negatives don’t vanish, they just quietly leave, so it’s worth chasing reviews even the critical ones.
Showing Quantity Sold. “4.9K+ sold” or “almost sold out” does two jobs: social proof (others bought and were fine) and FOMO (don’t miss out), which nudges the impulse buy.
Showing Reference Price. People are genuinely bad at knowing what something should cost, so a crossed-out “was” price anchors the value and makes the real price feel like a win.
Call to Action (CTA). The button that tells people exactly what to do next. It needs to be obvious, action-worded and pointed at the goal. A vague or wrongly-aimed CTA leaks customers at the exact moment they were ready.
Psychological Pricing. Small tricks that change perception: charm pricing ($X.99 feels cheaper), or an oddly precise number which can read as more honest, or keeping a price under a round threshold. Same product, better-feeling price.
Pay It Off Options. Splitting the cost (“pay $7.28 today”) shrinks the perceived size of the purchase and lowers the mental barrier on bigger-ticket items, so more people clear it.
Variety. Offering colours, sizes or styles widens the net, so more people find the one that fits them and fewer leave because “close but not quite.”
Fast Shipping. Speed is a major factor, and just as importantly it’s about dispelling a fear. If your category has a reputation for slow delivery, saying “arrives in 4 days” head-on removes a known objection.
One I’d add: a sticky / persistent cart. Keeping the cart visible and saved across pages (and devices) lowers friction and makes it easy to come back and finish, which directly fights cart abandonment.

Task 2: The UI Audit (The Coffee Place)
I went through the live site (Home, Shop, a product page, cart and checkout). For each of the ten items: whether it’s actually needed here, what works, what’s noise, and what I’d change and why. Quick framing first: this is a coffee brand that seems torn between being a café (reservations, experiences) and a shop (beans and gear), and that split is the root of most of the UI problems below.
Hero Section
Needed? Yes. It’s the first impression and it has to set direction.
Like: “Brewed Happiness” is a clean, confident headline, the cappuccino photo is genuinely appealing, and the layout (text left, image right) reads clearly.
Noise: The hero sells a feeling but not a direction. The big CTA is “Book a Table,” which sends people to a reservation form, not the shop, even though the brand clearly sells beans and equipment.
Change & why: Make the primary hero CTA “Shop Coffee” (or “Shop Now”) and demote “Book a Table” to a secondary link. The hero should point at the main money-making path; right now it actively steers away from it.
Call to Action (CTA)
Needed? Yes, and it’s the single most important fix on the site.
Like: On the product page the “Add to Cart” button is full-width and obvious, which is right.
Noise: The CTAs are fighting each other. Hero says “Book a Table,” the header says “Order Now,” the product says “Add to Cart.” Three different primary actions and no clear hierarchy of what the site wants you to do.
Change & why: Pick one primary action (selling coffee) and make every page ladder up to it. “Order Now” and “Book a Table” shouldn’t both look like the main event. Unclear CTAs can make customers leave before buying.
Variety
Needed? Yes, but in proportion to a 9-product catalogue.
Like: The product page does this well: a “Grind Setting” choice (Fine / Medium / Coarse) and a quantity stepper give real, useful options.
Noise: The Shop filter panel is huge (colour, roast level, grind, glaze finish, steel finish, handle material, flavour profile…) for only nine products. That’s enterprise-store filtering on a tiny catalogue, which is overkill and a bit silly.
Change & why: Cut the filters down to the two or three that matter (category, maybe roast level and price) and drop the rest. Filters should save effort; here they add visual noise and imply a range that isn’t there.
Reviews / Social Proof
Needed? Yes. For an unknown brand this is one of the most important trust signals, and it’s missing.
Like: Nothing to like here, since there are no reviews, ratings or “sold” counts anywhere on the products.
Noise: The closest thing is a “Follow Our Journey” section and social icons, which is brand fluff, not proof that the coffee is good.
Change & why: Add a simple star rating and a few written reviews to each product page. With no brand recognition, a stranger has nothing to lean on; reviews let earlier buyers do the convincing.
Psychological Pricing
Needed? Worth using, though honestly it’s a smaller fix than the others.
Like: Prices are at least clean and easy to read ($45.00, $18.00).
Noise: Every price is a round number with no anchoring. There’s a “Buy One Get One Free” banner shouting a deal, but the product pages show a single plain price with no “was” price to make it feel like value.
Change & why: Test charm pricing ($17.99 instead of $18.00) and, where there’s a genuine discount, show a crossed-out reference price. People don’t know what a bag of beans ‘should’ cost, so give them an anchor.
Peace of Mind
Needed? Very. This is where trust is won or lost.
Like: There’s a “Secure Checkout” padlock label in the cart, which is the right idea.
Noise: It’s almost empty otherwise: no returns policy, no freshness or satisfaction guarantee, no shipping promise. And the “secure checkout” reassurance sits on top of a checkout that can’t actually take payment, which undercuts the whole signal.
Change & why: Add a short trust strip to the product page (e.g. freshly roasted guarantee, easy returns, secure payment) and, obviously, make the payment actually work. A safety promise you can’t keep is worse than none.
Shipping Information
Needed? Yes, it’s a top deciding factor and it’s buried.
Like: Delivery does show as “FREE” in the cart, which is a strong positive if it’s real.
Noise: There’s nothing on the product page, and the cart auto-detected the country as “Azerbaijan,” which instantly breaks trust for a local customer and makes the whole thing feel untested. No times, no methods, no conditions.
Change & why: Surface “Free shipping” as a selling point up on the product page, fix the location default, and add a delivery estimate. Free shipping is too good a lever to leave hidden two steps deep.
Attention Hierarchy
Needed? Yes, this is the ‘senses’ of the whole page.
Like: On the hero the order of attention is good: big headline, then photo, then button. The product page also focuses cleanly on name, price and “Add to Cart.”
Noise: The scrolling marquee at the very top (“Buy One Get One Free … Join Our Coffee Club … Freshly Roasted Daily” on a loop) pulls the eye upward and competes with the hero. Constantly moving text is the kind of thing people learn to tune out in seconds.
Change & why: Drop the looping marquee or replace it with one static line. The most important offer shouldn’t be moving, and it shouldn’t be fighting the hero for first place.
Product Description
Needed? Yes.
Like: The descriptions that exist are decent: the grinder copy covers the ceramic burrs, the adjustable grind and the steel body in a tight couple of sentences.
Noise: There’s only one photo per product (no angles), no specs dropdown for people who want detail, and the Shop grid has no description at all, just a name and price.
Change & why: Keep the short description but add a “details / specs” dropdown and a second photo or two. Give the quick-skimmers the summary and the careful researchers the depth, without cluttering the page.
Navigation
Needed? Yes.
Like: The Shop has a sensible category sidebar, breadcrumbs (“Home › All Products”), and the mobile menu collapses into a clean hamburger with the cart still visible.
Noise: The top menu has seven items (Home, Blog, Reservations, Experiences, Shop, Notifications, Menus) and “Shop” is buried in the middle of them. For a site that should be selling coffee, the shop isn’t the obvious destination.
Change & why: Trim the menu and pull “Shop” to a prominent spot (and consider whether “Notifications” and “Menus” earn a top-level slot). Navigation should make the main path the easy path.

Task 3: UX Evaluation (The Internal Logic)
UX is the overall feeling of using the site, and it shows up directly in the metrics: a frustrating experience pushes the bounce rate up and conversions down. Here’s how I’d optimise each piece, with The Coffee Place in mind since it has real issues on a couple of these.
Loading Times. Speed is the ‘senses’ of the UI because a slow page feels broken before the user has even judged the content, and every second of delay is friction (cortisol) that sends people back to the search results. On this site the cart and especially the checkout hang for several seconds on grey skeleton placeholders, which is exactly where you least want delay, right at the moment of buying. I’d optimise by compressing and lazy-loading images, cutting unnecessary scripts, and making sure the checkout in particular loads fast, because a slow checkout is a direct cause of cart abandonment.
Easy Checkout Flow. A near ‘1-click’ process works because every extra field and step is another chance to give up. Saved details, guest checkout (no forced account), and a one-page payment all strip out friction at the highest-intent moment. The Coffee Place is the cautionary tale here: the checkout doesn’t collect anything and dead-ends on “We can’t accept online payments,” so the friction is effectively infinite. Fixing the flow starts with simply making it functional, then making it short.
Mobile Responsiveness. The site has to be perfect on a phone because that’s where a lot of casual browsing happens (in bed, on the train), and a layout that breaks on mobile loses those people instantly. Credit where it’s due: this site is actually responsive, the hero stacks vertically and the menu collapses to a hamburger with the cart still visible. So here it’s about protecting what works: keep buttons thumb-sized, keep the “Add to Cart” reachable, and make sure the (fixed) checkout is just as smooth on mobile.
Navigation Logic. A clear path prevents frustration because a confused user is a user who leaves; if people can’t find the shop or don’t know where they are, they don’t hunt, they bounce. The fix for this site is to make “Shop” the obvious main route rather than one of seven equal menu items, keep the breadcrumbs, and make sure every page has a clear next step toward buying. Good navigation is really just lowering the effort it takes to get from ‘interested’ to ‘checked out’.

Task 4: Behavioural Barriers (Tax vs Friction)
Quick definitions in my own words. The tax is what I’m willing to spend to get the thing I want: the money, sure, but also the time, the effort, and the bit of risk I accept. Friction is the irritation that stops me: when a site is slow, confusing or makes me nervous, the friction climbs higher than the tax I was willing to pay, and I leave. The goal is to make the buying process feel acceptable instead of annoying.
Below I’ve worked through this with my own real experience: one site where I went ahead with the purchase, and one where I gave up.
A site where I completed the purchase: Amazon. The tax I paid was the time spent comparing a few products, checking reviews, and choosing delivery. I was willing to pay this tax because the product information was clear, the reviews made the risk feel lower, and the delivery date was shown before checkout.
A site where I abandoned the purchase: AliExpress. The friction was too high because the delivery time was unclear, there were too many similar sellers, and I was not fully sure about the product quality. Even though the price was low, the risk and confusion made me leave.
How they could downgrade friction to tax. They could have reduced the friction by showing a clearer delivery estimate, highlighting trusted sellers, and making the return policy easier to understand. I would still have to spend time choosing the product, but the risk would feel more acceptable.

Task 5: The Optimisation Loop (A/B Testing)
A/B testing is useful because it lets marketers check what actually works instead of only guessing. In practice, it means showing version A to half the visitors and version B to the other half at the same time, changing just one thing, and keeping whichever version wins on a chosen metric. The discipline is to change one variable at a time so you actually know what caused the difference, and to treat your first idea as a hypothesis, not a fact. Below are five things I’d test on The Coffee Place, each tied to a real problem from the audit.
How I’d actually run it: split traffic 50/50, run each test until there’s a clear and stable winner rather than calling it after a day or two, and only change one element per test so the result is trustworthy. Then the winner becomes the new ‘A’ and I test the next idea against it. That’s the loop: small change, measure, keep what works, repeat. Realistically the hero CTA (test 1) and the broken checkout are the biggest levers here, so that’s where I’d start.
