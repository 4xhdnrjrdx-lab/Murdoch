# BUS279 Sprint 3: The City Grind Strategic Overhaul

Faiaz Dzhafarov (35553299) · Digital and Social Media Marketing

This sprint is my strategic plan for The City Grind, the CBD cafe from the brief that spent fifteen years selling standard coffee on a good corner and is now watching revenue slide. For most of its life the shop never had to market itself. The location did the work: people walked past, people bought coffee. That era is over. Boutique competitors opened nearby, and the thing customers are buying has changed. It is not really caffeine anymore, it is novelty and something you can share.

Working through the five datasets I kept landing on the same two problems, and they happen to match the expert hint. One, the menu does not scratch the itch people actually have now. Two, the shop has fallen out of people's heads. The location used to get past the modern worker's RAS for free, and now a regular literally forgets the place exists. Everything in my plan is aimed at those two cracks, plus the digital and operational friction sitting on top of them. The end goal is to turn a passive corner shop into something closer to a strategic fortress, a business that holds its customers instead of borrowing them from foot traffic.

## What the data says

The POS dump is the clearest dataset. Regular coffee is collapsing while the specialty drinks grow, even though they cost more:

| Product | Jan '26 | Apr '26 (MTD) | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat White (Regular, $5.50) | 1,450 | 480 | falling fast |
| Cappuccino (Regular, $5.50) | 1,200 | 410 | falling fast |
| Oat Milk Latte ($6.80) | 120 | 145 | rising |
| Cold Brew ($8.00) | 45 | 92 | rising |
| Nitro Cold Brew ($9.50) | 0 | 38 | new and rising |

So people have not stopped buying coffee. They are buying different coffee, mostly at higher prices, just not from us. I read that as a variety problem, not a demand problem.

The socials tell the same story from the other side. The generic stuff dies: a stock photo of a coffee bean got 12 likes, "We are open" got 8, "Monday Motivation" got 6. Meanwhile Dave doing latte art got 185, Dave laughing with a regular got 94, and the dog sitting outside got 210 likes and 65 shares. The pattern is pretty obvious, people, craft and the dog work, and nobody is acting on it because the account is run by a casual staff member with no plan.

Reviews and the staff logs fill in the rest. No app "in 2026". A website that "looks like it was made in the 1900s". A customer who thought the shop was a dry cleaner. Three professionals walking out because there is no caramel macchiato. An office manager asking about catering packs, hearing no, and never coming back. Every one of those is money walking out the door.

## The Strategic Overhaul

## 1. Website & Digital Menu Design

What to do: Rebuild the website as a simple mobile-first storefront, on something like Wix or Squarespace to keep the cost down. One strong photo of the shop and the team, a live menu with real prices, a few reviews, and one obvious button: see menu, order ahead.

- Criticism of the current offering: The current site was last touched in 2017 by a staff member, and a reviewer called it "a picture of the front door" that was a "waste of time". One customer tried to check the menu online before walking over and gave up. Worse, the socials link to this site, so every post currently sends people to a dead end.

- How my plan improves it: The new site answers "what do they sell and how much" before someone commits to walking in, and it becomes the hub everything else plugs into: ordering, loyalty sign-up, the link in the bio. It also just makes the shop look alive, which is half the trust battle.

## 2. Mobile Ordering Workflow

What to do: A basic skip-the-queue flow: open the menu, pick the drink, pay, get a pickup time, collect from a shelf. I would not build a custom app for this budget, a white-label ordering platform does the job.

- Criticism of the current offering: There is no pre-order at all. One review says it plainly: "why is there no way to order on an app, it's 2026". The Tech Worker interview is more brutal, if he cannot order from his desk and pick up in 30 seconds he is not coming. And the staff log already shows a customer skipping our queue and ending up across the road.

- How my plan improves it: People are clearly fine paying the tax, the price. What kills them is the friction, the queue. Order-ahead removes the queue for exactly the office crowd that surrounds the shop, and it turns the walk-past location back into an advantage: the coffee is ready as you pass.

## 3. CRM & Digital Loyalty Framework

What to do: Capture the customer's name, an email or phone number as the identifier, and their usual order, through a loyalty program built into the ordering flow (sixth coffee free, save your usual). Run it on a light CRM like HubSpot. What I want stored is simple: who they are, what they drink, how often they come.

- Criticism of the current offering: The brief says it straight, the company has never bothered with data and holds none. Meanwhile Sarah counted four customers standing in our own queue holding the competitor's loyalty card. Nothing holds anyone to The City Grind except habit, and habit is exactly what is breaking.

- How my plan improves it: Once we know who people are and what they drink we can do actual retention: a message in the afternoon slump, "your usual is one tap away", a free drink at the right moment. It creates a small switching cost and starts building the fortress the shop never had. It also gives us numbers to check whether any of this plan is actually working, which matters to me because right now the shop is flying blind.

## 4. Human-Centric Content Strategy

What to do: Stop posting randomly and run a simple calendar around the three things the data already proved: Dave and the team, the craft, and the dog. Two or three posts a week is enough if they are the right posts. Order link in the bio.

- Criticism of the current offering: The account is run by a casual with "no clear Strategic Logic", the brief's own words, and the numbers show it. Generic posts get single digits while Dave and the dog pull between 94 and 210 likes. They are sitting on a goldmine and treating it like a chore.

- How my plan improves it: Content that shows real people builds the trust, the oxytocin, that the counter cannot build in advance, and it keeps the shop in people's heads between visits. One thing I would be careful about: every post needs the shop's name and look on it, so people remember the cafe and not just the dog.

## 5. Aesthetic Product Upgrades

What to do: Set a few basic "worth photographing" standards: latte art as the default on milk drinks, a signature glass for the cold range, and one or two drinks deliberately designed to look good on a phone.

- Criticism of the current offering: "Coffee is okay, but it's just... coffee." The Trend Seeker says the place is not Instagrammable, and a reviewer is openly tempted by the competitor's lavender lattes because they look great on Insta. The product gives people nothing to share and nothing that feels like a reward.

- How my plan improves it: If the new itch is social standing and experience, the drink itself has to be the scratch. A photogenic drink also turns customers into free content for point 4, at basically no extra cost.

## 6. High-Margin Menu Expansion

What to do: Formally add what the data is already asking for: lock in cold brew and nitro, add a small flavoured latte line (yes, including a caramel macchiato), proper alt-milk options, one protein or superfood item, and swap the tired muffins for something like avo toast or banana bread.

- Criticism of the current offering: The menu is stuck on regular while regular collapses in the POS. The staff logs read like a list of lost sales: the macchiato group walking out, the "health place" guy, people getting irritated about avo toast. These are customers standing inside the shop trying to give it money and failing.

- How my plan improves it: Specialty sells at $6.80 to $9.50 against $5.00 to $5.50 for regulars, so the new items are higher margin as well as on trend. I would keep the list short though. The goal is to cover the requests that keep repeating, not to build a forty-item menu that slows the line down even more.

## 7. The "Dog" Marketing Trigger

What to do: Make the dog official. Give him a name, a schedule ("dog days"), a small sign at the door, and a regular slot in the content calendar.

- Criticism of the current offering: The dog post is the best performing thing the shop has ever published, 210 likes and 65 shares, and it happened by accident. One person even came in because of the dog, found no dog, and left a "0/10" review. A regular also admitted they forgot the shop existed until they saw the dog. So the single best marketing asset is unmanaged and sometimes actively backfiring.

- How my plan improves it: A dog is a natural pattern interrupt. It stops the scroll and gets past the RAS in a way a stock photo never will, and it carries real emotional pull. A schedule turns a lucky spike into a repeatable hook, and managing the expectation, posting the dog only when the dog is there, fixes the 0/10 problem.

## 8. Physical Signage & Identity Refresh

What to do: Refresh the exterior: a clear name, a bold coffee-first sign, and the same colours and logo as the new website so the physical and digital brand finally match. The storefront should be treated as a marketing asset, because it will keep showing up in photos and posts.

- Criticism of the current offering: The Busy Exec walked past every day for a year thinking the shop was a dry cleaner. Someone on the phone could not name the shop while physically standing at it. Findability is broken in the most literal way possible.

- How my plan improves it: Good signage is the offline version of getting past the RAS. A shop people can name is a shop people can recommend, and a decent-looking exterior doubles as a content backdrop, so every photo taken out front quietly works as marketing.

## 9. Operational Capacity Upgrade

What to do: A quick audit of the serving process, then two targeted fixes: a second steam wand or a dedicated alt-milk station, and a separate pickup shelf for mobile orders so pre-orders stay out of the main line.

- Criticism of the current offering: The oat latte "took forever" because there is one small steam wand and everyone was getting annoyed. Another reviewer paid $6.80 and still waited ten minutes, "the friction is real". One customer with a meeting just bailed and bought from the competitor. The shop is selling more and more specialty drinks on equipment set up for regular ones.

- How my plan improves it: This is where the halo and horns effect bites. A slow, annoying serve drags the whole brand down no matter how good the coffee is, and a fast smooth one makes everything feel more premium. Fixing capacity also protects point 6, there is no point adding drinks the bar cannot make quickly.

## 10. B2B Corporate Catering Strategy

What to do: A catering page on the new site plus direct outreach, plain email and LinkedIn, to office managers in the surrounding towers. The offer: morning packs with the new variety, ordered ahead, collected or delivered.

- Criticism of the current offering: An actual office manager asked for catering packs, was told no, and never came back. That was a warm, high-value lead from a building full of potential repeat customers, and the shop had nothing to offer them.

- How my plan improves it: Catering is repeat revenue at real volume, and it finally uses the location on purpose instead of passively. Once the CRM is running, the outreach can even say "we already serve thirty people in your building", which lands far better with a business buyer than any cold ad. I have kept this high level as the brief asks: the channel is the site plus direct outreach, the proof is our own customer data.

## Timeline

This is a realistic order of work rather than an exact schedule. Cheap, visible wins go first, the bigger digital build follows once there is something worth pointing it at.

| Phase | Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Quick wins | 1-4 | Exterior signage refresh, the dog becomes a scheduled feature, content calendar starts (Dave, craft, dog), the specialty drinks that already sell get formally added, second steam wand installed. |
| 2. The digital build | 5-8 | New mobile-first website with the live menu, skip-the-queue ordering and pay-ahead, CRM and loyalty sign-up go live. |
| 3. Scale and adjust | 9-12 | B2B catering page and outreach to the towers, product photo standards written down, first proper look at the data to see what is working and move spend accordingly. |

## Budget ($20,000)

The brief treats the $20,000 as a limiter, so I am splitting it by area instead of line items. Roughly $8,000 goes to the digital build (website, mobile ordering, CRM setup), $4,000 to brand identity and exterior signage, $4,000 to menu and equipment including the second steam wand and the signature glassware, $3,000 to content, one proper photo shoot and a small starter ad budget, and about $1,000 held back for the B2B push and surprises. The logic of the split: the digital storefront, ordering and CRM are the fortress, so they get the biggest share, while the awareness fixes stay cheap on purpose because the data shows they work for almost nothing.

## How I would know it is working

I would keep the measurement simple and check it monthly. Specialty share of POS sales, it is roughly a fifth of volume now and I would want it past a third. Mobile orders as a share of all orders once ordering is live. Loyalty sign-ups, and how many of them come back within two weeks. On socials the number I would actually watch is link clicks to the menu and order page, likes are nice but clicks are intent. And one operational number: the length of the 8am queue. When we change something big, the menu or the signage, I would try to change one thing at a time where possible, the same logic as the ad testing in Sprint 2, otherwise we will not know which fix did the work.

## The 5-Minute Pitch

The City Grind in one line: a fifteen-year-old cafe that survived on location and a standard cup, and both of those stopped being enough.

When I went through the five datasets, two cracks kept showing up. First, variety. The customer's itch has moved from caffeine to novelty and something worth sharing, and a plain flat white does not scratch that anymore. The proof is in the POS: regular coffee is collapsing while oat lattes, cold brew and nitro climb, and the staff logs are full of people walking out over drinks we do not make. Second, awareness. The location used to do the marketing for free and it does not now. A regular forgot we existed, a customer thought we were a dry cleaner, and four people stood in our queue holding a competitor's loyalty card.

The plan is to turn a passive corner shop into a fortress, and four moves do most of the work.

One, fix the digital front door. The website is from 2017 and reads as "waste of time", so we build a modern mobile-first site with a live menu and, most importantly, skip-the-queue mobile ordering. People are happy to pay, they just will not wait.

Two, build the awareness engine for almost nothing. The data already told us what works: Dave, the craft, and the dog. The dog post outperformed everything the shop has ever published. So we run a consistent calendar around people and that dog, and use him as a deliberate pattern interrupt.

Three, fix the product. We formally add the specialty range that is already selling and make the drinks worth photographing, because the reward people are buying now is one they can share.

Four, build the moat. A simple loyalty and CRM setup captures names and usual orders, which gives us a switching cost and lets us nudge people right when the afternoon slump hits.

All of it fits in the $20,000, cheap awareness wins first, the digital build right behind, and a B2B catering line later to finally use the office towers next door. The whole plan comes back to two things: give people a scratch that matches today's itch, and make sure they remember we exist.
