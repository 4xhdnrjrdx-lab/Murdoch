BUS279 Digital and Social Media Marketing
Sprint 1b AuraFlow / Quasar Rush Launch
Client: AuraFlow Energy  ·  Product: Quasar Rush (250ml Slimline)
Here is my plan for the AuraFlow launch. I have worked through the four tasks first and then the CRM database in Phase 2 at the end. The main point I kept coming back to is this: getting someone onto the site is only the first step.A good ad or a 20% offer gives them the little dopamine hit that earns a click. The hard part is getting them to trust us enough to actually buy, and then to stay. Friction is the enemy at every one of those steps, and the brief’s own number (95% walk away when you make it harder than it needs to be) is the reason most of my decisions below come back to removing it.

Task 1 The Value Exchange (Capture)
For me, the issue is not just getting a signup. If someone visits the site and leaves anonymously, the brand loses the chance to follow up with them later. I can’t put them in the CRM, can’t attribute the sale, can’t email them later. The email address is the key that unlocks all of that. So what I’m really designing is enough trust for someone to hand over that key while they’re still interested.
The Component (what I’m offering)
At first I thought a discount would be the easiest option, but I do not think it should be the whole offer. A “20% off” magnet pulls in bargain hunters, and bargain hunters leave the moment something cheaper turns up. That’s the exact “buy whatever’s in the fridge” habit we’re trying to break, so I’d build the offer around information and use the discount as the hook underneath it.
The main reward is a short guide, “The Afternoon Slump Protocol” one page on beating the 3pm crash, built around our actual formula (L-Theanine plus natural caffeine, and why zero sugar avoids the glucose dip). This does the positioning work for us before they’ve even tasted the drink: it frames Quasar Rush as a performance tool, not just another can.
Inside the guide sits the physical reward: free shipping on the trial 4-pack (or 20% off the first order). It feels like the natural next step rather than a hard sell.
And there’s a small experiential layer: early access to the “Focus Lab” content, which I lean on again later for loyalty in Task 4.
Stacking the three means the trade feels worth it for an email address, and the guide carries the brand message a bare coupon never could.
The Path (how I frame the ask)
I’d lead with the value and use the community angle as the identity hook, because they do two different jobs. The headline sells the benefit; the membership wording makes signing up feel like joining something.
Headline: “Beat the 3pm Slump get the Protocol + 20% off your first can.”
Button / membership label: “Join the Flight Crew,” so it reads as belonging, not redeeming a coupon.
One rule I’m firm on: ask for the email only. Every extra field, and a forced “enter your mobile number” box especially, is friction that pushes the abandonment rate up. I’ll go after the phone number later, once we’ve earned a bit of goodwill.
The Strategic Why (dopamine vs oxytocin)
The reward is the dopamine: the guide and the discount are the bit of excitement that earns the click. But people protect a phone number far more than an email, and that hesitation is really a trust gap, so the oxytocin has to be built into how the box looks and reads. A plain line like “No spam. One email, then you choose what you hear from us” does more than it looks, and keeping it to a single field keeps the perceived risk low enough that the reward wins. The phone number then comes as a warm second ask later (“want a heads-up before your 3pm slump? add your number”) instead of a cold demand on a box from a brand they don’t know yet.

Task 2 The Relationship Sequence
I planned the three emails around the same basic structure: a subject line, a hero image, short body copy, and one clear CTA. I also tried to put the most important message at the top, so the reader understands the offer quickly. What changes between the emails is the customer’s stage in the funnel and their likely state of mind.
Email A ‘Welcome & Scratch’ (instant)
They’ve just handed over their data and they’re as interested as they’ll ever be, so making them wait is the worst thing I could do. This goes out immediately and gives them the reward while the motivation is hot.
The plan for turning them from interested into a first-time buyer is to let the guide do the trust work and the code do the nudging. I put the “read” button first so someone who isn’t quite ready can take the value without feeling pushed, while anyone who is ready has the buy button right there. This is also where I slip in the quiet phone-number ask.
Email B ‘We See You’ (cookie response)
The cookies say they’ve hit the Ingredients page three times and still haven’t bought. That’s a pretty clear tell: they’re interested enough to keep researching, but something specific is holding them back. The job here is to sell off the risk, not to shout the offer louder.
I meet them on the exact page they kept coming back to and turn that research into reassurance, because transparency is what builds the trust they’re missing. The returns line matters more than it looks: it quietly removes the downside of buying. I deliberately don’t throw a bigger discount at this person, because someone who’s carefully reading a label tends to read a deep discount as “so what’s wrong with it?” That would add doubt, not remove it.
Email C ‘Rescue’ (2-week inactivity)
Two weeks, no opens, no visits. The relationship is going cold and the next step is either we win them back or we tidy them off the list. The problem is just that we’ve fallen out of mind in a very noisy inbox. This was the email I went back and forth on the most, mainly over how hard to push the discount.
Here I’ll use the strongest discount of the three, which is the opposite of what I did in Email B. The reason is the person, not a rule: intent is low and trust is thin, so this is the moment a bigger reward actually makes sense. The “update preferences” option isn’t just being polite either. A clean opt-down protects our sender reputation far better than someone hitting spam, and if reactivation stays low after a couple of attempts I’d rather suppress the contact than keep paying to email a dead lead.
Across the three, the numbers I’d track are different for each: for A it’s the open rate and how many go on to a first purchase; for B it’s clicks back to the Ingredients page and conversions out of that segment; for C it’s the reactivation rate, but watched alongside the unsubscribe and complaint rate, because a rescue email can quietly damage the list if you’re not careful.

Task 3 Handing Off to the ‘Brain’ (CRM & Automation)
The catch with a bought list of 5,000 is that these people never agreed to hear from us, so emailing them straight away is both spammy and a quick way to wreck our sender reputation. The first email would land from a brand they’ve never heard of and go straight to junk. So the trick is to make us feel familiar before that email ever arrives.
Import them carefully and keep them separate from the main email list. Import the 5,000 into HubSpot (or Salesforce) tagged as Data Buy, flagged “unaware / unconsented,” kept apart from the consented list so they can’t hurt our deliverability.
Hash the emails and push them to a Custom Audience. This is the actual move. Instead of emailing strangers, I load them into Meta and LinkedIn and show them ads first, so the brand starts to feel like a face they recognise. It’s the mere-exposure effect doing the trust-building that a cold email can’t.
Run a soft familiarity flight, no hard sell. Top-of-funnel creative about the 3pm slump, not a buy-now ad. The only goal is recognition.
Only email once they go warm. Hold the first email until they click an ad or the pixel catches a site visit. At that point the CRM moves them from unaware to warm and fires the welcome sequence into someone who now knows who we are and showed up with a bit of intent.
That turns the list from a spam risk into a managed pipeline, which is basically the CEO’s point: once we hold the identifier we stop marketing at people and start managing a sequence. The thing I’d measure to prove it was worth it is simple. Keep a small slice as a cold-email holdout and compare its complaint and open rates against the warmed-up group. If the warm-up works, the difference shows up there.

Task 4 The Loyalty Framework
Loyalty is about making it feel too much hassle, or too unappealing, to leave. But the Commitment Spectrum is a useful warning here: people who keep buying aren’t necessarily devoted, they might just be on autopilot, and counter-intuitively the “fandom” group is actually the weakest driver of repeat buying. The Dedicated group in the middle is where the real money is. For a brand nobody’s heard of yet, that rules out the pillars that need scale or time we don’t have.
So I’d go with Convenience and Prestige. For convenience, the CRM runs the subscription and auto-reorder: it learns roughly how fast someone gets through a pack and fires a one-tap “running low?” reminder just before they run out, with payment and address already saved. The customer never has to think about it. For prestige, the CRM tracks who’s engaging with the Focus Lab and tiers the relationship around it (early access, being recognised by name, small status perks in the Flight Crew). Remembering someone’s name and last order at scale is what nudges a habitual buyer into a genuinely loyal one. The thing I’d watch over time is whether contacts are actually moving up the Commitment Spectrum, and the subscription churn rate, because that’s the honest test of whether convenience is sticking.

Phase 2 The Master CRM Database
Reading the live slice of the database and deciding where the rest of the budget should go. Each lead’s status, interest score, history and lifecycle stage tells me what they need next, which is the whole point of the CRM: I can see the journey in detail instead of guessing.
#8807 the Unaware Lead (mere-exposure warm-up)
Data Buy, Office Manager, 45, 10% interest, already hashed for Meta sync, never opened anything. Emailing this person cold is pointless and risky, so I’d keep them out of email completely and warm them through ads. Given a 45-year-old professional in the CBD, LinkedIn and Facebook fit far better than TikTok. The thing I’d tease in the ad is the mid-afternoon work slump specifically: something like “the focus drink for the 3pm wall, without the sugar crash before your 4pm meeting.” I’m selling relief from the slump, not a can. The email only fires once they click an ad or visit the site and flip from unaware to warm.
#8803 the Advocacy engine
This is the best lead on the sheet: 95% interest, three bulk 24-pack orders, and they’ve already referred two friends on their own. So the Advocacy pillar I parked in Task 4 becomes worth turning on, because now there’s a happy customer to build it around. The CRM has already flagged the high interest and the referrals, so I’d invite them into a proper ambassador tier with their own referral code tracked back to their ID. The reward that keeps them is status, not cash: being named in the Flight Crew, getting new flavours first, a vote on what we make next. Their loyalty is tied to identity, so what they’d lose by leaving is the belonging and the status, and that’s a switching cost a rival’s discount can’t easily buy back. Cash rewards would actually cheapen it.
#8805 the recovery workflow (and the judgement call)
Instagram, apprentice, 19, abandoned a 4-pack 48 hours ago. The brief asks whether the recovery email should lead with scientific proof (trust) or a limited-time discount (excitement), and I’d lead with the discount, with one small credibility line underneath.
My reasoning is mostly about who this is. A 19-year-old apprentice is price-sensitive, and they’d already put the pack in the cart, so they were sold on the idea; the thing that stopped them was almost certainly the price or a fiddly checkout, not lingering doubt about the ingredients. Scientific proof is the right lever for the careful researcher (that was Email B, and it suits someone like #8804 who was searching “electrolyte science”). But for a young, price-led buyer who was one tap from finishing, the faster fix is a time-boxed discount plus free shipping to clear the last bit of friction at checkout, and a 48-hour-old cart is still warm enough to chase. I’d keep a short “zero sugar, no crash” line so the discount doesn’t come across as desperate. What I’d check afterwards is not just whether the cart got recovered but whether they buy again, because recovering a sale with a discount is only a win if it doesn’t train them to wait for the next one.
#8808 keeping the Easy Path easy
Gamer, 22, monthly subscription already running, active in the Focus Lab. This is the convenience-loyalty customer from Task 4, already on the path, so the job is mostly to not break it. The CRM keeps the subscription renewing quietly and watches for the boring thing that actually loses loyal customers: a card expiring or a failed payment. A pre-emptive “your card’s about to expire” nudge prevents an accidental churn that has nothing to do with how they feel about us. On top of that I’d use their Focus Lab activity to add small, low-effort extras that suit a gamer (streaks, an auto-add option for new flavours), which layers a bit of prestige on without ever adding a step to the reorder.
CEO challenge the Lookalike expansion
The CEO wants 10,000 more people who behave like #8803 and #8808. Those two are our highest-value patterns, the bulk-buying advocate and the loyal subscriber, so they’re exactly who I’d want to clone.
The mechanics are straightforward: pull our genuine best customers out of the CRM (high interest, repeat or subscription buying, advocacy), export that as a hashed list, upload it to Meta as a Custom Audience, and have Meta build a 1-3% Lookalike from it. Then the new leads drop straight back into the same funnel: warm-up ads, the lead magnet from Task 1, the welcome sequence from Task 2.
The reason this beats just targeting “people who like energy drinks” is that the broad interest is a guess, and it mostly catches the habit crowd who’ll buy whatever’s in the fridge. A Lookalike is modelled on the real behaviour of people who already buy in bulk, subscribe and refer friends, so it’s aimed at proven value instead of an assumption. It’s spending the budget against our own data rather than a hunch, which is the entire reason we collect the data in the first place. I’d judge it by comparing the Lookalike group against a broad-interest group on cost per customer and on what they’re worth after 90 days, and feed the better customers back into the seed as they convert.
