BUS279 — Presentation 1
Sprint 1 Overview & Defence (Sprint 1 + 1b) — 7-Minute Script (Simple Language)
Target length: ~7 minutes. Easy spoken English. Grey italics = stage directions, do not read them out.

1. Opening (about 40 seconds)
(Look at the camera. Calm and slow.)
Hi, my name is Faiaz. This is my overview and defence of Sprint 1. Sprint 1 had two parts. Part one was a UX and UI check of a real coffee website called The Coffee Place. Part two was an email and CRM plan for a new energy drink, Quasar Rush by AuraFlow.
(Small pause. This next line is your main idea. Say it slowly.)
One simple idea links both parts: friction. Friction just means anything that makes it harder or more annoying for a customer to act. Getting someone to a website, or to click an ad, is the easy part. The hard part is making them trust you enough to buy, and then to stay. So most of my choices, in both parts, were about removing that friction.

2. Part one: The Coffee Place (UX and UI) — about 2.5 minutes
(A bit quicker for this line, then slow down for the three points.)
Quick note on UX and UI first. UI is the skin of the site, the look, the buttons, the colours. UX is how the whole thing works and feels when you use it. For the coffee site I did five tasks, but the why matters more than the list, so let me defend the three choices that matter most.
Point 1 — the main button points the wrong way
(Your strongest point. Slow down.)
The big button on the home page says Book a Table. That sends people to a booking form. But this shop mostly sells coffee beans and gear. So the biggest button on the site actually pushes people away from buying. I would change it to Shop Coffee, and make Book a Table a smaller option. This is what the course calls attention hierarchy, which just means the biggest, boldest thing should point to what you most want people to do. Right now it doesn't.
Point 2 — add reviews, not a bigger discount
The product pages have no reviews at all. For a brand no one knows, that is a real trust problem. So I would add star ratings and a few short reviews. I picked reviews instead of a big discount on purpose. A big discount on a brand you don't know can feel a bit dodgy, like something is wrong with it. Reviews work better, because other buyers do the convincing for you. The course says it well: when people see no reviews, they don't complain, they just quietly leave.
Point 3 — the checkout is the real problem
(A bit more energy here.)
The worst problem is that the checkout doesn't work. It stops and says the site can't accept online payments. So a customer can do everything right, add to cart, and still not buy. I explained this as tax versus friction. People will pay a small tax of time and effort to buy something. But a broken checkout makes that effort basically endless. It doesn't matter how good the rest of the site is if the last step is impossible.
I also did not treat my fixes as facts. Each one is a guess I would test. For example, I would run an A/B test, that just means show half the people Book a Table and half the people Shop Coffee, and see which one gets more clicks to the shop. Change one thing, measure it, keep the winner.

3. Part two: AuraFlow / Quasar Rush (email and CRM) — about 2.5 minutes
(New section. Sit up, look back at the camera.)
Part two is the email and CRM plan for Quasar Rush, an energy drink sold on the idea of focus without the crash. Same enemy, friction, but a different moment. Here the job isn't fixing a site. It is getting someone's email and building enough trust to turn them into a loyal buyer. The brief even says ninety-five percent of people leave when you make it too hard, so again, most of my choices are about removing friction.
Point 1 — the sign-up offer, and the trust behind it
Instead of a boring sign up for ten percent off, I offered a short guide called The Afternoon Slump Protocol, plus a discount, behind just one email box, in a pop-up called Join the Flight Crew. The idea here is dopamine versus oxytocin. Dopamine is the reward, the little buzz, that is the guide and the discount, and it earns the click. Oxytocin is trust. People protect their data, so I keep it to one box only and add a simple line: no spam, one email, then you choose what you hear from us. I only ask for their phone number later, once they trust us, not straight away.
Point 2 — three emails, most important bit at the top
I planned three emails. Each one has a subject line, one main image, a short bit of text, and one clear button. I put the most important thing at the top so people get it fast. The course calls that the inverted pyramid. What changes between the three is where the person is and how they feel. One is a welcome email that sends the guide right away. One answers people who are still reading the ingredients, with the science. And one is a win-back email for people going cold. The win-back uses the biggest discount, twenty-five percent, on purpose, because by then they are not very interested, so a bigger reward is worth it.
Point 3 — the CRM and keeping people loyal
My rule for the CRM, which is basically the brain that stores all the customer info, was simple: only email someone once they go warm. That means after they click an ad or visit the site, not the second they exist. For loyalty I used the Commitment Spectrum from class. The surprising bit is that the loud super-fans are actually the smallest and weakest group for repeat buying. The steady middle group is where the real money is. So I went with two things, convenience and prestige. Convenience is a subscription that sends a running low reminder before they run out. Prestige is the Flight Crew identity, treating it as a focus tool, not just a drink.
The last bit was the CEO's question about growing bigger. Instead of just targeting people who like energy drinks, which is really a guess, I would take our best real customers, the ones who subscribe and tell their friends, and build a lookalike audience from them on Meta. A lookalike just means Meta finds new people who behave like your best buyers. So we spend the money on people who act like our proven customers, not on a guess.

4. Closing (about 30 seconds)
(Look at the camera. Slow down for the last two lines. Hold for one second before you stop recording.)
So to bring both parts together. The coffee site loses people on friction: a button that points the wrong way, no reviews to build trust, and a checkout that doesn't work. The AuraFlow plan removes friction earlier, gets the email with low risk, then uses email and the CRM to build trust and keep people coming back. Both parts come down to the same thing: find the friction, remove it, and test my ideas instead of just guessing.
Thanks for watching.

Bonus: quick answers if you get asked questions
(Not spoken in the video. Just in case there are questions.)
“Why reviews instead of a discount?” A big discount on an unknown brand can look dodgy. Reviews build trust because other buyers vouch for it, which is what a new brand is missing.
“Why ask for email but not the phone number first?” People guard their phone number more. Asking for it early is friction. Email first is low risk, then the phone later once they trust us.
“Why not chase the super-fans?” On the Commitment Spectrum the loud fans are the smallest, weakest group for repeat buying. The steady middle group spends the most, so I aim at them.
“Why a lookalike instead of just targeting energy-drink fans?” Broad targeting is a guess. A lookalike copies the behaviour of our best real customers, so the money goes to people likely to act the same way.
“What is friction, in one line?” Anything that makes it harder or more annoying to act. The job is to keep it lower than the effort the customer is willing to give.
