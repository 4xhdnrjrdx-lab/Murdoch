My Assignment 1 venture is a small craft chocolate brand, and my Assignment 3 group is building the plan for taking it into the UAE, so this question is close to work I am already doing. What this session's frameworks make clear is that the three countries in the question need three different playbooks. The mistake would be to write one international strategy and translate it three times.

The US is the closest match to how I would sell by default. Hall places it at the low-context end of communication, and the Trompenaars testimony data puts Americans among the most rule-focused people surveyed. People there will buy from a stranger if the claims are precise and the paperwork is in order. So the approach is direct: a single-origin bar sold online, blunt product claims about cocoa percentage and origin, and serious attention to FDA labelling, because in a universalist market the written rules are the relationship. What I would not do is spend the first year building a personal network. Nobody is waiting for that.

Japan needs the opposite instincts, but only in places. Communication is high-context and negative feedback is indirect, so when a distributor says something may be difficult, that is a no, and pushing past it costs face on both sides. Deciding is consensual and slow, then execution is fast. Yet Japan sits with the monochronic countries on scheduling, so patience with decisions does not buy tolerance for a late shipment. The product consequence is concrete. Chocolate in Japan is largely a gifting purchase, so the gift box that leads my UAE line would lead there too, except the reason is ritual rather than occasion, and the packaging carries more of the message than the bar itself.

Russia runs on a third logic. The Trompenaars data places it clearly on the particularist side, where relationships are obligations and a very detailed contract can read as distrust. Hofstede's power distance is high and deciding is top-down, so the meeting worth having is with the one person who can say yes, not with a committee. Communication is high-context like Japan, but on Erin Meyer's evaluating scale Russians give unusually direct negative feedback, a combination both the American and the Japanese playbooks would misread. The approach there is a local partner first and distribution second, because without someone whose personal network answers the phone, emails do not get replies.

The nuance I am carrying into Assignment 3 is that these dimensions do not travel together. Japan is indirect in communication but strict on time. Russia is indirect in register but blunt in judgement. Assuming one general "foreign" pattern would make me wrong in both directions at once.
