Pre-workshop tasks

I watched Adam Galinsky's talk on speaking up for yourself first. The part that stayed with me was the idea of a range of acceptable behaviour. When you have little power that range is narrow, so speaking up feels risky and most people just stay quiet. His way out of it is to argue on behalf of someone else, or to signal flexibility before you ask for something. I used the second one without realising it when I emailed our lecturer about my Assignment 3 group being formed across two classes. Instead of only asking for permission, I explained what had happened and asked what we should do about it. The group was accepted. Watching the video afterwards, I could see that I had stumbled into a technique rather than invented one.

The reading on cross-sector collaboration made a point I had not thought about before. A problem should be solved at the scale at which it exists. One organisation working alone on something bigger than itself will always fall short, and the article argues for designing with the people affected rather than for them. That connects back to the customer-centric approach from Session 3 more closely than I expected.

The list of nine collaborator types was the least useful part for me, mainly because I could see myself in three of them depending on the week. If I have to pick one, I am closest to the type who does the work quietly and shares it late. That is a habit worth breaking in a group assignment.

In class activities

Choosing a collaboration partner. The three tests are shared target markets with similar buyer personas, a complementary brand identity, and similar marketing goals. On top of that the partner should not be a competitor. I applied this to the small chocolate brand I used in Assignment 1. A specialty coffee roaster passes all three: the same kind of customer, the same interest in where the product comes from, and we are not selling the same thing. A large supermarket own-brand would fail the second test, because the whole story of the product is that it is small and traceable, and a supermarket label erases that. The examples given in class were Nike with Apple, and MINI with Woolmark, and the pattern behind both is the same.

Collaboration tools. The task was to set up Trello or Asana together with Slack or WhatsApp for the Assignment 3 group. We did both. The WhatsApp group came first, on the day the group was confirmed, because we sit in two different classes and there is no other way to reach each other between sessions. I set up the Trello board that evening with three lists, one for the script, one for the slides and one for the recording, and put the same deadline on every card. The split between the two is not what I expected. Almost everything happens in the chat, and the board turns out to be useful for one thing only, which is seeing whose part has not moved. I keep it current because I created it. If I stopped, I do not think anyone else would pick it up.

Publicity and influencers. The session listed networking events, podcasting and interviews as channels. The examples of Lewis Howes and Marie Forleo were the interesting ones, because they built their own brands by interviewing people who were already known. That is a cheap way to borrow attention when you have none of your own yet.

In person collaboration. This part pointed to personality testing through Crystal, which is DISC based. I would not claim a personality result is a revelation, but the underlying point holds. Knowing that one teammate wants detail and warning before a decision, while another wants the summary and nothing else, saves a lot of friction later.

Three take-aways

1. A collaboration is not a favour. The three criteria work as a filter, and a partner who fails one of them costs more than working alone.

2. The tool matters less than the owner of the tool. A board nobody updates is worse than a chat everyone reads.

3. Speaking up has technique behind it, not just confidence. That was the most practical thing in the session.
