Pre-workshop tasks

The Stampfl article on business model scalability was the harder of the two readings and the more useful one. The authors separate five design antecedents that decide whether a model can scale: technology, the cost and revenue structure, adaptability to legal regimes, network effects, and user orientation. What I took from it is that scalability is designed in, not discovered later. They also split conceptualisation from realisation, which explains why a business can look scalable on paper and still fail to grow. I tested my own Assignment 1 idea, a small chocolate brand, against the five. Technology came out weakest, because there is no real online channel, and legal adaptability came out as the one that decides everything the moment the product crosses a border.

The Forbes article on Pokemon GO is the opposite case, a product that scaled faster than almost anything before it. Reading it after Stampfl, the interesting part is not the download numbers but which of the five antecedents were doing the work. Network effects and technology carried it. Cost structure barely moved, because adding a million players costs the company almost nothing extra. That is exactly the definition of scalability the session used later.

In class activities

Defining scalability. Hallowell defines a scalable business model as one where revenue grows faster than the corresponding cost base. Before this session I would have used growth and scale as the same word. They are not. Opening a second workshop is growth, because costs rise in step with sales. Selling the same product through a website into another country is closer to scaling. The Harvard framing in the slides was blunter: it is not enough for the technology to be scalable, the whole business model has to be, and investors ask whether it will scale before almost anything else.

Sustaining and disruptive innovation. The Airbnb clip made this concrete. Airbnb grew without owning the asset that its industry is built on, which is why hotels could not simply copy it. I found it useful to admit that most ventures, including mine, are sustaining rather than disruptive. Saying so honestly seems better than claiming to disrupt an industry that has not noticed you.

Framing and consumer decisions. The gambles and the yoghurt example are simple but they land. Twenty percent fat and eighty percent fat free are the same yoghurt, and people choose the second one. The same effect works on grades: below the top twenty five percent sounds like a failure, in the top seventy five percent sounds fine. For a product launch this is a warning as much as a tool. If a claim only works because of how it is worded, it will not survive contact with a customer who thinks about it.

Values. The four types are global, domain specific, terminal and instrumental. The part that mattered for me was the reminder that values shift over time, so the list of perceived Western values in the slides is a snapshot and not a rule. Attitudes to smoking and to the environment were the clearest examples on the slide.

Group activity: product and country. The activity was to take a product and a country at random and build an entry strategy. I worked through it with a pair that is directly relevant to me, Australian craft chocolate going into the United Arab Emirates. Three things came out quickly. Heat is a product problem, not a logistics problem, since the bar itself has to be reformulated. Certification and labelling are the real gate to entry. And the leading product should be a gift box rather than a single bar, because the gifting occasion is what drives the purchase here.

Three take-aways

1. Growth and scale are different things, and confusing them makes a plan look better than it is.

2. Scalability is a design decision made early, not a result that appears once sales rise.

3. Framing changes choices even when the facts are identical, which is useful for marketing and dangerous for planning.

Post-workshop tasks

I reviewed the Forbes article again with the scalability model in mind rather than reading it as a news story. I also went through the VALS survey, which sorts respondents into one of eight types: innovator, thinker, believer, achiever, striver, experiencer, survivor and maker. It put me in the achiever group, with innovator as the secondary type. The description of an achiever is someone who works towards a defined goal and prefers products that are established rather than experimental, and I recognise the first half of that. The second half is wrong about me, since most of what I buy is new rather than proven. That is my main criticism of the instrument. It reads the goal and then assumes the shopping habits that usually travel with it. As a shortcut for segmenting a market it is still faster than building personas from scratch, and for the UAE launch it is enough to tell us that the gift box is aimed at achievers buying for someone else rather than at experiencers buying for themselves. The output of the international strategy activity is the chocolate into the UAE plan described above, which I am now carrying forward into Assignment 3.
