Pre-workshop task

The pre-reading was the review of hubristic leadership by Sadler-Smith, Akstinaite and colleagues. My first reaction was that hubris is just a strong word for arrogance, and the reading corrected that. The argument is that hubris is acquired rather than innate. It develops in people who hold power for a long time and who keep being told they were right, until they lose the connection between their confidence and any evidence for it. That framing matters, because if hubris is acquired then it can be designed against, whereas a personality flaw can only be avoided at hiring. The paper also separates hubris from narcissism, which I had treated as the same thing.

In class activities

Defining leadership. The session opened with Stogdill's line that there are almost as many definitions of leadership as people who have tried to define it, and then gave Northouse's four components: leadership is a process, it involves influence, it happens in a group context, and it is directed at common goals. The component I had underrated is the group context. My working definition before the session was closer to a personal quality that someone either has or does not.

Assigned and emergent leadership. This one applies directly to my own situation. In my Assignment 3 group nobody was appointed to lead. The person who ends up leading is whoever keeps communicating, which is exactly what the slides say emergent leadership looks like. It emerges through communication behaviour over time rather than through a title. Looking back at how the group has worked so far, I took that role without anyone deciding it. When the group turned out to be formed across two classes I was the one who wrote to the lecturer and asked what we should do, and once that email had been sent the rest followed. I set up the board, I put the deadlines on it, and I am the one who asks in the chat whether a part is finished. None of that came from a conversation about who leads. It came from being the person who sent the first message and then kept sending them.

Leaders and managers. Kotter treats them as two complementary systems of action: management produces order and consistency, leadership produces change and movement. Planning and budgeting sit opposite establishing direction, organising and staffing opposite aligning people, controlling opposite motivating. Zaleznik goes further and treats them as different kinds of people. I find Kotter more convincing, because the same person clearly has to do both in a small business, and the reconciliation slide made that point directly.

The strataplex. Mumford, Campion and Morgeson split leadership skills into cognitive, interpersonal, business and strategic layers, and the requirement shifts upward as the organisation grows. This was the most useful model in the session for me. It explains something I had seen without having a name for it, that a founder who is excellent at running one workshop can be genuinely bad at running two countries. The skills that made the first level work are not the ones the next level needs, and nobody warns you at the transition.

Hubris. The path in the slides runs from influence to power to leadership, and then to leaders becoming intoxicated by power. The equation given was leadership plus power equals hubris. The quotes used as examples were uncomfortable to read, especially the count of self references in a single speech. The distinction from Tracy and Robins between authentic pride and hubristic pride was the part I want to keep, because authentic pride tracks something you actually did, while hubristic pride is about who you believe you are. The first is checkable and the second is not.

Three take-aways

1. Leadership is a process inside a group, not a personal quality someone carries around. That changes how I read my own group work.

2. The skills that run a small operation are not the skills that run a large one, and the strataplex gives that a structure instead of leaving it as a feeling.

3. Hubris is acquired, which means it can be designed against with governance rather than only screened for at hiring.

Post-workshop tasks

The post-session material was the Theranos case, in two articles and the whistleblower video. Reading it straight after the hubris material made the sequence obvious. A founder with an unusually large share of the voting rights, a board that could not challenge her, and a culture where raising a problem was treated as disloyalty. What struck me is that the technology failing was not really the story. The governance failing was, because the technology could have been fixed if anyone had been able to say out loud that it did not work. The whistleblower video is the part that stays with you, mostly because of what speaking up cost him personally, which connects back to the Galinsky material from Session 8 more directly than I expected.

For my own Assignment 3 work I have taken one practical thing from this session. Any plan to scale a venture should include a person outside the founding team who has the authority to stop it, because that is the mechanism Theranos did not have.
