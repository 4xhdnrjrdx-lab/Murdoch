## A small basket at Mall of the Emirates

One member of our group, Faiaz, used Self Checkout at Carrefour in Mall of the Emirates, Dubai. He had picked up snacks, drinks and a few household essentials. With only a small basket, he chose a machine to avoid waiting in the regular cashier line. Clear instructions in English guided him through scanning each item, placing it in the bagging area and paying by bank card. One barcode took several attempts to register. It eventually scanned, and he found the purchase straightforward overall.

Faiaz felt that Self Checkout was quicker for this purchase. His account gives us a reason for that preference, although we have no timings for the two lanes. The English prompts made the normal sequence easy to follow. The moment that caused difficulty arose during scanning. He knew what he had to do, but the machine did not read one item on the first attempt. He would have valued a member of staff nearby if the retries had failed. That distinction shapes the improvement we propose at stage 4 of Figure 1.

## Where the scanning happens

Faiaz carried his items to a terminal before scanning. A handheld Scan&Go journey changes the order of the work. The director of a Carrefour demonstration filmed at City Centre Deira in 2020 describes picking up a scanner, scanning before bagging and paying at a dedicated counter (thatguyDeni, 2023). Carrefour UAE’s guide also describes Mobile Scan&Go, where shoppers use their own phones to scan in the aisles and pay in the app (Carrefour UAE, n.d.). These are separate routes. An entrance sign could tell customers who want to check prices as they shop where to collect a scanner. Someone buying just a few things can leave the choice of cashier or terminal until they reach the tills, as Faiaz did.

## Finding goods on the shelf

For a shopper looking for a particular drink, the first question is whether the product is on the shelf. Majid Al Futtaim (2022, p. 44) reported that Tally checked aisles three times daily for issues including low stock, misplaced goods and pricing errors. Deployment extended to 11 additional UAE Carrefour stores in the 2021 reporting period. Our account of Tally comes from this report. Its relevance to Faiaz’s basket lies upstream of checkout, where the accuracy of a shelf check could determine which drinks are available to choose.

## From a shelf alert to an item in the basket

Consider a missing pack at stage 2. A robot can recognise a gap while the stock record still shows a case in the building. Another shopper could also take the last pack just after a scan. We propose giving a Tally alert, where deployed, and a shopper’s question the same route to a stockroom check. The aisle colleague can then refill the shelf or give the shopper a definite answer about alternatives. That answer is useful while the shopper is still choosing what to buy.

The shopper sees the colleague return. Checking whether a case is reserved, missing or awaiting replenishment happens out of view. Following Bitner et al. (2008), Figure 1 connects both activities to the people and records needed to complete them. Wirtz (2023) describes failure-proofing as designing a process to prevent an error. Here that means keeping the refill task open until somebody verifies the shelf.

## What the barcode problem changes

Stage 3 is the choice Faiaz actually faced. The cashier line meant waiting for another person to scan a small basket. At Self Checkout he could do it himself. Stage 4 shows the trade-off. When a barcode fails to register, the same shopper who saved time by changing lanes now has to decide whether to try again or ask for help. The bagging area gave him somewhere to place each scanned purchase. A retry interrupts that rhythm, especially if the next item is already in hand.

We would keep the clear English prompts and make the last accepted item and quantity easy to see. Before retrying, the shopper could check whether the item had already registered. If it remained unreadable, an attendant could identify the product while the rest of the basket stayed on screen. This would address the difficulty Faiaz described without assuming the barcode was damaged or that he needed an employee to finish. Barcode reading is ordinary automation. Interpreting a shelf image to distinguish a gap from a misplaced pack involves the analytical work described by Huang and Rust (2021).

Faiaz paid by bank card at stage 5. Figure 1 also includes an optional recovery step for a different outcome. If a shopper later finds two charges for one pack or questions a promotion, the service desk should retrieve the relevant receipt line. Tax et al. (1998) distinguish the outcome of a complaint, the procedure and how the customer is treated. In this case that means correcting any overcharge, asking for the receipt once, and explaining what happened. It also gives the pricing team a chance to correct an inaccurate ticket for the next shopper.

## What Carrefour UAE could learn from other stores

Two developments in grocery robotics have different implications for this trip. In the United States, Marty’s role at Stop & Shop expanded from spotting floor hazards to checking shelf availability. Its supplier reported the upgrade across more than 300 stores (Badger Technologies, 2023). One machine can collect several kinds of information on a single round. A spill near the dairy cabinet still needs a faster response than a missing yoghurt label. Carrefour UAE could assign different priorities and owners before adding another type of alert.

In Romania, Carrefour’s MARCEL, also called ERIS, pairs a human operator with cameras that read shelf labels. It can compare the label with the price database and print a replacement (Preda, 2022). The useful transfer to Dubai is the short distance between detecting a wrong ticket and fixing it. Staff could trial label correction in one dairy aisle using existing alerts before buying another robot. In Poland, Carrefour’s Kerfuś attracted shoppers who wanted to interact with the moving robot (Carrefour Polska, 2022). Attention has value during a promotion, but a crowd around a robot in a grocery aisle could make goods harder to reach. We would give stock and price accuracy priority over a promotional robot and keep any demonstration away from narrow aisles. These company and supplier accounts describe deployments. They provide no measure of time saved on this UAE trip.

## Which changes would make the trip easier?

First, the store operations manager should run a four-week shelf pilot against a comparable aisle, matching busy periods and recording deliveries and promotions. Give each F2 gap one owner. Measure minutes from report to verified replenishment and, separately, minutes until a shopper gets a definite answer when no stock is available. Also record repeat gaps and staff time. A colleague who correctly reports an empty stockroom should be able to close the customer enquiry while the replenishment task stays open. Otherwise the measure rewards closing alerts prematurely.

Second, start with the barcode retry at Self Checkout. The checkout supervisor could pilot a clearer last-item display and a visible help point. Compare the time from the first failed scan to a correctly recorded item, count abandoned purchases, and check for duplicate charges. Overall checkout time alone would hide this part of the experience. English instructions worked for Faiaz. Testing with shoppers who prefer another language or need assistance would show whether they can use the same route independently. Retaining staffed payment gives shoppers a choice about how much scanning work they take on.

The same pilot needs to account for staff time. An attendant covering several terminals cannot respond to simultaneous calls immediately. Record how often calls overlap before setting staffing levels. For shelf checks, limit incidental images of shoppers and restrict access. An alert left open when the stockroom is empty should prompt an inventory review rather than count against an employee’s performance.

Finally, give the checkout supervisor and service desk shared ownership of price disputes. With the shopper’s agreement, transfer the receipt line and promotion check under one case number. Keep payment details outside the case. Test a two-minute acknowledgement for in-store help and track time to resolution separately. Ask one practical question at the end. Did the customer have to explain the same price problem twice? On a future small shop, we would look for a terminal that lets us scan at our own pace and an attendant we can find if a product will not scan.
