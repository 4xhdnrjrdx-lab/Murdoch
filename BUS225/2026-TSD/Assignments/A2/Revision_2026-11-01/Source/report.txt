## Choosing how to shop at the entrance

Suppose we stop at Carrefour in City Centre Deira after class for spaghetti, tinned tomatoes, plain yoghurt and bread. We have AED 40 for dinner and want to know the total before we reach the till. Would picking up a scanner at the entrance help? The answer depends on what happens in the aisles, especially if a product is missing or a promotion scans at a different price. Figure 1 follows that basket from the entrance to the service desk.

Our evidence comes from comparing two public accounts of the shopping process. The director of a 2020 Carrefour demonstration at City Centre Deira describes unlocking a handheld scanner, scanning before bagging and paying at a dedicated counter (thatguyDeni, 2023). Carrefour UAE’s shopper guide describes that handheld route alongside two alternatives. Mobile Scan&Go lets customers scan with their phones and pay in the app. At Self Checkout, customers take their purchases to a terminal, then scan and pay there (Carrefour UAE, n.d.). The Deira example anchors our walkthrough in a particular store. The guide describes services across the UAE, so the choice on a future trip would depend on the options signposted at the entrance.

For our basket we would take the handheld route if a scanner were ready. Checking the running total while choosing yoghurt could save a walk back from the till. A shopper with a flat phone or an unfamiliar loyalty app might make a different choice. A sign saying only “self-service” leaves a practical question unanswered. Should the shopper scan the first pack here or at the end? At stage 1 we propose showing both instructions where customers pick up baskets, with a cashier route alongside them. Giving people that choice before they bag anything avoids making them unpack four items to change lanes.

## What an empty shelf tells the shopper

At the pasta shelf, suppose the label for the 500 g pack is in place but the space behind it is empty. A larger pack might cost less per kilogram and still take too much of our dinner budget. Our first question would be whether a colleague could find the smaller pack. Majid Al Futtaim (2022, p. 44) reported that Tally checked aisles three times daily for issues including low stock, misplaced stock and pricing errors, and that deployment extended to 11 additional UAE Carrefour stores in the 2021 reporting period. That establishes how Tally was used locally. It gives no live reading for this shelf in Deira.

## From a shelf alert to an item in the basket

A robot can recognise a gap while the stock record still shows a case in the building. Conversely, another shopper could take the last packet just after a scan. At stage 2, either a Tally alert where deployed or a shopper’s question can start the same proposed task. An inventory colleague checks the stockroom. The aisle colleague either puts the pack on the shelf and verifies it, or explains that it is unavailable and offers an alternative. Knowing the answer soon enough to change dinner plans would matter more to us than knowing how many gaps the robot detected.

The shopper sees the colleague return to the shelf. Checking whether the last case is reserved, missing or still awaiting replenishment happens out of view. Following Bitner et al. (2008), the blueprint traces both activities back to the records and people needed to complete them. Wirtz (2023) describes failure-proofing as designing the process to prevent an error. Here that means keeping a replenishment task open until somebody checks the facing.

## Scanning, paying and getting a price corrected

At stage 3 we would scan the tin before putting it in the bag. If the screen registered two tins, we would want to see the item description and quantity while the tin was still in our hand. With conventional Self Checkout, the same error could appear later, with the rest of the basket on the counter and someone waiting behind us. Both methods shift scanning work to the customer. Only the aisle route gives this shopper the chance to change the dinner while still beside the shelf. Reading a barcode is ordinary automation. Interpreting an image to distinguish a gap from a misplaced pack adds the analytical task described by Huang and Rust (2021).

Suppose the shelf ticket offers a discount on one yoghurt, but the scanner displays the regular price. Before promising a correction, the colleague needs the barcode, pack size and conditions on the ticket. The offer might apply to two pots. In that case, explaining the condition lets us decide whether to put the pot back. If the ticket really is out of date, the supervisor should resolve the basket discrepancy and send a separate task to correct the ticket. Otherwise the very next shopper inherits the same problem. At stage 5 a handheld basket goes to its dedicated payment counter. In the separate Self Checkout lane, the shopper scans each pack at the terminal. Charging both customers accurately matters more than which lane finishes first.

If we only noticed the discrepancy on the receipt, a correction would involve another stop before leaving. Tax et al. (1998) distinguish the outcome of a complaint, the procedure and the way the customer is treated. For this purchase that means receiving the right adjustment, showing the receipt once, and hearing why the price differed. A prompt acknowledgement would do little for us if nobody at the desk could authorise the remedy.

## What Carrefour UAE could learn from other stores

Two developments in grocery robotics have different implications for this trip. In the United States, Marty’s role at Stop & Shop expanded from spotting floor hazards to checking shelf availability. Its supplier reported the upgrade across more than 300 stores (Badger Technologies, 2023). One machine can collect several kinds of information on a single round. A spill near the dairy cabinet still needs a faster response than a missing yoghurt label. Carrefour UAE could assign different priorities and owners before adding another type of alert.

In Romania, Carrefour’s MARCEL, also called ERIS, pairs a human operator with cameras that read shelf labels. It can compare the label with the price database and print a replacement (Preda, 2022). The useful transfer to Dubai is the short distance between detecting a wrong ticket and fixing it. Staff could trial label correction in one dairy aisle using existing alerts before buying another robot. In Poland, Carrefour’s Kerfuś attracted shoppers who wanted to interact with the moving robot (Carrefour Polska, 2022). Attention has value during a promotion, but in our pasta aisle a crowd could make the goods harder to reach. We would give stock and price accuracy priority over a promotional robot and keep any demonstration away from narrow aisles. These company and supplier accounts describe deployments. They provide no measure of time saved on this UAE trip.

## Which changes would make the trip easier?

First, the store operations manager should run a four-week shelf pilot against a comparable aisle, matching busy periods and recording deliveries and promotions. Give each F2 gap one owner. Measure minutes from report to verified replenishment and, separately, minutes until a shopper gets a definite answer when no stock is available. Also record repeat gaps and staff time. A colleague who correctly reports an empty stockroom should be able to close the customer enquiry while the replenishment task stays open. Otherwise the measure rewards closing alerts prematurely.

Second, test the entrance signs and a visible help point with shoppers using each checkout route. Compare time from the first item picked to a correct paid receipt, plus rescans and baskets that have to be unpacked. Invite shoppers who prefer a cashier or need assistance to take part. An improvement in terminal speed would be poor value if the queue simply moved to the scanner rack. Retaining staffed payment also protects access for people who cannot handle a scanner or read its screen. Tally’s camera needs shelf information. Limit incidental images of people, restrict access and avoid using a colleague’s alert count as an individual performance score.

Finally, give the checkout supervisor and service desk shared ownership of price disputes. With the shopper’s agreement, transfer the receipt line and promotion check under one case number. Keep payment details outside the case. Test a two-minute acknowledgement for in-store help and track time to resolution separately. Ask one practical question at the end. Did the customer have to explain the same price problem twice? For our next grocery trip, being able to keep the basket, understand the price and get on with dinner would be a better reason to use Scan&Go again than a promise of faster payment.
