1 Service context
Carrefour UAE combines groceries with services that help shoppers find, compare and pay for them. The UAE business is managed by Majid Al Futtaim. Its ESG report records Tally inventory robots being introduced to 11 more UAE Carrefour stores in 2021 (Majid Al Futtaim, 2022, p. 44). This enables the study of robots in these stores, but doesn't mean they are in every store today. Figure 1 shows our proposed improved blueprint for a physical grocery store, from planning a basket to dealing with problems after purchase.
What we're measuring is how much effort automation saves the shopper. Accurate shelf alerts are useful only if someone acts on them. When receipt errors are hard to fix, fast checkout isn't as useful. The blueprint thus assigns intelligent systems to certain staff roles.
2 Matching technology and the service task
Huang and Rust (2021) identify mechanical AI for repetitive tasks, thinking AI for analysis, and feeling AI for interpreting emotions. Shelf scanning mainly fits the first type; recognizing missing items and recommending alternatives require analysis. These features can be integrated into the same system. Just saying hello is not enough proof of emotional comprehension. A barcode scanner is self-service technology, not necessarily AI. In this case intelligence is in interpreting shelf images and product requests; the payment interface is still the checkout. We shouldn't overstate AI's benefits or overlook traditional automation.
Tally is a physical robot, which moves through aisles and gathers shelf data. The Majid Al Futtaim report describes checks for missing, misplaced and incorrectly priced items. A chatbot doesn't restock shelves; it processes information. The proposed integration of a robot alert and an employee's restocking task addresses fail point F2: recorded availability does not match what a customer can purchase. Although the products are physical, it is a service reliability issue. Restocking is still a human task and staff need to confirm any unclear alerts.
Carrefour has also experimented with software tools in countries outside the UAE. In June 2023, it introduced Hopla on its French website, giving shoppers ideas based on their budget and dietary requirements (Carrefour, 2023). In June 2021, Carrefour announced Brazil's Nutri Choice, an algorithm comparing nutritional scores and prices using purchase history (Carrefour, 2021). Neither announcement confirms a UAE deployment. These examples provide information to our proposed optional planning assistant in stage 1 and product comparisons in stage 4. Recommendations should use local catalog information and explain their rationale; nutritional ratings should not replace checking ingredients.
Wirtz et al. (2018) categorize service tasks into routine services and emotional and social judgment services. Their framework supports automated checks while retaining staff for disputes. These predictions don't establish local customer acceptance. Company publications describe initiatives, not independent satisfaction tests. Before scaling up, Carrefour UAE should assess task performance and customer effort. Carrefour's examples extend automation to conversational advice. In a bakery study, recommendation effects varied with visit frequency (Song et al., 2024). Its remotely controlled robot limits conclusions about autonomous performance.
3 Reflecting on the customer journey
When we picture the buying process, we think of a better quality service. In stage 1, customers can benefit from recommendations within their budget while still checking package sizes and current prices. At fail point F1, persuasive advice can create expectations the store cannot meet. Showing the source and update time helps customers judge uncertainty before visiting. We feel it is better to warn of possible unavailability than promise that an item is in stock.
Even with digital tools, aisle signs and accessible routes continue to be crucial when entering a store and looking for products. The customer as co-producer approach explains how moving tasks to shoppers may lower labor costs while increasing customer work (Wirtz, 2023). A search screen helps if using it takes less time than asking an employee. Stage 2 therefore keeps a route without an app. The same journey should be facilitated through the physical servicescape and digital interface.
At F2, we think that it might be better to have a stocked shelf than a robot. The arrows in Figure 1 indicate that detection, staff verification, and restocking must occur prior to the change in the availability message. Just scanning won't get all the benefits. This is reflected in the line of visibility: the shoppers rely on the work that is done out of their line of sight.
Who is in control of the choice at stage 4? A customer can reject something that fits within the target price but doesn't align with their food choices. At F3, to support that choice, it would be helpful to explain the recommendation and provide staff support. With self-checkout, the shopper is responsible for scanning items and checking the total. If a discount is missing at F4, staff should check the price and save the basket so the shopper need not restart.
After purchase, we would judge the retailer partly on how easily it corrects errors. If someone was to repeat the information to a chatbot and a staff member at F5, it would cause a lot of repetitive work. If the case record were forwarded to an authorized colleague, it would prevent the customer from having to re-enter the record. This follows the course's process-redesign principle: remove unnecessary steps and provide recovery if self-service fails (Wirtz, 2023). We would seek assistance which was reliable during the interaction, particularly when there was something wrong.
4 Ethical implications
Personalization creates a conflict between useful recommendations and excessive data collection. Brazil's Nutri Choice shows how purchase history can support recommendations (Carrefour, 2021). For the proposed UAE assistant, these records could also reveal sensitive dietary preferences. At stages 1 and 4, guest mode should work without saving a purchase profile. Separate consent for saved preferences and marketing would let shoppers choose convenience without disclosing unrelated data. Short retention and restricted access would reduce exposure, although less historical data could weaken personalization.
Commercial interests can also influence recommendations. Huang and Rust (2021) warn that optimization can produce biased results without intentionally discriminatory instructions. If Carrefour's proposed assistant prioritizes profit margins, cheaper suitable products may become harder to find. The retailer should label sponsored offers and allow sorting by unit price. Shoppers retain the final choice. Advertising revenue should not depend on shoppers mistaking paid placements for unbiased advice.
Physical robotics affects more than app users. Wirtz et al. (2018) identify privacy and safety concerns arising from robots' sensing capabilities. At stage 3, shelf analysis should minimize capture of identifiable shoppers, with restricted use and retention of incidental images. The robot must yield to people and have an accessible stop procedure. At F3, staffed assistance and readable instructions protect shoppers with limited digital confidence, language difficulties, or disabilities. Equal access costs money, but compulsory self-service would shift the retailer's operational burden onto those least able to handle it.
Employees may save time through automated checks while facing more alerts, monitoring, and difficult customer cases. Carrefour should involve shelf staff in alert design, provide paid training, and measure workload alongside response times. An unresolved alert should not count as poor employee performance when stock is unavailable. Managers remain responsible for staffing and recovery; using third-party software does not remove their responsibility to customers. Integration and maintenance costs may exceed savings if alerts are inaccurate. A limited pilot protects service continuity while testing this risk. Managers should audit rankings and workload weekly.
5 Recommendations and learning
Before adding more customer-facing automation, the store operations manager and inventory staff should test drive the stage 3 alert-to-restocking process. Apply a four-week pilot with matched peak and off-peak periods after obtaining a baseline. Operating savings vs equipment, maintenance and training costs should be compared by the finance manager. Expansion should improve availability without increasing complaints or staff workload. Compare alert accuracy, time to verified shelf correction and product availability with manual checking over similar trading times. Close the alert at F2 only after staff confirm the item is available on the shelf. This failure-proofing control ensures that unfinished tasks do not lead to false promises of availability. Keep an eye on blocked aisles and staff workload; suspend robot operations following a safety incident.
The digital product and merchandising personnel need to check and compare the optional assistant's performance at F1 and F3 with the standard product search. Track budget adherence, unsupported recommendations and task completion, including customers using staff support. Evaluate various language and accessibility requirements. Maintain the same offers and service levels across routes. If catalog data can't support the advice, show uncertainty and provide staff assistance. Don't personalize ranking until there is clarity on commercial motivation and data controls.
The checkout supervisor and the customer service manager should be co-owners of fail points F4 and F5. Have all item, price and receipt information in one place, get customer confirmation before making changes, and empower employees within a predetermined refund policy. Proposed pilot standards are acknowledgment within 2 minutes of an in-store help request and a human response within 1 working day of a post-purchase case. Monitor the percentage meeting these standards, repeat contacts and total resolution time. These are targets, not measured outcomes.
More automation still requires people to take action with the information. We would assess Carrefour's smart service by its information accuracy and how easily shoppers can get help.
