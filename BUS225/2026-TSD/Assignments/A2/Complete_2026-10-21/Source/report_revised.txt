1 Service context
Carrefour UAE combines the sale of tangible groceries with services that help customers find, compare and pay for them. Majid Al Futtaim operates the UAE business. Its ESG report records the expansion of Tally inventory robots to 11 additional UAE Carrefour stores during 2021 (Majid Al Futtaim, 2022, p. 44). This gives a basis for examining robots in these stores, although it does not show that every store has them today. Figure 1 proposes an improved journey for an ordinary physical grocery store, from planning a basket to resolving a problem after purchase.

We assess automation by whether it reduces the customer's total effort. An accurate shelf alert is useful only when someone acts on it. A quick payment is less valuable if correcting a charge becomes difficult. The blueprint therefore connects intelligent systems to named human responsibilities.

2 Matching technology to the service task
Huang and Rust (2021) distinguish mechanical AI for repetitive activities, thinking AI for analytical decisions, and feeling AI for interpreting interactions and emotions. Applied to Figure 1, repetitive shelf scanning belongs mainly to the first category; recognising missing products and recommending alternatives require analytical processing. These capabilities may coexist within one system. Friendly wording alone does not demonstrate emotional understanding. A barcode scanner is self-service technology, not necessarily AI. In this proposal, intelligence lies in interpreting shelf images and product requests; the checkout remains a payment interface. We should not credit AI with benefits that ordinary automation already provides.

Tally is a physical robot: it moves through aisles and collects shelf information. Majid Al Futtaim's report describes checks for missing, misplaced and incorrectly priced products. A chatbot is software that handles information; it cannot replenish a shelf. At stage 3, the proposed connection between a robot alert and an employee's replenishment task addresses F2, where recorded availability differs from what a customer can actually buy. This is a service reliability issue despite the tangible nature of the goods. Replenishment remains a human action, and staff must verify uncertain alerts.

Carrefour has also tried software tools outside the UAE. Carrefour announced Hopla on its French website in June 2023 to suggest purchases using budgets and food constraints (Carrefour, 2023). In Brazil, its June 2021 Nutri Choice announcement described an algorithm comparing nutritional scores and prices, with suggestions linked to purchase history (Carrefour, 2021). Neither announcement establishes a UAE deployment. They inform the proposed optional planning assistant at stage 1 and product comparisons at stage 4. Advice must use local catalogue data and show its basis; nutritional ranking cannot replace checking ingredients.

Wirtz et al. (2018) distinguish routine service tasks from encounters requiring emotional and social judgement. Their framework supports automating repeated checks while retaining employees for disputes. However, their conceptual predictions do not prove local customer acceptance. Likewise, company publications describe initiatives, not independent tests of satisfaction. Carrefour UAE should test task performance and customer effort before expanding the proposed system.

3 Reflecting on the customer journey
Mapping the journey changes how we assess good service. At stage 1, shoppers could value suggestions within their stated budget while still cheking pack size and current prices. F1 shows how convincing advice can create an expectation that the store cannot meet. Displaying the source and update time would help shoppers judge uncertainty before travelling. We consider a clear warning about uncertain availability more useful than a confident promise.

At entry and product search, aisle signs and accessible routes remain important even with digital assistance. The course's treatment of customers as co-producers explains why transferring tasks to shoppers can reduce staff costs while increasing customer work (Wirtz, 2023). A search screen would help if it saved walking, unless registration took longer than asking an employee. Stage 2 therefore retains a route without an app. The physical servicescape and the digital interface must support the same journey.

At F2, we see a stocked shelf as more important than the presence of a robot. Following the arrows in Figure 1 reveals that detection, staff verification and replenishment must happen before the availability message changes. Scanning alone cannot deliver the promised benefit. The line of visibility makes sense here: customers depend on work that usually happens out of their sight.

Stage 4 raises a different question: who controls the choice? A shopper could reasonably reject an alternative that met a price target but conflicted with their dietary preference. Explaining the recommendation and offering staff help at F3 would support that choice. At payment, self-service also makes the shopper responsible for scanning and checking the total. A missing discount at F4 should trigger a price check, with the basket preserved so the shopper need not restart.

After purchase, we would assess the retailer partly by how easily it corrects mistakes. At F5, repeating the same details to a chatbot and then an employee would add avoidable effort. Passing the case record to an authorised colleague would avoid asking the customer to start again. This applies the course's process-redesign principle: remove unnecessary steps and provide recovery when self-service fails (Wirtz, 2023). We would look for reliable help throughout the journey, especially when something goes wrong.

4 Ethical implications
Personalisation creates a conflict between useful advice and excessive collection. Brazil's Nutri Choice illustrates how purchase history can support recommendations (Carrefour, 2021). For the proposed UAE assistant, those records could also expose sensitive dietary preferences. At stages 1 and 4, a guest option should work without storing a purchase profile. Seperate consent for saved preferences and marketing would let shoppers choose convenience without surrendering unrelated data. Short retention and restricted access would reduce exposure, although less historical data could weaken personalisation.

Commercial interests can also affect recommendations. Huang and Rust (2021) warn that optimisation can produce biased outcomes even without deliberately discriminatory instructions. If Carrefour's proposed assistant prioritised margin, cheaper suitable products might become harder to find. The retailer should label sponsored suggestions and allow sorting by unit price. Customers retain the final choice. Advertising income should not depend on shoppers mistaking a paid placement for impartial advice.

Physical robotics affects people beyond app users. Wirtz et al. (2018) identify privacy and safety concerns arising from robots' sensing capabilities. In stage 3, shelf analysis should minimise capture of identifiable shoppers, and any incidental images should have restricted use and retention. A robot must yield to people and have an accessible stop procedure. At F3, keeping staffed help and readable instructions protects customers with limited digital confidence, language difficulties or disabilities. Equivalent access costs money, but compulsory self-service would shift the retailer's operational burden onto those least able to manage it.

Employees may gain time from automated checks while facing more alerts, monitoring and difficult customer exceptions. Carrefour should involve shelf staff in alert design, provide paid training and measure workload alongside response speed. An unresolved alert must not automatically count as poor employee performance when stock is unavailable. Managers remain responsible for staffing and recovery decisions; outsourcing software does not transfer their responsibility to customers. For the company, integration and maintenance costs may outweigh savings if alerts are inaccurate. A limited pilot protects service continuity while testing that risk.

5 Recommendations and learning
The store operations manager and inventory team should pilot the stage 3 alert-to-replenishment process before adding more customer-facing automation. Use a four-week pilot after recording a baseline, with matched busy and quiet periods. The finance manager should compare operating savings with equipment, maintenence and training costs. Expansion should require better availability without increased complaints or workload, rather than simply more alerts processed. Record alert accuracy, time to verified shelf correction and shelf availability against manual checks during comparable trading periods. F2 closes only after staff verify the shelf. This failure-proofing control prevents an uncompleted task from creating a false availability promise. Monitor blocked aisles and staff workload; pause robot operation after a safety incident.

The digital product and merchandising teams should test the optional assistant at F1 and F3 against ordinary product search. Measure budget compliance, unsupported recommendations and task completion, including customers using the staffed alternative. Test diferent language and accessibility needs. Keep the same offers and service entitlement across routes. If advice cannot be supported by current catalogue data, show uncertainty and offer employee help. Avoid deploying personalised ranking until its commercial incentives and data controls are understandable.

The checkout supervisor and customer-service manager should jointly own F4 and F5. Transfer item, price and receipt details into one case, obtain customer confirmation before corrections, and give employees authority within an agreed refund policy. Proposed pilot standards are staff acknowledgement within two minutes of an in-store help request and a human response within one working day for a post-purchase case. Track the share meeting these standards, repeat contacts and total resolution time; these are proposed targets, not measured results.

Automation still depends on staff being able to act on the information. We would judge Carrefour's intelligent service by the reliability of its information and how easily shoppers can reach a person for help.
