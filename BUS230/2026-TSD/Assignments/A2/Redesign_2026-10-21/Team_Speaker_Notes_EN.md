## 1 — Using ADKAR with
Context and Evidence

Presenter: Mazin. Budget: 55 seconds.

Good afternoon. We are Class 2 Group 4. Today we will run a formal training session for managers using ADKAR with context and evidence. Our aim is to help you decide what to do when a colleague understands a change, but the working conditions still prevent them from carrying it out.

We will explain the original model, examine four bounded limitations of using it alone, and demonstrate our modified decision record. The retail case is fictional. The record is a teaching adaptation developed for this assessment, and its effectiveness has not been tested in a real organisation.



Sources: Prosci n.d.-a; BUS230 Unit Outline §5.2.2

## 2 — The management session

Presenter: Mazin. Budget: 60 seconds.

Our session has six sections. First, we define the change and the manager's task. We then explain ADKAR and its useful features, before examining where a simple individual checklist needs more explicit decision rules.

Next, we show the modified record, including actions, responsibilities, and evidence. We connect those design choices to academic research and compare our addition with what Prosci already provides. Finally, we demonstrate a short scenario and return to the manager's next decision.

Mazin will introduce the model. Beryl will develop the analysis and the first records. Faiaz will connect the actions into one tool. Aram will explain the evidence and lead the demonstration. The presentation does not require printed material.



Sources: BUS230 Unit Outline §5.2.2

## 3 — Cedar Retail: the change

Presenter: Mazin. Budget: 70 seconds.

Cedar Retail is a fictional retailer introducing barcode stock receiving across its day and evening shifts. The affected task is specific: receive a delivery, identify a stock mismatch, and report it correctly. We use one task so that the manager can observe a concrete behaviour rather than claim that the whole store is ready.

In the scenario, the evening colleague understands the purpose and procedure. However, reliable scanner access and protected practice time are missing. The colleague also worries that old stock errors will reduce their performance rating.

These are scenario assumptions, not findings from employee interviews or company data. They let us examine technology, staffing, authority, rewards, and individual support in the same decision. There is no invented performance improvement or consent from real employees.



Sources: Fictional teaching scenario; no company research data

## 4 — Three manager objectives

Presenter: Mazin. Budget: 65 seconds.

By the end of the training, a manager should be able to do three things. First, identify a personal ADKAR barrier and explain the relevant support. Second, connect that barrier to working conditions and the people who can change them. Third, explain a decision to continue, revise, or pause an affected task using evidence.

We will check these objectives with a delivery scenario near the end. The learning check asks for a reasoned decision, not a memorised list of letters. A manager must explain the missing prerequisite, name its owner, and say what would need to change before the decision is reviewed.

These objectives are preparation targets for this training. They do not claim that the class demonstration can establish business benefits or validate a readiness measure.



Sources: Prosci n.d.-a; Nilsen 2015

## 5 — The original ADKAR model

Presenter: Mazin. Budget: 85 seconds.

ADKAR stands for Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement. Prosci presents it as a model of individual change. Each element identifies a different outcome that the person needs. Understanding the purpose does not automatically create willingness. Knowing the procedure does not automatically demonstrate the ability to perform it.

The model provides a memorable language for choosing support. A knowledge difficulty may call for instruction. An ability difficulty may require practice or changes to working conditions. Reinforcement helps sustain use after the initial introduction.

The elements are individual prerequisites rather than five fixed calendar phases for an entire project. In our case, the colleague can explain the receiving procedure. That supports a Knowledge judgement, but the manager still needs to observe performance with usable equipment and time. We retain this distinction in the modified tool.



Sources: Prosci n.d.-a; Creasey 2025

## 6 — ADKAR and the wider method

Presenter: Mazin. Budget: 85 seconds.

The model's strengths are clarity and focus. It helps managers avoid giving the same training to every person. Prosci's public assessment uses a one-to-five scale and identifies the first element at three or below as a barrier point. We use that as a conversation prompt, without treating it as a universal prediction of success.

Assessment, targeted action, practice, and reassessment already exist. They are not innovations from our group. More importantly, the full Prosci Methodology also includes the PCT Model and the 3-Phase Process. These address sponsorship, project health, planning, and sustainment.

Our critique therefore concerns the standalone five-outcome checklist and the explicit rules in a small manager record. It would be inaccurate to say that Prosci has no organisational planning or evaluation. Beryl will now examine the four remaining gaps.



Sources: Prosci 2022; Prosci n.d.-b, n.d.-c, n.d.-d; Holt et al. 2007

## 7 — 01 / Shared working conditions

Presenter: Beryl. Budget: 70 seconds.

The first limitation concerns shared working conditions. Ability can include external constraints, so we are not arguing that ADKAR ignores resources. The narrower gap is that the five outcome labels do not provide a complete shared record of conditions, owners, and prerequisites across roles.

Weiner distinguishes organisational readiness from individual readiness. Rafferty and colleagues also examine readiness at individual, group, and organisational levels. Oreg and colleagues show that responses to change involve both personal and contextual factors. These works support examining the wider setting. They do not test our record.

At Cedar Retail, more instruction cannot repair a scanner or protect time in the roster. The manager must connect those conditions to the task and assign them to people with authority. Our first addition makes that ownership and prerequisite explicit.



Sources: Weiner 2009; Rafferty, Jimmieson, and Armenakis 2013; Oreg, Vakola, and Armenakis 2011

## 8 — 02 / A reasonable objection

Presenter: Beryl. Budget: 75 seconds.

The second limitation concerns reasonable objections. A colleague can support accurate stock records while disagreeing with a penalty rule. Piderit describes responses to change as multidimensional and potentially ambivalent. Oreg's research connects resistance with context as well as personal characteristics. The recent review by Oreg and Sverdlik also draws attention to ambivalence and timing.

A low Desire score therefore does not tell the manager whether the proposed requirement is fair or workable. Ford and colleagues argue that change agents can contribute to resistance through their own actions and inactions. This supports treating a concern as information rather than automatically as a problem inside the employee.

We retain the Desire conversation, but add an accountable sponsor response. The sponsor must explain whether to retain, revise, or stop the disputed requirement. This rule does not guarantee agreement or remove unequal power.



Sources: Piderit 2000; Oreg 2006; Ford, Ford, and D'Amelio 2008; Oreg and Sverdlik 2026

## 9 — 03 / Dependencies across roles

Presenter: Beryl. Budget: 70 seconds.

The third limitation concerns the order of actions across roles. The earliest individual barrier helps target support for the colleague. It does not, by itself, provide a project schedule for IT, the store manager, and the sponsor.

Burke and Litwin describe links between organisational systems, management practices, motivation, and performance. Herold and colleagues investigate how contextual and personal factors interact in commitment to change. Pettigrew and colleagues emphasise context, levels of analysis, time, and process. Together, these perspectives justify making dependencies visible.

In the scenario, the manager can discuss the colleague's concern while IT works on access. Those activities can happen in parallel. The affected receiving task still pauses if an essential condition is missing. We keep personal prerequisites and add a separate rule for coordinating organisational work.



Sources: Burke and Litwin 1992; Herold, Fedor, and Caldwell 2007; Pettigrew, Woodman, and Cameron 2001; BUS230 Topic 7

## 10 — Four limitations, four additions

Presenter: Beryl. Budget: 70 seconds.

The fourth limitation concerns evidence. A brief score is a useful snapshot, but it cannot automatically prove workplace performance. ADKAR already distinguishes Knowledge from Ability and supports observation. Our remaining question is what the manager should do when self-report, task performance, and working conditions disagree.

Holt and colleagues developed a multidimensional readiness instrument through a systematic process. Jo and Hong's newer scale also involves substantial development and testing. Their work reminds us that a readiness judgement needs a clear construct and appropriate evidence. Our classroom record has not undergone that validation.

The table brings all four bounded gaps together. Each has a specific addition, and each addition has a limit. We are examining a use case, not claiming four proven defects in every application of ADKAR.



Sources: Holt et al. 2007; Jo and Hong 2023; Prosci n.d.-d

## 11 — Our modified decision tool

Presenter: Beryl. Budget: 65 seconds.

Our modified tool is a digital decision record attached to the individual ADKAR assessment. We retain the five outcomes and the support they guide. Around that baseline, we add four connected records: working conditions, a response to a challenge, cross-role dependencies, and an evidence disagreement rule.

The output is a specific task decision. It names what can continue, what needs revision, and what must pause. It also names the people responsible and the evidence needed for review.

This is a teaching adaptation informed by academic work. It is not an official Prosci product or a replacement for the full methodology. Its contribution is the explicit connection between the records and the decision. The ideas of dialogue, learning, and reassessment already exist, so we do not present those alone as new.



Sources: Prosci n.d.-a, n.d.-b, n.d.-c, n.d.-d; design proposal

## 12 — Conditions and concerns

Presenter: Beryl. Budget: 70 seconds.

Here are the first two records filled with our fictional case. The IT lead owns reliable scanner access. The store manager owns protected practice time. Each entry needs completion evidence rather than a promise that someone will fix it.

The concern record preserves the colleague's meaning: inherited errors may reduce their performance rating. It asks for evidence and an alternative, such as separating inherited errors from new receiving mistakes. The operations sponsor owns the response because that role controls the policy.

For demonstration, the record requests a decision before the affected task goes live. We have not invented a sponsor approval. A staff representative can check that the concern is represented accurately, without implying everyone consents. Faiaz will now connect these records to the actions and the final decision.



Sources: Ford, Ford, and D'Amelio 2008; design proposal; fictional entries

## 13 — The dependency register

Presenter: Faiaz. Budget: 75 seconds.

My part connects the records into a usable decision. The dependency register links an upstream condition to a downstream task, its owner, and completion evidence.

For example, the colleague's practice depends on reliable access. IT owns that condition. The store manager controls time in the roster. The sponsor controls the response to the disputed rating rule. Naming the employee on every action would hide these differences in authority.

The three organisational owners can work while the manager continues the individual support conversation. However, parallel work does not mean the receiving task is ready to go live. Essential prerequisites still need verification.

This rule draws on systems and contextual perspectives. It does not prove that the ADKAR prerequisite sequence is false. It makes the separate organisational action order visible and links it to the colleague's support.



Sources: Burke and Litwin 1992; Herold, Fedor, and Caldwell 2007; Pettigrew, Woodman, and Cameron 2001

## 14 — One complete decision record

Presenter: Faiaz. Budget: 75 seconds.

The complete record begins with the task, role, and shift. That prevents a decision about one activity from turning into an unsupported statement that the entire organisation is ready.

Next, the manager records the individual ADKAR need and the appropriate support. The conditions and challenge fields then identify organisational inputs and disputed requirements. The dependency fields link those inputs to the affected action. The evidence fields keep self-report, task performance, and working conditions separate.

Finally, the manager records a decision, its reason, the owners, and the trigger for review. The same version of the record is used across the case, so one presenter does not quietly change another presenter's prerequisite.

The accompanying PDF is fillable. It contains a blank record for another task and a completed fictional example. It is a decision aid that still requires informed judgement.



Sources: Design proposal informed by Weiner 2009 and Pettigrew, Woodman, and Cameron 2001

## 15 — Implementation and owners

Presenter: Faiaz. Budget: 65 seconds.

Implementation starts by agreeing the exact task and what acceptable evidence would look like. For Cedar Retail, the colleague receives a delivery, spots an unexpected quantity, and follows the agreed reporting route. These are local teaching criteria, not thresholds validated by Prosci.

The manager then allocates conditions and obtains a reasoned response to the policy concern. IT demonstrates access on the actual shift. The manager makes time available. The sponsor explains the rule decision to affected colleagues.

Practice follows when the essential inputs are usable. The manager records what actually happened and reviews the decision when equipment, staffing, or the procedure changes. This reflects the course's emphasis on process and context. A universal timetable would be inappropriate, so each action receives a locally agreed date in the working record.



Sources: Pettigrew, Woodman, and Cameron 2001; BUS230 Topics 4-7

## 16 — Continue, revise or pause

Presenter: Faiaz. Budget: 80 seconds.

The record has three decision states. Pause means an essential prerequisite is missing. The manager holds the affected task and assigns the condition to its owner. This is a scoped pause, not automatically a stop to the entire change.

Revise means the proposed design or requirement needs a reasoned decision. A concern about penalties goes to the sponsor. If the sponsor retains the rule, the reason still needs review and communication. A recorded response does not automatically make the policy acceptable or eliminate the employee's concern.

Continue means essential prerequisites and suitable task evidence have been verified, with support and reinforcement continuing. A high average score cannot override a missing condition. The overall judgement remains with the manager. When evidence is incomplete or contradictory, the record says not yet verified and triggers investigation.



Sources: Design proposal; Prosci n.d.-d; Nilsen 2015

## 17 — Feasibility and evaluation

Presenter: Faiaz. Budget: 65 seconds.

The tool is feasible as a small shared record using existing roles. It does not require new software, a company-wide survey, or a numerical readiness index. Managers still need time, authority, and a clear task definition.

We expect the record could make responsibility and decision reasons easier to inspect. Those are proposed benefits, not achieved results. A pilot would track task completion accuracy, unresolved dependencies, reasoned responses, and the time spent maintaining the record.

Nilsen distinguishes approaches that guide implementation from those that explain or evaluate it. That distinction prevents us from treating a useful guide as proof of effectiveness. Errida and Lotfi also show the importance of context in a model review and a construction-company case. Their findings cannot simply be treated as validation for retail or for our adaptation.



Sources: Nilsen 2015; Errida and Lotfi 2021

## 18 — Risks and practical safeguards

Presenter: Faiaz. Budget: 60 seconds.

Four risks need managing. Keep paperwork limited to the task. Name an escalation route if the sponsor does not respond. Agree a realistic task before observing Ability. Keep evidence gaps visible instead of assuming readiness.

Stouten and colleagues compare management advice with change research. Errida and Lotfi review wider success factors. We use that literature to justify checking conditions, participation and review. It does not validate our record. The research comparison and its limits are in the appendix.

The tool supports judgement; it cannot create resources or replace authority. Aram will now show how to verify evidence and make a decision.



Sources: Nilsen 2015; design risks

## 19 — Beyond the ADKAR Score

Presenter: Aram. Budget: 60 seconds.

An ADKAR score is a useful starting point, but it is not the whole decision. A colleague may report that they understand the change and feel ready. We still need to see whether they can perform the task, with the equipment and time that the job requires.

Our fourth addition is the Evidence Disagreement Rule. If self-report, task performance and working conditions conflict, readiness is not yet verified. The manager investigates the difference instead of averaging it away.

This rule is our team’s proposal, not an official Prosci feature. It has not been scientifically validated. Let us make the distinction between Knowledge and Ability clear.

## 20 — Knowing Is Not the Same as Doing

Presenter: Aram. Budget: 60 seconds.

At Cedar Retail, the employee can explain how to receive a delivery, scan the items and report a stock mismatch. That gives us evidence of Knowledge. It does not yet demonstrate Ability.

For Ability, we need a realistic task: use the actual scanner, receive a sample delivery and report a mismatch correctly. Reliable equipment, protected practice time and a safe learning setting make that demonstration possible.

Baldwin and Ford help us think about transfer from training to work. Edmondson helps explain why people need to feel safe to discuss mistakes and learn.

Our takeaway is simple: Knowledge must be explained. Ability must be demonstrated. Now you will apply that distinction.

01:00–02:00. Point to Knowledge, then Ability. Do not claim that an explanation proves complete Knowledge.

Sources: Baldwin and Ford 1988; Edmondson 1999

## 23 — The Evidence-Based Decision

Presenter: Aram. Budget: 75 seconds.

Our recommended answer is C: pause the affected task. This does not mean stopping the entire change project. The employee can still learn the procedure while the team fixes the conditions needed for this task.

The IT Lead provides reliable scanner access. The Store Manager arranges protected practice time. The Operations Sponsor reviews the disputed penalty policy and communicates a reasoned decision. If the rule unfairly penalises inherited errors, it needs revision; the review should establish that, rather than assume the facts.

After these conditions are addressed, the manager observes a realistic task to verify Ability. Policy revision matters, but the task stays paused until its essential prerequisites are met.

A motivational talk alone cannot solve missing organisational conditions. The response needs named owners, practical action and a new evidence check.

04:45–06:00. Reveal after hearing the group. Emphasise task-level scope. Restart only after prerequisites and task evidence are verified. Do not invent completed scanner repairs, sponsor approval or a successful demonstration.

## 24 — From ADKAR Scores to Better Decisions

Presenter: Aram. Budget: 60 seconds.

To conclude our group presentation, keep four principles in mind.

First, identify the individual ADKAR barrier and provide relevant support. Second, check the organisational conditions needed for the actual task. Third, listen to employee concerns and provide a reasoned response. Fourth, verify real workplace performance before assuming readiness.

We keep ADKAR’s useful language and add explicit owners, dependencies and an evidence-based task decision. Our record is a teaching proposal. Its usefulness still needs to be tested in practice.

The next step for a manager is to try the record on one task, review what happens and improve it.

Don’t assume readiness. Verify it. Thank you. We are happy to take your questions.

## 29 — Research and evidence boundaries

Presenter: Appendix. Budget: 0 seconds.

Our benchmark combines existing Prosci provision with change research. Stouten and colleagues connect practitioner models with scholarly evidence. Errida and Lotfi review change models and apply success factors in a construction-company case.

We use these works to justify examining diagnosis, enabling conditions, participation, and review. Their settings and methods also limit the conclusion. Neither source tests our retail record. They support the design rationale without establishing the adaptation's effectiveness.

ADKAR identifies individual support needs. Wider Prosci already provides organisational process and sponsorship. Research adds perspectives on context, voice, learning, and evidence.

Our record makes owners and decision reasons explicit. It draws on those existing ideas. Nilsen helps distinguish a guide to action from an approach to evaluation.

The next step is a local usability check, followed by suitable evaluation before wider use. The classroom exercise can check understanding, but cannot establish business impact.

Benchmarking here means comparison with research-informed design criteria, not evidence that Cedar Retail is a real company or that a field evaluation has been conducted.

## 30 — Communication and manager workflow

Presenter: Appendix. Budget: 0 seconds.

The manager defines a task, diagnoses the person's support needs, and connects them to conditions and responsibilities. They then verify evidence, record a decision, and set a review trigger.

This workflow keeps both individual support and organisational responsibility visible. Each decision stays linked to the affected task and its evidence.

Colleagues on both shifts receive an explanation, practice, and a chance to question the design. IT and the manager confirm dependencies. The sponsor explains the policy decision.

Lewis examines information-giving and input-seeking channels. Morrison reviews employee voice and silence. Oreg and Sverdlik highlight context and timing. These works support asking how communication affects a decision.

Following Topic 8's underscore and explore approach, the manager explains the purpose and invites useful questions. An invitation alone does not guarantee meaningful participation. The record therefore includes a response owner.
