§0 How to write each answer (student style)

Basic paragraph (use for every point)

  1. Point: name the idea from the unit.
  2. Meaning: one sentence on what it means.
  3. Case: a concrete fact, name or quote from the case.
  4. So what: why it matters for the change.
"One [concept] is … . In the case, [fact]. This shows that … , which means the change …"

Recommendation formula ("develop and justify")

  1. What to do (one clear action).
  2. Who does it and how.
  3. Why: link to the model and to the problem in the case.
  4. Expected result (+ one risk if there is time).
"I recommend that [who] … . This links to [model / cell] because … . In the case, [problem], so this should …"

Tips from the past papers

  • Respect the numbers: "two (2) change actors", "at least three each", "two (2) recommendations". Number them: Benefit 1, 2, 3 / Limitation 1, 2, 3.
  • "Apply the model" = go through its parts on the case facts, not just define it.
  • "Discuss" = several sides + a short conclusion.
  • Use the names from the case (e.g. Ian, Anna, John, Widdows).
  • Name the theory once: Tichy (1983), Kotter and Schlesinger (2008), Lewin, Galbraith (Star Model), Johnson & Scholes (Cultural Web), McKinsey 7-S.
  • Do not invent numbers that are not in the case.
  • Short intro sentence → points → one-line conclusion.

Useful linking phrases

This can be seen when… · For example, in the case… · This suggests that… · As a result… · On the other hand… · However, a limitation is… · Overall, …

§1 Drivers of Change (Topic 1)

Почему происходят изменения, какие они бывают.

Three approaches to change

  • Tool-based: recipes, techniques and tools to manage change.
  • Process-based: understand how change actually unfolds.
  • Critical: question traditional beliefs, e.g. the "70% of change fails" myth (no empirical evidence).

Planned vs emergent

  • Planned: top-down, follows objectives, steps, situational analysis; managers assume control.
  • Emergent: change appears in practice, complex and dynamic, depends on interpretations. Managers support it by adapting conditions.

Content, scope, rate

  • Content = what is changed; scope = how far it reaches; rate = how fast.
  • Radical: extensive in content and scope, complex.
  • Revolutionary: radical + changes the fundamental orientation (culture / structure), whole organisation, short time.
  • Evolutionary: small, incremental, cumulative; can give radical results long term.
  • Risks of radical / revolutionary change: threatens belonging, anxiety, conflict and power struggles, identity intensive.
  • What works best depends on context; you cannot just copy others.

External drivers (PESTE / PESTL)

  • Political, economic, sociocultural, technological, demographic, new knowledge.
  • Examples: globalisation (digital, services), climate change, new expectations of work (autonomy, flexibility, purpose), ageing workforce, automation.
  • They interact and are all active at the same time.

8 common internal drivers

  1. Low performance
  2. Inadequate skills and knowledge
  3. Facilities relocation
  4. Reshuffling of responsibilities (restructure)
  5. Product and service innovations
  6. Accidents and scandals
  7. New top management
  8. Organisational politics

Critical points

  • Cause and effect are hard to prove; many changes happen at once.
  • The "stated" reason may not be the real one (e.g. a new manager who wants to look decisive, §5).
  • Management fashions and recipes: rarely a quick fix.
"The main driver is external / internal: [fact]. There is also a second driver, [fact], so the causes interact. The change looks planned and revolutionary because … , which can create anxiety and conflict."

§2.2 Networks

Change agents are more successful when they: (1) hold central positions in the informal network, whatever their formal role; (2) their network matches the type of change; (3) have good relationships with "fence-sitters" (people who are unsure). Power often comes from informal networks, not a senior title.

They also need skills, knowledge and attributes (Change Management Institute framework), e.g. communication, influencing, planning, resilience.

§2.3 Recipients as co-producers

  • Recipients are often blamed for failure, and managers for poor or unclear communication.
  • The result of change depends on people's interpretations. Different experiences and interests → ambiguity and disagreement.
  • Treat employees as active co-producers, not passive recipients.
  • Ask: how are the plans received? What do they mean to the people affected?

§3 Change Readiness (Topic 2)

Готовность к изменениям: 5 убеждений и что на неё влияет.

Readiness is an individual attitude with cognitive and emotional parts. Collective readiness = shared beliefs from social interaction. Check whether the organisation is receptive and whether the people affected are ready; if low, do the "groundwork" first.

Five readiness beliefs

BeliefQuestion to ask in the case
DiscrepancyDo people believe change is needed?
AppropriatenessDo they think this change is the right response?
EfficacyDo they feel able to do it?
Principal supportWill management and peers give resources and information?
ValenceWhat is in it for me? Personal costs vs benefits.

Three groups of factors

  • External pressures: industry and technology change, regulation, professional groups.
  • Internal context enablers: participation, communication, leadership (strongest evidence).
  • Personal characteristics: needs, values, self-confidence, risk tolerance, self-efficacy, dispositional resistance.

How to increase readiness (cheap steps)

  • Participation in decisions.
  • High-quality change communication (why + what it means for me).
  • Deal with the organisation's history of change (past experience, support, value fit).
  • Show support: training, resources, time.
  • Show personal benefits (valence), see the list in §4.
"Readiness for change was low / high. For discrepancy, … . For principal support, … . Recommendation 1: … to increase efficacy because … ."

§4 Resistance to Change (Topic 3)

Сопротивление: признаки, типы, как работать.

Perspectives (resistance can be seen as…)

a troublesome element · a valuable contribution · a natural occurrence · identity work · an instrument of power · compliance. Question the idea that resistance must be "dealt with". The label "resistance" can be used to delegitimise employees.

Symptoms

ActivePassive / hidden
Being critical (ridiculing, arguing) · appealing to fear, starting rumours · distorting or selectively using facts · blaming, accusing, intimidating · manipulating, sabotaging · blocking, underminingAgreeing but not following through · failing to implement change · procrastinating · feigning ignorance · withholding information or support · standing by and allowing change to fail

Passive resistance (blind acceptance or quiet non-action) is often more problematic long term than open resistance.

Complexity

  • More than one cause; can be about the process, not the content.
  • Changes over time: fades or escalates, shifts in focus or type.
  • Ambivalent: for and against at the same time. Compliance ≠ acceptance.

Four types of resistance (cause → how to handle)

TypeCauseHandle by
PsychologicalLoss of control, uncertainty, confusion. E.g. innate dislike of change, low tolerance of uncertainty. Grief cycle: shock → defensive retreat → acknowledgement → adaptation & change.Detailed information, involvement, more feeling of control.
PoliticalDifferent goals and interests; change threatens power, status, identity. E.g. "not in my interest", breach of psychological contract, ethical conflict.Understand and work with politics; support alternative identities.
CulturalDifferent values and beliefs about what is important. E.g. attachment to the organisational culture and identity.Work with the culture(s), respect sacred values, do not rush.
Sensemaking-relatedE.g. do not believe change is needed or appropriate, lack of process clarity, wrong timing, too much change (at work or in personal life), legacy of past changes.Build readiness, understand local context, trust, dialogue.

Who resists?

  • Not only frontline staff: middle managers (tension), senior managers (can block; can be wrong, e.g. Ballmer on Google, 2005), other departments.
  • Support is highest when middle managers initiate and top or middle managers execute.

Resistance as a valuable contribution: 5 actions

  1. Encourage dialogue, even with complaints.
  2. Clarify the purpose: why roles have to change.
  3. Consider new possibilities: accept good ideas from resisters (they are close to operations).
  4. Listen to the voices, participation.
  5. Deal with the past: leftover issues from old failures.

What's in it for them? (reduces resistance)

Security · money · authority · status · responsibility · better conditions · self-satisfaction · personal contacts · less time and effort.

Kotter & Schlesinger (2008): pace

Move fast if there is a crisis. Move slowly if you need information and commitment from others, if the change manager has less power than the resisters, or if resistance will be intense.

Six strategiesUse when…AdvantageDrawback
1 Education & communicationLack of information or wrong informationOnce persuaded, people helpSlow if many people
2 Participation & involvementInitiators lack information; others can resistCommitment, their ideas usedSlow; may produce a poor design
3 Facilitation & supportFear and anxiety, adjustment problemsBest for adjustment problemsTime, cost, can still fail
4 Negotiation & agreementA group will lose out and has powerAvoids major resistanceExpensive; others may want deals
5 Manipulation & co-optationOther tactics fail or too costlyQuick and cheapProblems later if people feel manipulated
6 Explicit & implicit coercionSpeed is essential; initiator has powerFast, overcomes resistanceRisky, people stay angry

Strategies can be combined. Be careful: calling problems "resistance" can hide bad management practice.

"[Name] shows passive resistance: [symptom]. The type looks sensemaking-related / political because … . To handle it, management could use participation and involvement (Kotter & Schlesinger, 2008) because …"

§5 Tichy's (1983) TPC Model & the Political System (Topics 4–6)

Матрица 3×3: технический, политический, культурный ряд × миссия/стратегия, структура, HR.

All 9 boxes are interrelated: change in one area affects the others. Use it as a planning tool so the change is relevant, aligned and implemented well. Long-term success needs alignment between the subsystems and with the external environment.

Mission & strategyStructureHuman resources
Technical (§7)Assessing the environment · assessing the organisation · defining the mission and fitting resources (SWOT, vision, scenario planning)Differentiating · integration · aligning structure to strategy ("structure follows strategy")Fitting people to roles · specifying performance criteria · measuring performance · staffing and development

Political system: key ideas (Topic 4)

  • Organisations are political systems: many stakeholders compete for people, money and space. Change upsets the allocation of resources → conflict.
  • The change manager must "play the game". Politics can be positive when it benefits the organisation.
  • Political tasks are rarely discussed openly but take much senior time.
  • A "simple" restructure (removing a layer) also shifts power → snowballing problems.
  • Change can be a power game: e.g. a new manager wants to look decisive, then uses "external forces" to legitimise it.
  • Ask: whose voices are heard or excluded? Whose interests drive the change? Are all stakeholders identified and key ones involved? Is decision authority in the right place? Is it clear who gets rewarded and for what?
  • Political skill, especially networking, is highly valued.

Example recommendations (Political row)

  • M&S: involve key stakeholders (e.g. the resisting manager, staff reps) in a steering group so their interests are heard → builds a coalition and reduces political resistance.
  • M&S: use networks — meet senior sponsors and fence-sitters one by one to win support before the decision meeting.
  • Structure: give clear decision-making authority / a budget to the person running the change so it is not stuck waiting for approval.
  • HR: link rewards and appraisal to supporting the change (e.g. a team bonus, recognition for local managers) so people see a personal benefit.
  • HR: make promotion and succession decisions transparent so people do not feel the change is unfair.

Lean Production case (Topic 4): managers said "good for all and empowering", but workers had little input, informal agreements were broken and benefits went to management → mistrust and resistance.

§6 Cultural System, 7-S and Cultural Web (Topic 5)

Культура — самое трудное для изменения. Модели 7-S и Cultural Web.

  • Culture = shared values, beliefs and norms that shape how people think, feel and act; "the way we do things around here".
  • Culture affects public image, effectiveness, direction, attracting and keeping staff. It can cause falling sales, complaints, absenteeism, turnover.
  • Strong culture: values widely shared and intensely held, "walking the talk". Good only if the behaviours it encourages still help the organisation.
  • Weak culture: little agreement on values → may need strengthening.
  • Why hard to change: organisational values are linked to personal values; people were hired and socialised for fit. Changing culture can feel like rejecting their experience → cultural resistance.
  • How to change: change behaviour through new practices, systems and HR policies; values follow when the benefits become clear.

McKinsey 7-S

§7 Technical System, Vision, Scenario Planning, Star Model (Topic 6)

Технический ряд Tichy, видение, сценарии, Star Model.

What changes & contradictions

Strategy, structure, technology, systems and procedures, HR practices, relationships, leadership style, culture. They should reinforce each other but can contradict:

  • teamwork expected, but rewards are individual;
  • middle manager autonomy, but key performance information withheld;
  • cross-functional sharing inside strong silos;
  • staff engagement policy + autocratic leadership.

Ripple / knock-on effects: new technology → new skills, practices, rewards, appraisal, leadership. Also ask "What doesn't change?"

Vision

Gives clarity on goals (not "just cost cutting"), motivates, sets scope, depth and time frame.

Benefits: enhances performance · facilitates change (road map) · sound strategic planning · recruits talent · focus for decision making (appraisal and incentives aligned).

Criticisms: hard to set a clear vision early in big change ("building the bridge as you walk it") · ignores operational detail · distracts from current problems · gap between vision and capabilities · managers too committed to re-test it ("escalation of commitment" bias) · assumes a stable future, can block new opportunities.

Leadership: the leader is a catalyst / facilitator of the vision. An organisational vision (core ideology = values + core purpose, lasting) is better than a charismatic CEO's personal vision.

Scenario planning ("Shell method")

  1. Decide the drivers for change / assumptions
  2. Bring the drivers into a viable framework
  3. Produce 7–9 initial mini-scenarios
  4. Reduce to 2–3 scenarios
  5. Draft the scenarios
  6. Identify the issues arising

Example: PwC future of work — Orange world (small is beautiful), Green world (companies care), Blue world (corporate is king). Not a waste if wrong: people react faster to what really happens. PESTLE can feed it. Limit: needs decision making and aligned systems, otherwise "just discussion".

Galbraith Star Model (5 points)

StrategyDominant point: how to reach the vision. If unclear, there is no basis for other decisions.
StructureFormal authority, grouping of activities (org chart)
Processes & lateral capabilityFormal and informal coordination across units
RewardsHow performance is measured and paid; aligns individual and organisational goals
PeopleHR: selection, training and development, performance management

All 5 must be aligned; changing only structure has little effect without the others. Overlaps with 7-S. Can implement scenario-planning insights. Missing: cultural and political aspects.

Star Model recommendation: "The rewards point is not aligned with the strategy: [fact]. I recommend … , because the Star Model says all five points must fit together. This should …"

§8 Models and Theory (Topic 7)

Диагностические модели и модели внедрения; сильные стороны и критика.

§8.1 Diagnostic models

Find misalignments and opportunities: Tichy TPC, 7-S, Star Model, Cultural Web, Lewin's Force Field.

Force Field: list driving forces (for change) and restraining forces (against) toward the "target situation". Driving forces much stronger → go ahead; restraining forces much stronger → abandon or delay; balanced → plan action: increase drivers and / or reduce restrainers (reducing restrainers usually creates less tension).

Criticism of diagnostic models: abstract, hard to apply locally, need lots of analysis but do not say what to do, reduce change to boxes and arrows, ignore how people experience change.

§8.2 Checklist models

  • DICE (BCG): score Duration, Integrity (team), Commitment (C1 senior, C2 local staff), Effort. Fits quantified cultures, timing and staff demands.
  • ADKAR (Prosci): Awareness → Desire → Knowledge → Ability → Reinforcement. Focus on the individual.
  • Stouten evidence-based, 10 steps: diagnose the problem · assess readiness · evidence-based solutions · leadership throughout · compelling vision · use social networks · enabling practices (goals, learning, participation, transitional structures; fair) · small-scale experiments · assess progress · institutionalise. Fits evidence cultures (engineering, R&D, health).

+ clear, simple, similar advice. − little theory ("worked for us"), only high-level guides, need adapting to the local context.

§8.3 Stage (n-step) models

  • Lewin: Unfreeze → Change (move) → Refreeze.
  • Kotter 8 steps: 1 sense of urgency · 2 guiding coalition · 3 vision & strategy · 4 communicate the vision · 5 empower action / remove obstacles · 6 short-term wins · 7 consolidate gains, more change · 8 anchor in culture.
  • McKinsey 5A: Aspire · Assess · Architect · Act · Advance.

+ time dimension: anticipate problems at each stage. − ignore the past (earlier change attempts) · stop at "refreeze" (change decays, can block new innovation) · still do not say exactly what to do.

§8.4 Contingency approaches

  • Pivotal roles: start where change has the biggest impact.
  • Styles continuum: choose a leadership style by time, expertise and commitment.
  • Stace–Dunphy: when change is vital, time is short and consensus unlikely, a dictatorial / directive style can work. Other styles: collaborative, consultative, coercive; scale from fine-tuning to corporate transformation.
  • Change Kaleidoscope (Hope Hailey & Balogun): 8 context factors — Time, Scope, Preservation, Diversity, Capability, Capacity, Readiness, Power → 6 design options: path (scope and speed) · start point (top-down, bottom-up, combination, pilot sites) · style (coercive ↔ collaborative, can vary by group and phase) · target (outputs, behaviours, attitudes, values) · levers (technical, political, cultural, education, communication) · roles (leadership, change teams, external facilitation). It shows the change the organisation is able to make, not only the one it needs.

− hard to "fit" in practice; complex to explain; needs flexible leaders; changing style may hurt credibility; is everything contingent?

§8.5 Process perspective

Change is a process, not an event; it has a past, present and future. Look at: context (incl. history) · substance (scale, scope) · transition process (tasks, timing) · political activity · their interactions. It says "be aware of this", not "do this".

8 lessons: no universal recipes · change is political · time, planning, flexibility · challenge assumptions (critical reflection) · learn from good and bad experience · align training with new procedures · communication ("the way you tell it / sell it") · contradictions are food for reflection.

− can make change look too complex, people look like minor characters, awareness but no clear direction.

§8.6 Criticism of tools in general

  • Simple cause and effect, not real human interaction.
  • Must be adapted to each context.
  • Need rule following, which varies with context.
  • Experts use practical judgement, not rules.
  • Tools give a false sense of control, but stay popular because they make a messy process look simple.

British Airways (Topic 7 case): compare Lewin, a checklist and a contingency lens on the same story; each one misses something.

§9 Communicating Change (Topic 8)

Коммуникация: диалог, разговоры, истории, легитимация, метафоры, стратегии.

  • Communication conveys the message, builds understanding and commitment, reduces resistance. But too much = information overload.
  • Monologic (one way) shuts out voices → silence, detachment, resistance, even sabotage. Dialogic: many voices, open and transparent, employees invited in.

Four types of conversation

  1. Initiative: starting a change (formal CEO statement or chat in the canteen)
  2. Understanding: making sense of the problem and options
  3. Performance: action, requests, promises
  4. Closure: declaring it finished, what works, new possibilities

Language must match the intention: flatter organisation → do not just send top-down directives.

Storytelling

  • Sensemaking: people tell stories to understand uncertain change; stories often challenge changes that do not fit their worldview.
  • Nostalgia: idealised "good old days" → shock absorber (continuity, less anxiety) but also a preventer of change.
  • Sensegiving: managers cannot stop sensemaking but can influence it with their own stories (impression management). Stories mobilise people for or against change.
  • Stories that stick are plausible: they fit identity and culture. Most common justification: "weak market / customer orientation".

§11 Worked examples (2025 exam — 3CA / CRM)

Пример того, как студент отвечает по шпаргалке. Не копировать на другой кейс без изменений.

Q1 Two change actors, benefits and limitations (13 marks) — short model

Ian is the main change actor. He is an internal change agent and a middle manager (marketing and sales director). Benefit 1: he designed the CRM in Australia, so he knows the system and its results (8–10% better retention). Benefit 2: as an internal agent he understands the business and its staff, so he saw that the real work is helping staff use the system and redesign processes. Benefit 3: he built good links with the NZ staff, who became excited about the system, which helps readiness. Limitation 1: as a middle manager he does not have the power to approve the project, so he has to wait for the COO and CEO. Limitation 2: he is from Australia, so for the NZ office he is almost an outsider, and Anna does not support him.
