## PART A — My contribution & key findings  (~1.5 min, ~185 words)

Hi, I'm Faiaz. For our BUS293 team report, Group 11 looked at team dynamics inside Westpac Banking Corporation, one of Australia's big four banks.

My section was team trust, and I also pulled together the reference list and the appendices, and wrote the conclusion once everyone's parts were in.

What I found is that trust isn't one single thing. It rests on three foundations: whether you see someone as having integrity, as genuinely caring about your wellbeing, and as being good at the job. Take any one of those away and trust slips, and because it builds slowly, even one broken promise can set you right back.

The part that really got me was how trust sits underneath psychological safety. In a tightly regulated bank, if people don't trust that speaking up is safe, they go quiet about risks. Westpac learned that the hard way. In 2020 it paid a record one-point-three billion dollar penalty for over twenty-three million breaches of anti-money-laundering law, partly because problems weren't escalated. So my recommendations focused on protected speak-up channels and consistent, fair leadership.

## PART B — Reflection on our teamwork (Ch.8 Team Dynamics)  (~2 min, ~295 words)

Now, looking back at how we actually worked, chapter eight gave me the language for it.

The first thing that hit us was a change in team composition. We started as four and dropped to three when one member was withdrawn from the unit, so three of us had to re-coordinate and absorb his share. The upside, which the chapter points out, is that smaller teams often coordinate and trust each other better, and that's how it played out.

Roles mattered here. Nobody was the formal leader, but one teammate, Owethu, informally took on what chapter eight calls the Organiser role: she set up our shared document, the schedule and the forum posts, and chased deadlines. The chapter says roles are often informally assumed, not assigned, and that's exactly what we saw.

In Tuckman's terms, losing a member pushed us from norming back into a short re-storming, then re-norming once roles settled. Out of the Five C's, our Coordinating was strong thanks to Owethu, but our Comforting and face-to-face Communicating were weaker, since we were a distributed team across time zones, working mostly asynchronously.

That setup is where my own research topic showed up for real: swift trust. We had no time to build trust the slow way, so we leaned on clear roles and shared deadlines instead. Seeing my report topic play out in my own team was the best part for me.

If I'm honest, our weak spot was team reflexivity, Schippers' idea that a team should stop and review its own process. We rarely did, so we hit coordination losses and confusion over who owned what. My takeaway: in a remote team, structured roles do the work that being in the same room normally would.
