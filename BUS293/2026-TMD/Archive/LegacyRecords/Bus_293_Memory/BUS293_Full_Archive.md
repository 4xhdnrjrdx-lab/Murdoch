# TOPIC 1 — Introduction to Organisational Behaviour (Ch 1)

## 1.1 Lecture Podcast — Topic 1 (transcript, verbatim)

Welcome to BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour. This unit provides an overview of the different dynamics of individual and group behaviour in organisations and explores organisational theories and management processes. The first topic is an introduction to organisational behaviour, which will define organisational behaviour and explain its importance, introduce four perspectives of organisational effectiveness, and discuss challenges and changes to the field of organisational behaviour.

Organisations have existed since the beginning of time and are defined as "people working together to achieve a common goal or purpose" and they are not just for-profit businesses. Throughout history, craftspeople and merchants have formed guilds to achieve common purposes. Large-scale irrigation projects in Egypt and Mesopotamia, and the building of ancient temples, required the organisation and coordination of massive numbers of people. And organisations continue to exist because of the synergy created through this effective division of labour – through humans being able to achieve more as a team than as individuals.

Organisational Behaviour is the study of what people, as individuals and groups, think, feel, and do in and around organisations. It is examined on three levels, the individual level, the work group level, and the organisational level. The purpose is to apply this knowledge to improve the organisations performance through the management of human behaviour.

The effectiveness, or performance of the organisation, was traditionally gauged on how well it achieved its stated goals or objectives. However, if a company achieved goals or sales targets that were set too low, or weren't relevant to the current industry, economic, or social environment, this still wouldn't result in success. Now, organisations are seen as effective if they have a good fit with their external environment, are a learning organisation, have efficient and adaptive internal subsystems, and satisfy their stakeholders.

The open systems perspective is the foremost method of thinking about organisations and their interactions with the external environment. It describes the organisation as a complex organism that uses inputs from the external environment, like raw materials, human resources, information, financial resources, and equipment, and transforms them through all the interdependent organisational subsystems, into outputs that are then put back into the external environment. These outputs can include products or services, shareholder dividends, community support and services, and waste and pollution. Some organisational outputs, such as dividends, services, and community support, are valued by the external environment, but others, like pollution and employee layoffs, are seen as less desirable, and throughout this process those evaluations are received as feedback by the organisation. Organisations also receive feedback about the availability of inputs such as skilled labour shortages, or changing population demographics, and through this combined feedback have the opportunity to adjust their subsystems, and thus the behaviours in the organisation, to 'fit' and continue to be effective in the changing external environment. The other perspectives on organisational effectiveness can be explained in relation to the open systems perspective.

An input that is occasionally looked over in the open systems perspective is knowledge. The organisational learning perspective suggests the effectiveness of an organisation depends on the organisations ability to acquire, share, use, and store valuable knowledge. Knowledge is seen as a resource, or asset, called intellectual capital, and it exists in three forms.

Human capital is the stock of knowledge, skills, and abilities among employees that provide economic value to the organisation. It is valuable and rare, as employees are essential for organisational survival and success, and talented people are difficult to find and replicate. Human capital cannot easily be replaced by technology and is thus a competitive advantage and a huge risk, as when people leave the organisation their knowledge goes with them.

Structural capital is what would remain if every employee left the organisation and is embedded in organisations systems and structures. It includes documentation of work procedures, physical layout of the production line, and finished products that can be reverse engineered.

Finally, relationship capital is the value of an organisations' relationships with its suppliers, customers, and other stakeholders. This includes the goodwill and brand image of the organisation.

An organisations intellectual capital develops and is maintained through the four organisational learning processes of Acquiring knowledge, Sharing knowledge, Using knowledge, and Storing knowledge.

The High-Performance Work Practices perspective of organisational effectiveness looks closely at the transformational subsystems of the open systems perspective and suggests four work practices that improve effectiveness. Although each of these practices will improve effectiveness, they have a stronger effect when bundled together. They are employee involvement in decision making, employee autonomy, employee development, and rewards valued by employees linked with performance and skill development. These practices improve organisational effectiveness by improving individual employee performance, increasing organisational adaptability to changing external environments, and improving employee motivation and job satisfaction.

The stakeholder perspective of organisational effectiveness expands on the external environment aspect of the open systems perspective. It identifies specific people and groups that affect, or are affected by, the organisations objectives and actions, and recognises that stakeholder relations are dynamic. This perspective incorporates values, ethics, and corporate social responsibility, and in general, organisations are more effective when they satisfy stakeholder needs and expectations.

The increasing focus on corporate social responsibility is one of the current challenges facing organisations. The concept of corporate social responsibility is that an organisation has a responsibility to the society in which it operates, and it should be sustainable, not just as a going concern in continuing to profit, but also in ensuring that it meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Managers are therefore challenged to ensure that all their inputs, social, environmental, and financial are used in a way that all stakeholders receive value and that the resources required by future generations are not exploited.

Technological change is another challenge facing organisations. It has always been a disruptive force in organisations and society, boosting productivity but displacing employees and sometimes making entire occupational groups obsolete. Other technologies have altered our relationships and patterns of behaviour with co-workers and stakeholders, like the introduction of the internet, smartphones, email, social media, and data analytics. These new technologies have both positive and negative impacts on organisations and the people within them, facilitating ease in communication and networking, yet tethering people to their jobs for longer hours, reducing work-life balance, and increasing techno-stress.

Globalisation is the increasing interconnectedness to other countries and cultures through liberalisation of trade and immigration policies and the ability to communicate using new forms of technology. It benefits organisations through access to larger and new markets, lower costs of production, and greater access to knowledge and innovation. It can also have less positive impacts and has been attributed to work intensification, and the reduction of job security and work-life balance in developed countries. It increases the complexity of teamwork, diversity, values, organisational structure, leadership, and other subsystems that impact behaviour in the organisation.

The nature of work is also changing due to globalisation and technology, with knowledge work increasing. This makes knowledge management and brain drain – where employees leave jobs and take their knowledge with them – a new area managers need to pay attention to.

The composition of the workforce is shifting as well, with increasing cultural diversity through globalisation which impacts leadership, motivation, and decision making. The population is aging and this changing demographic could mean labour shortages, loss of organisational memory, and generational differences in terms of values, skills, loyalty, occupational mobility, and technical skills. Increasing gender equality, with more women in the workforce, and in senior positions is also altering organisational membership demographics, as well as breaking down the traditional idea that some jobs are gender specific.

Employment relationships, and the psychological contract, are another challenging aspect as flexible work arrangements become standard along with remote work. There is also the growing casualisation of roles, which promotes flexibility for the organisation but decreasing job security for employees, and encourages a gig economy. The emerging gig economy, where people independently contract or freelance for multiple organisations, also offers increased flexibility for workers, but is criticised as workers do not receive sick leave, annual leave, or parental leave, and have very little job security.

These challenges change the way managers and organisations need to be, think, and act in order to succeed and perform effectively. We study organisational behaviour to understand the dynamics of why things happen in organisations, and to know how to predict, and manage these events through adjusting the behaviours in and of the organisation. As organisational behaviour practices are powerful predictors of an organisations survival and success they are therefore important for both managers and employees to know and understand. Thank you.

# TOPIC 2 — Team Dynamics (Ch 8)

## 2.1 Lecture Podcast — Topic 2 (transcript, verbatim)

Welcome back to BUS293, Organisational Theory and Behaviour. As mentioned in the previous topic, working together is a fundamental aspect of the human experience and workplace teams are increasingly perceived as necessary for success. This topic introduces the team effectiveness model, team processes, team decision making, and emerging team types.

Firstly, although similar, there are some differences between teams and groups. Teams are a type of group, where the members have complementary skills and work together as a unit to achieve a common purpose associated with organisational objectives for which the collectively hold themselves accountable. Teams perceive themselves as a social entity within an organisation. Informal groups are NOT formally designated as a part of the organisation and they emerge unofficially. These groups include friendship and interest groups, and they help people accomplish their work, satisfy their individual needs, reduce stress, and therefore improve employee wellbeing. The main determining factor that separate teams from just being a group, is the shared commitment of members towards achieving organisational objectives.

Teams have both advantages and disadvantages. Under the right conditions, teams make better decisions, better products and services, and a more motivated and engaged workforce than individuals working alone. Teams can quickly share information and coordinate tasks, which speeds up processes and increases breadth of knowledge, consequently increasing quality of customer service. People are also more motivated when working in teams, as there is a social identity to uphold, increased accountability to team members who monitor performance more closely than a supervisor, and the tendency to compare performance to other team members.

Despite these advantages, there are also disadvantages in the forms of process losses encountered by teams. Coordination process losses include the time and energy taken to develop and maintain the team. Teams need to develop understanding of their goals, determine strategies, negotiate roles and rules of conduct, and resolve disagreements, to ensure the team functions effectively. Process losses increase as team size increases and are amplified when a new member joins the team, as time and energy must be used to accommodate and integrate the new member. Motivational process loss is seen mostly in social loafing. Social loafing, or freeloading, is the tendency of people to not work as hard in a team as they would individually. Social loafing is reduced when the task is interesting, when the team's objective is important, and when team members value team membership.

There are a few main types of teams in the workplace. Self-directed teams are small teams who have been empowered to manage themselves and the work they do on a day-to-day basis. They are very project-focussed, completing an entire piece of work requiring several interdependent tasks and usually develop their own goals. They have substantial autonomy, operate with fewer layers than traditional teams, and the position of supervisor is no longer needed. Self-directed teams are increasing in popularity and research indicates they have increased productivity and job satisfaction.

Multicultural teams are also increasing as organisations become increasingly global and their members become more diverse. The heterogeneity of multicultural teams increases their creativity and performance; however it is important to develop norms that allow the team to reach consensus. Cultural differences in norms about power, status, and authority, communicating, and decision making may cause tension and frustration between team members, however these can be managed through adaptation, variation of team structure, or managerial intervention in setting ground rules for interaction.

Virtual teams are a response to the increasing degree of separation between team members created by new forms of organisational structure, advances in technologies, and globalisation. They could be separated by different floors in the same building, different base organisations, or even different countries. Members of virtual teams require strong communication technology skills and strong self-leadership skills, as well as access to multiple communication channels, increased task structure, and face-to-face meetings early in the team development process.

A team is effective when it benefits the organisation through task performance, benefits its members through satisfying members needs, and survives long enough to accomplish its purpose. Ensuring the right conditions for team effectiveness is therefore important, and the team effectiveness model represents the conditions that can influence team effectiveness.

The organisational and team environments either support or inhibit the team's ability to function and achieve its objectives. Team members work together more effectively when organisationally there is team-based rewards, supportive leadership, structural support, suitable information systems, and physical space for teamwork.

The design of the team also contributes to the team's effectiveness. Task characteristics are the first element of team design and consist of task complexity, task structure, and task interdependence. Complex tasks require the skills and knowledge of more than one person, and thus teams are more suited as the work can be divided into more specialised roles. It then follows that well structured work is easier to coordinate in teams, in contrast to ambiguous tasks. However, teams can perform ambiguous tasks with well-structured roles. Finally, task interdependence, the way in which members must share information, materials, or expertise, in order to do their jobs, impacts team effectiveness, and the higher the level of interdependence, the greater need for coordination and use of teams. If the members do not directly interact with each other and are only indirectly affected by the others' actions, this is pooled, or low, interdependence. In this case, very little attention is needed to the management of team dynamics.

If teams are designed in such a way that the outputs of one member become inputs for another member, this is sequential, or medium, interdependence. The second member depends on the first and cannot perform effectively if the first member is not performing effectively. In this situation there is more potential for problems in team dynamics, which creates a need for greater coordination and attention to team functioning.

The third interdependence, reciprocal, or high interdependence - occurs when each member has outputs that are inputs to the other member. This increases the complexity of team dynamics as there will be frequent and varied interactions between the members on a daily basis. Any problems will spill over to other points of contact, and so teams need to not only pay attention, but have skills in creating and maintaining effective team dynamics and relationships.

The second element of team design is team size. The most effective number of team members is debated, however it must be large enough to provide the necessary competencies and resources to perform the work, yet small enough to allow efficient coordination and meaningful involvement of each member. Smaller teams have less process losses, are more engaged because they have a greater influence on the teams norms and goals, experience more mutual trust and perceptions of support, and feel more responsible for the team's success and failure.

The final element of team design is team composition. The members capability to work well in a team, or their team member competency, affect both task performance and team maintenance. Team member competency is summarised in the five C's: Cooperating, Coordinating, Communicating, Comforting, and Conflict resolving. Team diversity is the second aspect of team composition, with diverse teams making better decisions than homogenous teams. Diverse teams also have a broader range of abilities and skills. However, increased team diversity poses challenges as diverse teams tend to take longer to bond and thus progress towards becoming a high-performance team. They also have increased challenges in communication and motivation to coordinate. Team roles are the third aspect of team composition, and are the set of behaviours a member is expected to perform because they hold a certain position in a team and/or organisation. Roles can either be formally assigned, or informally assumed. Finally, transactive memory is knowing who knows what, or more simply put, allocating the right tasks and roles to the most suited members.

Once the team is effectively designed, or formed, it must then then follow effective team states and processes.

Norms are the informal rules and shared expectations that teams establish to regulate the behaviours of their members. They can become deeply anchored, so the best way to avoid norms that are detrimental to the team is to establish positive norms when the team is first formed, for example by establishing a code of conduct which sets out basic principles and rules governing team norms and behaviours.

Team cohesion is the degree to which members are attracted to and motived to remain a part of the team. It strongly influences the extent to which members of a team conform to team norms, and thus when team performance norms are positive, high cohesiveness has positive effects for the organisation, but when team performance norms are negative, high cohesiveness has detrimental effects on the organisation. The factors that improve team cohesion include higher member similarity, smaller team size, increased member interaction, difficulty of entry, team success, and external competition and challenges.

Team efficacy is the collective belief among team members of the team's ability to successfully complete the task, and as a general rule, the higher the team efficacy, the higher the performance.

The final team state is team trust. Trust is both cognitive and emotional, based on perceptions of competence and social identity. It is usually high in team formation, and then events of poor individual performance, or other violations of trust, decrease the level. A lower level of trust results in team members being less forgiving and less cooperative, which undermines team and organisational effectiveness.

Team processes are both internal and external and are the interactions and activities, generally classified as team work or task work behaviours, or team boundary spanning, that the team engage in as they work towards their goal.

In the process of becoming an effective work unit, teams go through stages of team development. Tuckman's model of team development shows systematic stages that teams go through to become a cohesive, effective team. Initially there is the forming stage, where members discover team expectations and test he boundaries of behaviour. They then progress to the storming stage where there is interpersonal conflict and negotiation of roles, goals, and norms. As these roles, goals, and norms are established, the team moves to the norming stage, where a sense of cohesion is established and there is a consensus around objectives and behaviours. The team is then able to perform, as members have learned to efficiently coordinate and resolve conflicts. For high-performance teams, there is high level of cooperation, trust, commitment to group objectives, and identification with the team. Once the team objective is complete, teams' transition to the adjourning stage, and members shift their focus to relationships, away from the task. Although Tuckman's model depicts team development very well, it is not a perfect representation of the process, and different teams will develop differently. There may be inertia, revisiting of stages, or skipping of stages, as the team progresses through their own individual experience.

Team development can also be accelerated through team building. Team building uses formal activities to improve the development and functioning of a team. Experiential activities include games that help to build cooperation, competition, and trust. Challenging viewpoints approaches helps team members to break down unhelpful viewpoints that are impeding team performance through using techniques like the 'devil's advocate' process, or redefining roles within the team. Reaching consensus approaches like brainstorming reduce members fear of criticism or failure and result in enthusiasm, involvement, and a free flow of ideas. These team building techniques can be conducted in a range of settings, from formal retreats, to regular meetings in a vein of continual improvement, or outdoor experiences, however ongoing reflection of the teams work experiences and a willingness to experiment is generally more effective.

By ensuring that the organisational and team environment, team design, team states, and team processes are all optimised for team performance, teams are much more likely to be effective.

Teams are expected to make decisions, and under certain conditions do better at this than individuals alone. However, there are some constraints that reduce the effectiveness of team decision making. Time constraints such as production blocking, where only one team member can speak at a time, undermines idea generation. Evaluation apprehension, where people are reluctant to mention ideas in case they will be judged or seen as silly, also reduces idea generation. Pressure to conform causes team members to not speak out if they disagree with an idea, which could lead to the team making a suboptimal decision. Overconfidence can also reduce the quality of decision made as overconfident teams are less vigilant when making decisions and are less likely to seek out outside information. Finally, groupthink is when highly cohesive groups focus on consensus rather than decision quality. It limits both idea generation and idea quality, and is usually present when pressure to conform and overconfidence are also factors.

To improve effective decision making, teams can use the following techniques to reduce the constraints. Brainstorming helps to increase idea generation and minimise evaluation apprehension, however it is still impacted by production blocking and pressure to conform. Brainwriting, which is a version of brainstorming where team members write ideas on cards and place them in the centre of the table, helps remove production blocking, and arguably produces more and better quality ideas than brainstorming. Electronic brainstorming, where brainwriting is done anonymously using technology, again improves on the benefits of brainwriting, and minimises production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and pressure to confirm, however it tends to be too structured and technology bound. Finally, nominal group technique is another variation of brainwriting conducted in three stages. Firstly, members individually and silently document their ideas, then they collectively describe these to the group without criticism, and then again silently and independently the members evaluate the ideas presented. This technique again improves on the benefits of brainstorming, however production blocking and evaluation apprehension do still occur to some extent. Despite the limitations, using techniques for team decision making can improve the effectiveness of the decisions made.

Team work is essential, as research has shown that teams consistently out-perform individuals or random groups, but as this topic has discussed, they are not easy to create or manage. In order to ensure teams have positive norms, desired team behaviours need to be actively shaped and reinforced. This is relevant not just for its importance to organisational behaviour, but also to your performance in your assignment teams. Try using this new knowledge to form and develop an effective team, and if there is disfunction, or a lack of performance, consider modifying one or more of the inputs or processes, to try and improve the outputs of the team. Thank you, and all the best.

# TOPIC 3 — Individual Differences: Personality, Values & Perception (Ch 2, 3)

## 3.1 Lecture Podcast — Topic 3 (transcript, verbatim)

Welcome back to BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour. Previously, we have looked at the importance of effectively managing groups in the workplace, however managers also need to understand individual differences to influence individual performance in the workplace. Perception, self-concept, and the perceptual process all shape the way an individual interprets the environment around them, and thus informs and compounds these individual differences. This topic examines individual differences in behaviour, personality, values, and perception.

The MARS model identifies the four variables of individual behaviour and performance, Motivation, Ability, Role Perceptions, and Situational Factors. Motivation is the force within a person that affects their willingness to perform a job. Ability is the aptitudes – the potential abilities – and the learned capabilities – which are the skills and knowledge people already possess. Cognitive, physical, and emotional aptitudes all contribute towards work performance, and are usually tested for during the recruitment process. The related job skills can then be enhanced through training and professional development. Role perceptions are how much a person understands their job duties, and they help clarify where the person should be directing their effort. Situational factors are conditions outside of the employees control that help or hinder behaviour and performance. If one or more of motivation, ability, role perception, or situational factors are low, then behaviour and individual performance will be impacted.

There are five main types of workplace behaviour. Firstly, Task performance refers to goal-directed behaviours under the individual's control that support organisational objectives. Proficient task performance is how well the work is performed, Adaptive task performance is how well employees respond to change in the workplace and their duties, and Proactive task performance is how well employees take the initiative to anticipate and change to benefit the organisation. Organisational citizenship behaviours consist of various forms of cooperation and helpfulness to others that support the organisation's social and psychological context, for example assisting co-workers and sharing work resources. They might be required behaviours, or discretionary behaviours. Counterproductive work behaviours are voluntary behaviours that have the potential to harm the organisation directly or indirectly. These include creating unnecessary conflict, avoiding work obligations, and taking shortcuts that risk work quality. Joining and staying with the organisation refers to agreeing to become an organisational member and remaining with the organisation. These behaviours are influenced by both individual and organisational factors, and are important to the organisation as increased employee turnover is costly as there is not only a high cost of replacing people who leave, but also the cost of lost knowledge in the form of intellectual capital leaving the organisation as well. Maintaining work attendance includes minimising absenteeism if capable of working and avoiding scheduled work if not fit for work. Low work attendance, or high absenteeism, is attributed to situational factors such as illness or family demands, however aspects such as work-related stress, or job dissatisfaction are more likely to result in absenteeism.

People exhibit a range of behaviours, however within these there are discernible patterns referred to as personality traits. Personality is the relatively enduring pattern of thoughts, emotions and behaviours that characterise a person, along with the psychological processes behind those characteristics. Personality traits are broad concepts about people that allow us to label and understand individual differences. They are impacted by both heredity and environment, and are generally categorised into five key dimensions of personality, known as "The Big Five". Conscientiousness and emotional stability (low neuroticism) predict individual performance in most job groups. Extraversion is associated with performance in sales and management jobs, whereas agreeableness is associated with performance in jobs requiring cooperation, and openness to experience is associated with performance in creative jobs.

The Myers Briggs Type Indicator is another method of categorising personality, and identifies competing orientations for getting energy, perceiving information, processing information and making decisions, and orienting to the external world. It is popular in organisations and widely used, however is a poor predictor of job performance and is not recommended for use in selection or promotion activities. These models of personality are not comprehensive, and thus miss out on some positive traits, such as honesty, interpersonal characteristics, and resilience, as well as negative traits such as narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy. Personality profiling helps organisations to filter potential employees with traits that are linked to required job-specific behaviour. however, there is still concern that it can unfairly discriminate, and there is the potential for 'faking', where people fill in the questionnaires with what they believe the organisation wants to hear, rather than an accurate representation of themselves.

Values reflect a persons' sense of right and wrong, and what they should do, whereas personality traits reflect what a person naturally tends to do. They can influence workplace attitudes, behaviours, and outputs and when personal values are similar to team shared values, and/or organisation values, there is value congruence. Value congruence is linked to increased job satisfaction and decreased turnover. however, too much congruity can lead to a corporate cult which undermines creativity, flexibility, and business ethics. Some value incongruence benefits an organisation as the diversity of opinion leads to better decision making, and creativity. Ethical values are viewed as highly important for corporate leaders to have, and a persons values, their sense of right and wrong, is likely to determine the level of their ethical behaviour. There are three ethical principles that underline ethical behaviour. End-result ethics, or utilitarianism, is the ends justify the means, and suggests the end result is what should be considered when making that decision. Duty based ethics, or individual rights, is the responsibility we have to others and ourselves to do good. And social contract ethics, or distributive justice, is the responsibility we have to the common good. All three principles should be considered when making ethical decisions. In addition to ethical principles and values, moral intensity- the degree to which an issue demands the application of ethical principles, moral thresholds – a person's ability to recognise and deal with ethical dilemmas, moral foundations – intuition-based principles that govern judgements and actions, and situational factors such as management pressure influence ethical conduct in the workforce. To support ethical behaviour in the workplace, organisations can implement codes of ethical conduct to motivate and guide employee behaviour, anonymous hotlines for employees to report unethical behaviour, and ethics audits to identify organisational gaps. However, the most powerful tool to ensure ethical conduct is shared values reinforcing the ethical conduct which is supported and modelled by corporate leaders.

Cross cultural values are those that differ across different cultures around the world. Hofstede summarises these into five categories. Individualism, which is valuing independence and personal uniqueness; collectivism which is valuing duty to groups we belong to and group harmony; power distance, which is the extent to which people accept unequal distribution of power; uncertainty avoidance, which is tolerating or feeling threatened by ambiguity and uncertainty; and achievement orientation, which is valuing competition over cooperation. An awareness of these differences can help in understanding different behaviours such as either a willingness, or hesitance to, speak up to management and voice a differing opinion. However, many studies into cross cultural values and knowledge have used small samples from subsets of the population, and generalise, which may not represent an entire culture. They also tend to assume that countries have one culture, which is increasingly not the case. And applying cross-cultural knowledge without keeping this in mind can lead to stereotyping, which is one of the errors that can influence the perceptual process.

Perception is the process of receiving information about, and making sense of, the world around us. The process usually follows the steps of Receiving environmental stimuli through our senses, subconsciously selecting which stimuli to focus on, organising, and interpreting that information using categorical thinking and mental models, to determine attitudes and behaviours. To efficiently manage the amount of information we have to process, our brain has developed methods to select and sort information. These methods, although increasing efficiency, sometimes result in errors.

Confirmation bias occurs as a result of selective attention, where we tend to focus on, or select, the information that confirms our beliefs, and ignore information that contradicts this. In the organisation and interpretation stage, the brain categorises people, objects, and situations with other similar items such as by gender, age, race, clothing style, proximity, or interests. It also attempts to fill in the blanks, so if there is information you don't know, it will assume it based on prior images or experience. This is how stereotyping can occur subconsciously, as the brain groups people into a category based on a small amount of information, and then assumes other characteristics based on them belonging to that category. Stereotypes are formed to some extent through personal experience, but are mostly developed through exposure to media. Although everyone engages in stereotyping as a non-conscious energy saving process that simplifies our understanding of the world, it leads to perceptual biases and flawed decision making, as every individual is unique, and not defined by a social group to which they belong.

The attribution process is another area of perception that can lead to perceptual errors and affect decision making. This is where a decision is made as to whether the behaviour, action, or event was caused by external or internal factors. Self-serving bias is the tendency to take credit for success, and blame others for failure. Self-fulfilling prophecy explains the situation where our perception influences our behaviour, which then reinforces the initial perception (e.g. manager belief → support/challenging goals → employee does well; or negative belief → reprimands/monitoring → employee resentful and underperforms). It is important that managers maintain a positive, but realistic, perception of all employees. Halo effect is similar to stereotyping, however rather than assuming characteristics based on a group, you assume characteristics based on one prominent characteristic or event. False-consensus effect is where we assume the way we think/behave is the way most people think/behave. Primacy effect is judging a book by its cover / the lasting nature of first impressions. Recency effect is when the most recent information takes precedence (e.g. annual review focusing on most recent performance).

As we are all human, we cannot bypass the perceptual process, however we should try to minimise perceptual errors and biases. We can improve perception through: awareness of perceptual biases; improving self-awareness (more aware of own beliefs/values/attitudes → more open-minded, less judgemental); and meaningful interaction (spending time working closely with others → increased empathy/understanding, rely less on mental shortcuts). These methods are also needed for developing a global mindset — the ability to perceive, appreciate, and empathise with people from other cultures and to process complex cross-cultural information. A global mindset enables better cross-cultural relationships, digesting cross-cultural information, faster networking/resource exchange across borders, and quicker response to global opportunities.

Self-concept is internal perceptions, or how we perceive ourselves. It is the self-beliefs and self-evaluations that guide decisions and actions, including whether a role or company is a good fit with who you are and want to be. Self-concept has three facets, complexity, consistency, and clarity. Complexity is the number of distinct identities or roles people fill. High complexity protects self-esteem when one identity is damaged by failure, and contributes to adaptive decision making and performance, however juggling these can be stressful. Low complexity people who identify mostly with their work identity often invest more in skill development and have lower absenteeism and turnover. Consistency is how similar these roles/identities are; occupational self-concept (ability to focus on work-self) is important when working from home. Clarity is the confidence in and awareness of important identities. High clarity is essential for leadership, enables efficient direction of effort toward career objectives, reduces threat of interpersonal conflict → more constructive problem solving; however can contribute to role inflexibility. Low self-concept clarity leads to increased stress when making decisions and increased threat to self-confidence/esteem. Wellbeing generally increases with higher levels of the aspects of self-concept.

There are four processes that shape self-concept. Self-enhancement is the motivation to have a positive self-concept and be perceived favourably; tends to lead to overconfidence (rating self above average), a can-do attitude, but also increased risk-taking and slower recognition of mistakes. Self-verification is the motivation to confirm and maintain self-concept (accepting confirming compliments, rejecting non-confirming); affects giving/accepting feedback. Self-evaluation is defined by three elements: self-esteem (overall evaluation — how much a person likes/respects/is satisfied with themselves), self-efficacy (belief they can accomplish a task), and locus of control (belief in ability to control personal life events). High self-esteem, high self-efficacy, and internal locus of control are generally associated with increased wellbeing. Finally, the social self identifies the motivation for individuality (personal identity) and the motivation for belonging (social identity). People who prioritise social identities are more likely to abide by team norms and be influenced by peer pressure.

Managers and team members need to understand individual differences to influence individual performance. These differences impact the motivation, ability, and role perceptions aspects of the MARS model. Try reducing perceptual biases and increasing meaningful interactions with your team members to improve communication and produce a better report. Thank you.

# TOPIC 4 — Foundations of Employee Motivation (Ch 5)

## 4.1 Lecture Podcast — Topic 4 (transcript, verbatim)

Welcome back to BUS293, Organisational Theory and Behaviour. This topic explores the conceptual foundations of employee motivation and the practical applications of these theories.

Motivation is one of the four essential drivers of individual behaviour and performance and plays a large part in employee engagement. Employee engagement is defined as an individual's emotional and rational motivation, particularly a focused, intense, persistent and purposive effort towards work-related goals. It is emotional involvement in, commitment to and satisfaction with the work, as well as a high level of absorption in the work and a sense of self-efficacy about performing the work.

There are two main theoretical approaches to motivation – content theories and process theories. Content theories look at What motivates people, also referred as their needs or drives, whereas process theories look at How people are motivated. The main content theories are: Four-drive theory, Maslow's hierarchy of needs, and McClellands learned needs theory. The content theories posit that people have certain drives and needs, and if they are met, the person will be motivated. Alternately, if they are not being met, they will be demotivated. It is important to note that the content theories are impacted by individual differences, and cultural differences emphasise different needs. For example, in countries with high uncertainty avoidance such as Japan and Greece, security tends to motivate employees more than self-actualisation. Regardless, managers being aware of the categories that employees may need for motivation is a useful tool in order to gauge what may be impacting on the work effort of employees.

The main Process Theories are Equity Theory and Expectancy Theory. Equity theory focuses on the motivating factor of unfairness, and posits that if a person feels that their situation is unfair, or inequitable, that they will want to take action to rectify the situation. People compare their inputs and rewards to others inputs and rewards, and if they feel they are receiving proportionately less than others, this is known as negative inequity, or underreward. If they feel they are receiving proportionality more than others, this is known as positive inequity, or over-reward. When there is this inequity, people will engage in behaviours to restore equity, for example: changing work inputs by reducing level of effort, changing rewards by asking for a raise, leaving the situation by quitting their job, changing comparison points by comparing with a different co-worker, distort the comparisons by rationalising the inequity, or acting to change the inputs or rewards of the comparison person by asking them to take on more work. The perceptual process again comes into play when making equity comparisons, as what one employee considers a fair pay rise may not be perceived the same way by another. This concept of fairness is referred to as organisational justice, which consists of distributive justice (perceived fairness in the outcomes we receive relative to our contributions and the outcomes and contributions of others) and procedural justice (the fairness of the procedures used to decide the distribution of resources). Companies need to consider not only equity of the distribution of resources but also fairness in the process of making resource allocation decisions to ensure the motivation and engagement of employees.

Expectancy theory looks to explain WHY a person would put in effort to contribute towards organisational performance. It looks at the expectancy, instrumentality, and valence components of the process. Expectancy is the probability that the person putting in effort will be able to perform at the task. If a person can put in effort and subsequently perform, expectancy, and therefore motivation, would be high. However, if a person puts in effort but, due to factors outside of their control – such as a lack of appropriate training or ability, is unable to perform, the low expectancy would cause motivation to be low. Instrumentality is the probability that performance will lead to an outcome. If a person knows that their performance will result in an outcome, instrumentality, and therefore motivation, will be high. However, if a person performs well and no outcome, or reward, is available, the low instrumentality would cause motivation to be low. Finally, Valence is the value a person places on the various outcomes. If the rewards or outcomes are valued then valence is high, and so motivation will be as well. If the rewards are not what the person wants or values, then valence will be low, and subsequently motivation. Even if two of these three factors are high, if any one of them is low, then motivation will be as well.

The implications for managers are that to maintain high levels of expectancy, they need to select employees with the ability to perform, train them to direct this ability appropriately, support them with organisational resources, and clearly identify their job requirements. To maintain high levels of instrumentality they need to have clear psychological contracts, communicate the rewards for good performance, and have reward systems that clearly reward employees for the stated good performance. To maintain high levels of valence, an awareness of what employees need, want, and value must be established, and rewards tailored to match these.

Expectancy theory is based in the probabilities of an outcome affecting motivation and behaviour, but doesn't explain how employees learn these probabilities. Organisational behaviour modification takes the behaviourist view that the environment teaches people to alter their behaviour so that they maximise positive consequences and minimise adverse consequences and consists of four basic reinforcement strategies. Positive reinforcement is when desired behaviours are rewarded by wanted, or positive, outcomes. It increases the likelihood of the desired behaviour being repeated, and is most effective when the reward is received as soon as possible after the desired behaviour is exhibited. Negative reinforcement is when desired behaviours are rewarded by the removal of negative, or unwanted, consequences. Both positive and negative reinforcement are intended to encouraged desired behaviours. Punishment is when undesired behaviours are responded to with negative consequences. It is used not to encourage positive behaviour, but to discourage negative behaviour. There are major reservations with using punishment, as it results in negative emotions towards the punisher and the organisation, so it should only be used in extreme cases (for example, theft). Extinction is when undesired behaviours are No Longer rewarded by positive or wanted outcomes. When the behaviour is then no longer reinforced, it will reduce in frequency. This strategy, like punishment, is used to eliminate undesired behaviours.

Expectancy beliefs are not only learned through personal interaction with the environment, but also through observing others and anticipating potential consequences of their actions. This is the social cognitive theory. People learn from stimulus, behaviours, and consequences experienced by others, not just themselves. This alters a person's instrumentality as they expect a similar outcome to the one they observed, if they have similar performance. A person uses modelling, or vicarious learning, to acquire behaviours by observing and imitating others. This alters a person's expectancy as they can more efficiently translate effort into performance. Mentoring is used in the workplace to capitalise on this idea of social learning. The theory emphasises self-regulation of individual behaviour, including self-reinforcement, which is the tendency of people to reward and punish themselves as a consequence of their actions, such as building natural rewards into your task and establishing constructive thought patterns.

Self regulation is also the cornerstone of motivation through goal-setting and feedback. Goal setting is the process of motivating employees and clarifying their role perceptions by establishing performance objectives. This helps improve the expectancy stage of expectancy theory by amplifying intensity and persistence of effort and by clarifying employee role perceptions. Goal setting is more complex than just picking a target or telling someone to 'do their best'; challenging and specific goals are more likely to motivate and lead to higher performance than easier or vague goals. The characteristics required for effective goals are summarised in the acronym SMARTER. Goals should be specific (clarifies expectations, improves effort-performance linkage). Measurable (clarifies expectations, allows self-monitoring). Achievable (challenging goals motivating, but easy goals → below potential, too difficult → lowers effort-performance expectancy). Relevant (to organisation and individual's role, within their control). Time-framed/due date (clear when measured/reviewed). Exciting (encourage commitment; challenging goals fulfil need for achievement; employee involvement increases commitment). Reviewed (feedback; ongoing reflection/discussion redirects effort; recognition fulfilling achievement needs).

Effective feedback should be specific, relevant, and timely (as soon as possible after behaviour/results), credible (complete, accurate, unbiased, supportive/empathetic), and sufficiently frequent (more often for new tasks, based on work cycle). Strengths-based coaching (appreciative coaching) maximises potential by focusing on strengths rather than weaknesses, minimising the impact of self-verification. Employees usually prefer non-social feedback sources (computer printouts, statistics) as more accurate and less damaging to self-esteem, but should receive some positive feedback from social, interpersonal sources.

Limitations to goal-setting: important aspects difficult to measure may be ignored (what gets measured gets done); very difficult goals may motivate unethical behaviour or create stress; goal setting interferes with learning (avoid with new employees / intense learning); goal achievement tied to financial rewards motivates employees to set easier goals and convince management they are difficult.

For organisations and teams to be successful, members must be motivated to perform the required behaviours. The use of monetary, or extrinsic rewards should be used with caution, as behaviours can be modified more effectively through improving the expectancy relationship, organisational justice, and goal setting. Managers need to enable competency building and empowerment, and minimise impediments to performance to maintain employee motivation and improve employee engagement. Thank you.

# TOPIC 5 — Emotions, Attitudes & Stress (Ch 4) + Applied Performance Practices (Ch 6)

## 5.1 Lecture Podcast — Topic 5 (transcript, verbatim)

Welcome back to BUS293 Organisational Theory and Behaviour. Last time, we looked at the conceptual foundations of employee motivation and the practical applications. Job satisfaction can also be impacted by emotions, attitudes, and stress. In addition, intrinsic job satisfaction can be improved through effective job design and flexible work arrangements. This topic introduces various types of job design, and organisational strategies to manage stress and improve individual satisfaction.

Job satisfaction represents a person's evaluation of their job and work context, and dissatisfaction with their job affects behaviour and results. There are four types of job dissatisfaction consequences, which are similar to those experienced when there is inequity in the workplace. Exit – which is quitting or otherwise getting away from the dissatisfying situation. Voice – which is attempting to change the dissatisfying situation. Loyalty – which is patiently waiting for the problem to sort itself out, and Neglect – which is reducing work effort and performance.

Loyalty is the employee's emotional attachment to, identification with and involvement in a particular organisation. This contrasts with continuance commitment, which is a logical, calculated bond with the organisation. Organisations can increase commitment through justice and support, shared values, trust, organisational comprehension and employee involvement.

Emotions such as those that contribute to organisational loyalty, are physiological, behavioural and psychological episodes experienced towards an object, person or event that create a state of readiness. They are brief experiences, and influence behaviour without cognitive awareness by predisposing us to either approach or avoid objects, people, or events based on the emotion experienced. Emotions differ from attitudes, as while emotions are subconscious, fleeting experiences, attitudes are judgements that are generally conscious, and stable over time. Attitudes have traditionally been described as a purely rational process, but we now know that emotions have an influence on behaviour that is equal to or greater than that of cognition. This dual process is apparent when we experience an internal conflict between what logically seems good or bad and what we emotionally feel is good or bad in a situation.

Emotional intelligence is the ability to perceive and express emotion, assimilate emotion in thought, understand and reason with emotion, and regulate emotion in oneself and others. This concept includes four components arranged in a hierarchy: Self-awareness, which is perceiving and understanding your own emotions. Self-management, which is keeping disruptive emotions in check and displaying positive emotions when required. This also includes generating and suppressing emotions. Social awareness, which is perceiving and understanding the emotions of other people, this includes having empathy, but also being organisationally aware and sensing office politics and understanding social networks. Finally, relationship management which is managing others' emotions through consoling someone who is sad, inspiring team members to complete a project, getting strangers to comfortably work with you, and managing dysfunctional conflict between team members. Emotional intelligence can be learned to some extent, particularly through personal coaching.

Jobs requiring a variety of emotions and more intense emotions, as well as in jobs where interaction with clients is frequent and has a long duration, generally involves a large amount of emotional labour. Emotional labour consists of the effort, planning and control needed to express organisationally desired emotions during interpersonal transactions. Cultures also differ on the norms of displaying or concealing a person's true emotions. Emotional dissonance occurs when required and true emotions are incompatible with each other. Deep acting, which is visualising reality differently to produce emotions more consistent with the required emotions, can minimise this dissonance, as can the practice of hiring people with a natural tendency to display desired emotions.

Stress another somewhat subconscious adaptive response to a situation that is perceived as challenging or threatening to a person's wellbeing. It is generally seen as negative, called distress, however some level of stress activates and motivates people to achieve their goals, and this is known as eustress. The causes of stress include any environmental conditions that place a physical or emotional demand on a person, and three stressors that have received considerable attention are harassment and incivility, work overload, and low task control. Interventions to manage work-related stress include removing the stressor, withdrawing from the stressor, changing stress perceptions, controlling stress consequences and receiving social support. One of the main organisational strategies to remove stressors is the introduction of policies to improve work-life balance. These include flexible work time, job sharing, telecommuting, personal leave, child care support, and job crafting.

The design of a job is a situational factor and can significantly increase or decrease the stressors of work overload and task control. Job design is the process of assigning tasks to a job, including the interdependency of those tasks with other jobs.

Job specialisation is standardising work procedures and employing people in clearly defined and specialised tasks, for example jobs on an assembly line in car manufacturing. Advantages include increased operating efficiency, low skill and low-cost labour, minimal training requirements, and controlled production quality. However, specialised jobs lack challenge and lead to boredom, and there are then costs in terms of higher rates of absenteeism and turnover, and the need to pay higher wages for people to do these "unattractive" jobs. Increasingly, the natural extension of job specialisation is automation, with machines replacing the employees needed to complete these highly repetitive jobs.

Job enlargement emerged in the 1950s as an attempt to reduce the boredom associated with simplified jobs. Employees were given an increased number of tasks, and this increased task variety was presumed to reduce the disadvantages of job simplification and therefore increase intrinsic motivation. However, job enlargement doesn't necessarily increase responsibility, or autonomy, it may simply just increase the number of mundane tasks an employee performs, so there are limits as to how much it can satisfy an employee.

Job rotation is similar to job enlargement in that it increases task variety, but does this through periodically shifting workers between jobs involving different tasks at a similar level. It has the advantage of enabling younger employees to learn the ropes in an organisation by rotating them through different areas. It also increases workforce flexibility, with the workforce more adaptable to accommodate when employees are sick, on leave, or have left the organisation. It has a similar disadvantage to job enlargement though, as periodically changing the mundane tasks performed does not increase their intrinsic motivation potential, only increases the horizontal loading, or breadth of jobs, by increasing the number of tasks performed. If job rotation includes vertical loading, where job depth is increased through adding responsibilities previously held by supervisors, then this can contribute to employee development and reduce some of the previous disadvantages of job rotation. Vertical loading is a key part of job enrichment.

Job enrichment is the practice of building motivating factors into job content. It focusses on increasing vertical loading, rather than the horizontal loading which is seen in job rotation and job enlargement. The job characteristics model sets out five core job characteristics required for a job to be enriched. These are skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback. The core job characteristics then need to transform into employees feeling that the work is meaningful, they are responsible for the outcomes, and knowledge of actual results of the work. If this occurs, it should result in high intrinsic work motivation and satisfaction, high quality work performance, and low absenteeism and turnover. However, the job characteristics model also takes into account individual differences of growth-need strength, knowledge and skill, and context satisfaction, and therefore the idea that different people will respond differently to job enrichment. Two ways to enrich jobs are clustering tasks into natural groups and establishing client relationships.

Job design is a major influence in empowerment, particularly autonomy, task identity, task significance and job feedback. Empowerment is also supported at the organisational level through a learning orientation culture, sufficient information and resources, and corporate leaders who trust employees.

Money and other financial rewards make up a fundamental aspect of the employment relationship and are another situational factor related to job satisfaction and performance. Financial rewards also affect our needs, our emotions and our self-concept. Organisations reward employees for a variety of things. Membership-based rewards are those offered for simply being an employee of the organisation such as fixed pay, benefits, and paid leave. They may attract job applicants, and seniority-based rewards reduce turnover, however these reward objectives also tend to discourage turnover among those with the lowest performance. Job status based rewards include pay increases on promotion and position-level benefits. They try to maintain internal equity and motivate employees to compete for promotions. However, they tend to encourage a bureaucratic hierarchy, support status differences and motivate employees to compete and hoard resources. Competency-based rewards such as skill-based pay are becoming increasingly popular because they encourage skill development. However, they tend to be subjectively measured and can result in higher costs as employees spend more time learning new skills. Task performance based rewards include commission based pay and profit sharing. These motivate task performance, attract performance-oriented staff, and create an ownership culture. However, they discourage creativity and tend to address symptoms, not underlying causes of behaviour. Employees often perceive a weak connection between individual performance and the organisational rewards such as profit sharing.

Reward effectiveness can be improved in several ways, which can be seen in relation to expectancy theory principles. Organisational leaders should ensure that rewards are linked to work performance, rewards are aligned with performance within the employee's control, team rewards are used where jobs are interdependent, rewards are valued by employees, rewards have no unintended consequences and rewards are adapted to suit the cultural context in which they are used.

Financial rewards and the design of the job have traditionally been considered as the main elements of job satisfaction, however it is now known that emotions, attitudes, and stress can have as much, if not more impact on how someone evaluates their job. In order to maximise job satisfaction, organisations should focus on the factors they control, such as stressors, and individuals should aim to be aware of their emotions and improve their emotional intelligence and perceptions of the job itself.
